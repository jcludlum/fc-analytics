1 (7 to 8 pound) Certified Angus Beef ® brand  
   bone-in rib roast; bones Frenched
2 cups roughly chopped onions
1 cup roughly chopped carrots
1 cup roughly chopped celery
2 cups beef demi-glace
1 cup red wine
1/4 cup extra-virgin olive oil
1/4 cup Bourbon Barrel Foods Woodford Reserve  
   Chef’s Spice Blend
2 tablespoons coarsely ground black pepper
2 tablespoons Kosher salt
2 tablespoons chopped fresh Italian parsley leaves
1 tablespoon chopped fresh rosemary leaves
2 teaspoons chopped fresh thyme leaves
6 cloves garlic, peeled and thinly sliced
• Preheat oven to 425° F. 
• Line a roasting pan with onions, carrots and celery; cover  
  with demi-glace and wine.  Place the roast in the pan,  
  bone side down. 
• Combine remaining ingredients; evenly coat the roast  
  with the rub. 
• Place roast in oven for 25 minutes to sear; lower oven  
  temperature to 300°.  Cook approximately 2 - 1/2  hours  
  for medium rare (125 - 130° F). 
• Remove roast from oven and roasting pan; cover and  
  let rest 20 minutes. 
• Remove vegetables from roasting pan and skim fat from  
  the liquid.  Place liquid in a sauce pan over medium heat;  
  season with salt and pepper if needed and simmer  
  5 minutes.
• To serve, slice the roast into desired portions and spoon  
  the pan sauce over top.
Serves 6 to 10

Back in the 
saddle again
Before I became chief editor of this magazine, I was its chief 
recipe tester. Every day, I thanked my lucky stars that I was actually 
being paid to do something I love—cooking—in an environment 
other than a stressful restaurant kitchen. Eventually, promotions 
took me out of the kitchen, but this past summer, when we found 
ourselves temporarily without a recipe tester, I traded my pen for a 
chef’s knife and headed back to the kitchen.
Working in the test kitchen for a few weeks was a great experience for me. I got to 
forget about boring stuﬀ  like budgets for a while and focus instead on the heart of this maga-
zine—the recipes—to a much deeper extent than I can from my desk. I also had a new kitchen 
intern to train, a lovely young woman named Neema who’s headed to culinary school soon. Her 
eagerness to learn and the way she soaked up every nugget of cooking advice I shared served 
as a great real-life reminder of why this magazine exists: to inspire and help you, our reader, to 
become a better cook. T at’s Fine Cooking’s mission in a nutshell.
So, after making just about every recipe in this issue, what were my favorites? Well, 
even though I’m not really a baker, I loved making the sweet rolls on pages 78-85. Handling 
the sof , yeasty dough and shaping it into beautiful spiral rolls were wonderfully satisfying 
and relaxing.
I also wouldn’t want you to miss the Sear-Roasted Duck Breasts with Grapefruit-
Balsamic Sauce on page 58 (I could eat that sauce with a spoon!), or the sof , chewy almond 
Italian Ricciarelli cookies on page 97, or the Dim Sum Meatballs on page 64 or ...
I could go on, but I think I’ll stop and instead ask you what your favorites are. Your 
feedback helps us make this magazine better for you, so af er you’ve had a chance to read 
this issue and try a few recipes, please let us know what you think by emailing us at fc@
taunton.com, tagging us in your social media posts (#ﬁ necooking), or joining our new 
advisory panel (see below). 
Happy holidays!                                                                        —Jennifer Armentrout, editor

Sun-Maid raisins add a little natural sweetness to any dish,
making ordinary recipes extraordinary.
Heat oven to 350°F. Place roast on rack in 
shallow roasting pan. Sprinkle with salt 
and pepper. Roast 2.5-3 hrs. or until 
internal temperature is 145°-150°F. 
Meanwhile, in 12” skillet, heat oil and butter 
on med. heat. Add celery and leeks. Cook 5 
mins. Stir in bell pepper, chestnuts, parsley, 
sage, salt and pepper. Cook 5 mins. In large 
bowl, combine stuffing mix, raisins, and 
vegetable mixture. Stir in broth. Cover 
bowl; refrigerate.
Remove roast from oven after about 2 hrs. 
Spoon 3 cups of stuffing in center of roast. 
Coat 3 qt. baking dish with cooking spray. 
Spoon remaining stuffing in dish. Bake the 
roast and remaining stuffing for 45 mins.
Plate the roast. Cover with foil. Let stand 
15 mins. Remove foil. Spoon stuffing from 
center of roast into small serving dish. 
Remove strings from roast.
To serve, slice between bones. Serve with 
stuffing.
Crown pork roasts are available by special 
order, so plan accordingly. Ask for chine 
(back) bone completely removed, and the 
exposed bones Frenched. Most butchers 
will include little paper stockings to cover
the rib bones.

include in every issue, but I wish you’d make it 
easier to tell how much a serving constitutes.

This version of the classic Italian 
bean-and-pasta soup features but-
ter beans (giant lima beans). The 
meaty yet silky texture of the beans 
contrasts nicely with the chewy dital-
ini pasta. Serve the soup with some 
good crusty bread.  Serves 6 to 8
 
3 Tbs. extra-virgin olive oil
 
4  large cloves garlic, finely chopped
 
2 ribs celery, finely chopped
 
1  medium onion, finely chopped
 
  Kosher salt and freshly ground 
black pepper
 
5 cups lower-salt chicken broth
 
1 28-oz. can crushed tomatoes
 
4   to 5 large basil leaves, coarsely 
torn; more small leaves for 
garnish (optional)
 
1 bay leaf
  
 2  15-oz. cans butter beans, drained 
and rinsed
 
1 cup ditalini pasta
 
13   oz. Grana Padano or Parmigiano-

Heat the olive oil in a 7- to 8-quart heavy-
duty pot over medium-low heat. Add the 
garlic, celery, onion, 1 tsp. salt, and ¼   tsp. 
pepper. Cook, stirring, until soft but not 
brown, about 5 minutes.

roast chicken
with chickpeas
Crisp-skinned bone-in chicken thighs 
braise in a stew of fragrant Indian 
spices, onion, and chickpeas, each 
flavoring the other, in this simple and 
satisfying main course.  Serves 4
 
1  Tbs. olive oil 
 
8   skin-on, bone-in chicken thighs 
(about 3 lb.)
 
 Kosher salt
 
1   small yellow onion, finely chopped
 
4  medium cloves garlic, finely chopped 
 
2 tsp. ground turmeric
 
2  tsp. ground cumin
 
1  tsp. ground coriander
 
¼  tsp. cayenne pepper;  more to taste
 
½  cup lower-salt chicken broth
 
2  15-oz. cans chickpeas, rinsed and 
drained
 
¼  cup chopped fresh cilantro
 
 Lemon wedges, for serving
Position a rack in the center of the oven and 
heat the oven to 425°F.
Heat the oil in a 12-inch ovenproof skillet 
over medium-high heat. Pat the chicken dry 
if wet and season lightly with salt. Work-
ing in two batches, brown the chicken on 
both sides, about 10 minutes per batch, 
adjusting the heat as necessary. Transfer to 
a plate.
Pour off all but 1 Tbs. of the fat in the skil-
let. Add the onion, garlic, turmeric, cumin, 
coriander, cayenne, and ½   tsp. salt. Cook 
over medium heat, stirring occasionally, 
until the onion softens, about 5 minutes. 
Stir in the broth, scraping up any 
browned bits. Add the chickpeas and bring 
to a simmer. Return the thighs to the skil-
let, skin side up. Transfer to the oven and 
roast until the chicken cooks through, 20 to 
25 minutes. Top with the cilantro and serve 
with lemon wedges.                   —Jan Newberry

penne with ricotta,  
caramelized onion,  
and radicchio
Sweet caramelized onion and creamy 
cheese tame radicchio’s bitter nature 
in this wintertime pasta. Fried capers 
add a crisp jolt of brightness to just 
about every bite.  Serves 6
 
1/4  cup olive oil
 
1/2  cup large capers, rinsed and patted dry
 
1  Tbs. fresh thyme leaves
 
 Kosher salt
 
1   lb. penne pasta
 
1   medium yellow onion,  
halved and thinly sliced
 
1   medium head radicchio,  
halved through the stem, cored, 
and thinly sliced (about 5 cups)
 
1/4   tsp. crushed red pepper flakes;  
more to taste
 11/2  cups fresh whole-milk ricotta
Bring a large pot of well-salted water to a boil. 
Heat 2 Tbs. of the oil in a 12-inch skillet 
over medium-high heat. add the capers 
and thyme, cover with a splatter shield (if 
you have one), and fry, stirring occasion-
ally, until the capers are golden and puffed, 
2 to 3 minutes. Using a slotted spoon, 
transfer the capers and thyme to a paper-
towel-lined plate. Wipe out the skillet.
Boil the pasta to al dente according to 
package directions. Reserve ½ cup of the 
cooking water, then drain the pasta. 
Meanwhile, heat the remaining 2 Tbs. 
oil in the skillet over medium heat. add the 
onion and cook, stirring occasionally, until 
golden, 6 to 8 minutes. add the radicchio, 
pepper flakes, and 1 tsp. salt. cook, stirring 
occasionally, until the radicchio and onion 
are soft and tender, about 4 minutes. 
Put ½ cup of the ricotta in a large bowl 
and whisk in the reserved pasta cooking 
water. add the pasta and toss to coat. add 
the onion and radicchio and toss to com-
bine. Stir in half of the capers and season 
to taste with salt. Serve dolloped with the 
remaining 1 cup ricotta and topped with 
the remaining capers.     —Erica Clark

red wines from italy’s Marche region 
have zesty dark fruits and salty over-
tones perfect for the sweet cheese 
and onions in this scrumptious pasta.

capers also make

Imagine a single ingredient that does

caramel-like sweetness, and just a pleasant

seared cod with black garlic sauce
Tomatoes make a tangy contrast to the sweet, umami-rich black garlic 
sauce and delicate, buttery cod. Serve with sautéed vegetables, such as 
bok choy.  serves 4

 Kosher salt

In an 8-inch skillet, melt 3 Tbs. of the

From Korea (we think)
The origins of black garlic are a bit mys-
terious, but some producers claim access 
to a 4,000-year-old Korean recipe. Others 
agree that black garlic’s history is that 
old, but say it originated in China. What 
we know for sure is that in 2004, a South 
Korean man named Scott Kim patented 
a machine to produce black garlic on a 
commercial scale. Other commercial 
producers followed suit, so black garlic 
is now available at high-end grocers 
nationwide. It’s also become a favorite in 
restaurant kitchens. Many chefs make 
their own in-house, cooking the garlic 
around 140°F for up to a month. 
It’s worth noting that while black gar-
lic is often called a fermented product, 
there’s no fermentation taking place, just 
low, extremely slow caramelization.
Keep it cool
Black garlic is sold packaged in the pro-
duce section of well-stocked supermar-
kets and specialty markets, so there’s not 
much to selecting it. Just check the  
expiration date on the package and pick 
the one that’s farthest away. It can be 
stored at room temperature for up to 
one month and refrigerated for up to four.
Go bold with flavor
Quite sticky and a bit chewy, black garlic 
is best chopped very finely or puréed. 
Use it in salad dressings, sweet or savory 
sauces, and dips. It’s also a great addition 
to braises, soups, and stir-fries.
The sweet flavor of black garlic pairs 
well with acidic ingredients, such as 
vinegar and citrus, and with pungent 
elements, such as cheese and strong fish. 
Fresh herbs, such as the cilantro in the 
fish at right, are a nice, bright counter-
point. Black garlic can even work well 
with sweet flavors like strawberries and 
vanilla, much the way balsamic vinegar 
does.
—Layla Schlack
Pair With: Zweigelt The delicate 
but spicy character of these austrian 
reds complements the black garlic 
and cilantro.

“Don’t flip a steak too much during cooking.” “Don’t eat cooked 
pork that’s pink inside.” “Rinse your chicken before you cook it.” 
You’ve probably heard these and other nuggets of kitchen wis-
dom when it comes to cooking meat, but is it good advice? Not 
necessarily. Here, we’ll take a closer look at some of the meat-
cooking “rules” that science—and common sense—disprove. 
Myth: Sear meat to seal in the juice
This persistent myth was started by German chemist and food 
scientist Justus von Liebig in his 1847 book, Researches on the 
Chemistry of Food. If it were true, there’d be no need for juice 
grooves on carving boards. You can see with your own eyes that 
after you sear a piece of meat, the juice leaks out as the meat rests. 
There’s still a good reason to sear your meat, though: to boost 
flavor. Searing causes chemical reactions that create hundreds of 
new flavor compounds, which taste absolutely delicious.
Myth: Poke or cut a steak, and it will lose 
most of its juice
Sticking a fork into meat to turn it while cooking will release some 
juice around the punctures, but won’t cause complete moisture 
loss. A steak isn’t a balloon. Its meat fibers are self-contained, and 
puncturing a few will release juice only in that group of fibers.
However, avoid using a blade “tenderizer,” a product available at 
kitchenware stores. This tool aims to soften meat before cooking by 
puncturing it with hundreds of pins, but this can cause significant 
moisture loss during cooking and does little to tenderize tough 
fibers. Don’t buy pre-tenderized beef for the same reason.
Myth:   Turn a steak as little as possible  
while cooking
Most steakhouse chefs do the exact opposite: They turn the meat 
often, creating a more evenly browned and flavorful crust all over the 
steak’s surface. Turning a grilled steak only once or twice confines 
browning to the grill marks and doesn’t deliver as much flavor as an 
evenly browned crust.
Myth:   Rare meat is bloody
It’s common to refer to juicy, rare red meat as bloody, but there’s 
no actual blood in meat. Blood is contained in an animal’s 
circulatory system, which is separate from the muscles that we 
eat as meat. The red liquid in meat is a combination of water 
and protein, particularly the iron-containing protein myoglo-
bin, which gives meat its red color while carrying and storing 
oxygen in muscle tissue. Myoglobin does for muscles what 
hemoglobin does for blood, but they aren’t the same thing.

Myth:  Cooked pork or chicken is unsafe  
to eat if it’s pink inside
Temperature is the only accurate measurement of doneness—
color is unreliable. The U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) 
says that as long as cooked pork reaches 145°F and chicken 
reaches 165°F, they’re perfectly safe. Though both pork and 
chicken become paler as they cook, myoglobin, the red protein 
in meat, can maintain its color at recommended doneness tem-
peratures. In addition, the soft, porous bones of young animals 
can leach iron-containing pigments from marrow into meat, 
causing it to darken. 
Myth:   Fat makes meat juicy
The water that’s bound to the protein in meat is the only thing that 
makes the meat juicy, but the presence of fat can make meat “taste” 
juicy. It does this by triggering a primal salivation response in the 
eater. Fat is a concentrated form of calories, and we humans are 
evolutionarily primed to seek out and appreciate calorie-rich foods. 
Hence, we salivate when we anticipate eating fat, and the moisture 
in our mouths makes the meat we’re eating seem juicier. 
Myth:   “Natural” meat is free of antibiotics
To date, the Food & Drug Administration hasn’t defined the 
term natural. The agency doesn’t object to using the term if 
food contains no added colors, artificial flavors, or synthetic 
substances. That loose guidance echoes the USDA’s definition, 
which requires that natural meat has “no artificial ingredient or 
added color and is only minimally processed.” This means that 
the meat was processed in a manner that does not fundamen-
tally alter the product.
However, these details from the USDA don’t govern what 
happens to animals prior to slaughter. Meat labeled natural  
may come from animals treated with antibiotics, fed geneti-
cally modified corn, and/or raised in a “concentrated animal 
feeding operation” (CAFO), all of which consumers may 
consider unnatural. 
Myth: Always rinse poultry before cooking
Yes, the surface of raw poultry is covered with bacteria, and 
washing a chicken or turkey under running water may get some 
of the bacteria off the bird. It also splashes the bacteria into your 
sink, onto your counters, and onto any food that may be nearby. 
So definitely don’t wash your poultry—just cook it to 165°F and 
let the heat kill the bacteria.

Duck!
With three easy methods 
for cooking this delicious bird, 
there’s no excuse not to. 
b y J o h n A s h

--- Page 53 ---
Americans love to eat chicken, 
but I’m here to extol another 
bird: duck. With its dark, rich, tender 
meat, often capped with crisp, crackly skin, 
duck is a treat too many of us reserve for 
dinner out or holiday eating. While duck is 
a perfect choice in both cases, it’s so deli-
cious and so versatile that it really should 
be enjoyed more than just a couple of times 
a year. If you can cook chicken, you can 
cook duck. On the following pages, I’ll 
show you how.
Look for duck at your supermarket. Not only is duck 
as easy to cook as chicken, it’s also becoming almost as easy 
to find as more and more supermarkets stock it. By a huge 
margin, the most popular domesticated breed in the United 
States is the mild, tender White Pekin. Originally from 
China, these ducks were brought to the U.S. in the late 1800s 
and became such a huge industry on Long Island in New 
York that they’re sometimes also referred to as Long Island 
ducklings. A hurricane all but wiped out the industry there, 
though, and Long Island property later became too valuable 
for duck farming. Now, most Pekin ducks actually come 
from Indiana, Wisconsin, California, and Canada.
Though Pekin is most widely available, you may also come 
across Muscovy and Moulard ducks (see Sources, p. 109). 
Muscovy, native to Mexico and northern South America, 
has a deeper, more gamey flavor, and breasts from the male 
(drake) can be quite large. Moulard, a sterile hybrid of the 
male Pekin and the female Muscovy, has a rich flavor that’s 
only slightly gamey. You can buy Moulard duck breasts    »
f i n e cooki n g .com  53

--- Page 54 ---
that are the same size as Pekin, but since Moulard is  
a popular breed to fatten for foie gras, their breasts  
can grow to be twice as large as normal. Such fattened 
breasts are called magrets, and one breast half can serve 
two people. 
No matter which duck you choose—and often this 
simply depends on what your market or butcher car-
ries—it will be just a little more expensive than chicken, 
$4 to $6 per pound compared to $2 to $3 per pound for a 
whole chicken. But perhaps it’s actually better to compare 
its cost with that of a good steak, because that’s the kind 
of full-flavored satisfaction duck delivers. By that mea-
sure, duck is a bargain.
The cut helps determine the cooking method. 
As they say, there’s more than one way to cook a goose, 
and that’s true for duck, too. Like chicken, a whole bird 
is the perfect choice for roasting. My favorite way to do 
that is to simply season it, add a few aromatics to its cav-
ity, and then stick it in a low oven for three hours. The 
meat gets cooked to well done but becomes as tender as 
can be. You can stop there and be really happy with just 
the duck meat, but that would be a crime because the 
juice and browned bits left in the pan—what chefs call a 
fond—can be used to make a delicious pan sauce. 
Duck breasts are perfect for sear-roasting—that 
is, searing them on the stovetop until the skin is a 
beautiful mahogany and then finishing them in the 
oven. Duck breasts cooked this way taste best rare to 
medium rare, which gives them a steak-like chew and 
almost beefy flavor.
Duck legs can be a little tougher than breasts, which 
is why I like to braise them. The slow, gentle, moist 
cooking guarantees they’ll turn out tender. To keep 
the skin crisp, the key is to make sure it stays above the 
liquid so it doesn’t soften during braising.
Duck requires a smidge more prep than  
chicken. One of the best things about duck is all the fat 
it stores; during cooking, that fat bastes the meat, keep-
ing it moist and flavorful. But the excess fat also means 
it needs a little more prep and attention during cooking. 
Before cooking, you’ll want to trim away any excess 
skin and fat, just as you would with chicken. During 
cooking, it may be necessary to spoon off the fat that 
accumulates in the pan. For duck breasts, there’s an  
additional step as well: The skin should be crosshatched 
to promote the rendering of the layer of fat below it (see 
p. 58), which makes for deliciously crisp skin. For any-
one with a sharp knife, this step is easy. Once the duck 
breast is ready to go, it cooks in minutes, which means 
there’s really no reason not to make it tonight. 
Chef John Ash is an instructor at the Culinary Institute of 
America’s Napa Valley campus. His most recent cookbook is 
Culinary Birds.

Roasted Rack of Pork
Need an impressive yet easy-to-make centerpiece for your holiday feast? 
Look no further.  BY BRUCE AIDELLS
Like most families, mine expects 
our holiday meal to be some-
thing extraordinary, and as the 
guy who’s expected to provide it, I think 
a roasted rack of pork ﬁ ts the bill perfect
ly. It’s a less expensive alternative to a 
standing beef rib roast, but just as 
impressive. Better still—especially if 
you’re overwhelmed by holiday anxiety—
is that this recipe is very easy to make. 
Just season the pork, preferably the night 
before, and pop it into the oven. A quick 
but elegant morel mushroom pan sauce 
is the ﬁ nishing touch, transforming the 
roast from simple to spectacular.
Meat expert Bruce Aidells is the author of 
12 cookbooks, including the James Beard 
Award-nominated Bruce Aidells’s Complete 
Book of Pork.

--- Page 77 ---
Need to Know
Look for heritage or natu-
rally raised pork. Many heritage 
breeds, such as Berkshire or Old 
Spot, have more marbling, which 
results in juicier, tastier meat. 
If there’s no shop near you that 
carries heritage pork, look for 
naturally raised pork that hasn’t 
been given antibiotics or growth 
stimulants. Definitely avoid so-
called “extra-juicy” or “enhanced” 
pork that’s been treated or 
soaked in a salt solution, which 
can negatively affect its texture. 
Whatever you choose, you’ll likely 
need to order the rack ahead, 
since it’s not an item butchers 
always have on hand.
Have your butcher remove the 
chine bone and french the rib 
bones. The chine bone runs per-
pendicular to the rib bones and 
interferes with carving the roast 
into chops if it’s not removed. 
Frenching the rib bones, or scrap-
ing them clean, gives the roast a 
polished look.
Start hot, then lower the heat. 
I like to start the pork roasting in  
a hot oven to kick off the brown-
ing process and give the fat a  
good chance to start rendering. 
Then, once the roast is golden 
brown, I lower the heat and let it 
finish more slowly to reduce the 
risk of overcooking and to let it 
stay juicy. Letting the rack sit at 
room temperature for a couple 
of hours before roasting helps it 
cook more evenly. 
Allow for carry-over cooking. 
Today’s pork is very lean and can 
get tough if it overcooks. Cooking 
the meat to a final temperature of 
145°F ensures that it’ll be tender 
and juicy. This means you’ll want 
to pull the roast from the oven 
when it reaches 130°F, tent it with 
foil, and let the roast’s residual 
heat finish cooking it to 145°F.
Use a continuous-read  
thermometer with an alarm to 
determine doneness. Just set 
it to go off when the pork reaches 
130°F, and you won’t have to keep 
opening the oven to check on the 
roast. (To learn more about this 
handy tool, see page 107.) Once 
the alarm goes off, check a couple 
of other areas of the roast with 
an instant-read thermometer to 
make sure the whole roast is at 
the correct temperature.

and savory toppings. So then I wondered: What 
other even more exciting flavored popcorns could 
I come up with?
I began by borrowing some of my favorite 
flavor combinations from cooking and baking. 
Take gremolata, for example. A simple mixture 
of parsley, lemon zest, and garlic, gremolata is 
traditionally sprinkled over braised veal shanks. 
Add some butter, and those bright flavors also 
punch up popcorn. As a pastry chef, I also 
looked to sweet combinations, such as chocolate 
and coconut. Indeed, once I got going, the  
options for flavoring popcorn seemed endless.
But why make your own flavored popcorn 
when you can buy it at the supermarket? For 
starters, the flavored popcorn you make at home 
will taste fresher. You’re also not likely to find 
anything like these flavorings, which range from 
intriguing yet oddly addicting (Dukkah-Spiced 
Popcorn, for example) to pleasantly aromatic 
(Truffle & Parmesan Popcorn) to supremely 
soothing (Cinnamon Toast Popcorn).
Plus, homemade flavored popcorn comes in 
handy during the holiday season. Put some out 
to enjoy with cocktails, add a savory flavored 
popcorn to the holiday buffet table, or serve 
one of the sweet ones along with dessert for 
people who want just a small bite of something 
to finish a meal. Flavored popcorn makes a 
great hostess gift, too—as long as you can keep 
from eating it yourself!

Popcorn Popping 101
Though you can use air-popped or even  
unflavored microwave popcorn for these recipes,  
I prefer popcorn popped on the stove. Here are  
some tips for successful popping.
• Start with fresh kernels. Old kernels can dry out and  
will either not pop or will pop into small pieces. 
• Use a heavy-bottomed pot that distributes heat evenly  
so there are fewer hot spots to burn the popcorn.
• Pop the corn quickly. A quick pop keeps it tender;  
this is why heating the oil is so essential.
• Leave the lid cracked or cover with a screen. Allowing  
some steam to escape keeps the popcorn from getting soggy.
• Shake the pot to prevent burning and to move unpopped  
kernels to the bottom of the pot so that they can pop.

Mirin matters
Mirin, a very sweet, low-
alcohol rice wine, is a widely 
used Japanese ingredient. 
(In this issue, it’s used in 
Lemongrass-Ginger Soup 
with Swiss Chard on p. 37.) 
You can find it in most super-
markets these days. Some 
mass-produced brands have 
more corn syrup and other 
sweeteners in them; they’re 
sometimes labeled aji-mirin, 
which roughly translates as 
“tastes like mirin.” We test 
our recipes with this widely 
available style as well as 
higher quality mirin. With 
hon-mirin, or “real mirin,” 
you get a flavor that’s both 
sweet and a little savory. 
Made from fermented rice, 
it’s usually higher in alcohol, 
too. Sake is another kind of 
rice wine made for drinking; 
it’s not as sweet as mirin and 
has a higher alcohol content. 
If you’re looking for a substi-
tute for mirin, a sweet sherry 
might be a better bet.
—Kelley Baylis

Truffle oil truths
Most commercial truﬄ  e oils, including those 
produced by D’Artagnan (right) and Urbani, are 
made by mixing vegetable oil or light olive oil with 
one or more synthetic chemical compounds that 
mimic the most prominent of the hundreds of aro-
matic molecules contained in truﬄ  es. Drizzled over 
popcorn (p. 93) or a breadcrumb topping (p. 75), its 
fragrance is unmistakable. T ough black truﬄ  e oil 
is generally made with vegetable oil and white with 
olive oil, their ﬂ avor is similar, and they can be used 
interchangeably. Truﬄ  e oil made from real truﬄ  es 
is rare nowadays. However, Oregon White Truﬄ  e 
Oil (see Sources, p. 109) contains no chemicals of 
any kind. Instead, it’s infused with genuine truﬄ  es 
harvested in Oregon. As the maker notes, a natu-
rally infused truﬄ  e oil can never be as powerful as 
a chemically infused truﬄ  e oil, but some ﬁ nd that 
what it lacks in potency, it makes up for in nuance 
and complexity.                                                  —J.S.
