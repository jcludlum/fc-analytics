Google the question above, and you’ll find there’s no 
shortage of opinions, from thoughtfully considered blog 
posts to semicoherent threads on message boards. My 
opinion lands squarely in the “heck yeah!” camp. Here’s 
why: I think we feel more alive when we’re outdoors. 
Maybe it’s primal. Maybe it’s because our senses are 
heightened outside. We feel the breeze on our skin, see 
and hear the birds darting about, smell the flowers or the 
fresh-cut grass. Some might say these are distractions, 
but I think they prime us to taste our food better, too. 
Or maybe it’s because when we eat outdoors, we’re often doing something else that 
makes us happy, like listening to a concert in the park or enjoying the company of 
friends on the patio, and that translates to enjoying our food more as well. What-
ever the reason, if given the choice, I prefer to eat outside.
In this issue, we have lots of recipes that are tailor-made for eating (and often 
cooking) alfresco. Let’s start with that picnic in the park. You could use our feature 
on grain bowls (p. 74) to create tasty, satisfying, and easy-to-transport one-bowl 
meals. Or you could throw together a classic salade Niçoise (p. 67), stuff it into a 
boule, and call it a pan bagnat (also on p. 67), the iconic French picnic sandwich. As 
for cooking outdoors, on p. 52 BBQ expert Elizabeth Karmel shares her best tips for 
how to grill boneless chicken breasts so they turn out juicy, not dry.
The rest of the issue is devoted to making the most of summer’s abundance of fresh, 
locally grown fruits and vegetables. Do you have a bumper crop of squash? You’ll find 
10 inventive and super-speedy ways to use it up starting on p. 60. Is company com-
ing? Try a galette (p. 78). Deceptively easy to make (which can be your little secret), 
these rustic tarts never fail to impress, and depending on whether you fill them with 
vegetables or fruit, they can be either your main course or your dessert. Last but not 
least, you might want to set aside some of the season’s bounty for future good eats, so 
we asked one of our favorite Italian cooking authorities, Domenica Marchetti, to show 
us how to make her favorite sweet-and-sour Italian pickles (p. 68). 
Happy cooking (whether indoors or out)!
—Jennifer Armentrout, editor

BOOKS THAT COOK
The Reading List
Get thee to the farmers’ market.  BY JOAN N E S MART
A Modern Way To Cook
150+ Vegetarian Recipes for Quick, 
Flavor-Packed Meals 
By Anna Jones
(Ten Speed Press; $35)
Apart from my love of bacon, what keeps 
me from being a vegetarian is the notion 
that preparing complete vegetarian meals 
requires a lot of time. Anna Jones, a London 
chef, food stylist, and writer who worked 
with Jamie Oliver for years, is making me 
rethink that notion with recipes the author 
swears come together in “life-friendly 
times.” Her first chapter, “In the Time It 
Takes to Set the Table,” features dishes 
ready in 15 minutes or less, while succeed-
ing chapters contain recipes ready in 20, 30, 
and 45 minutes. For the most part, these 
are single dishes (soups, salads, tacos, cas-
seroles) suited to a casual lunch or dinner. A 
chapter called “Investment Cooking,” which 
contains longer-cooking recipes for things 
like home-cooked beans, is also handy. The 
appealing photos are bright and airy, and 
spreads sprinkled throughout feature quick 
and easy recipe inspirations like One-Pan 
Dinners or Goodness Bowls, a create-your-
own-recipe approach similar to our own 
grain-bowl story on p. 74. The book’s friendly 
voice, intriguing flavor combinations, and 
helpful tips may inspire me to eat more 
vegetables, right alongside my bacon.  
One to try: Butternut and Sweet Leek 
Hash. A creamy herb dressing takes this 
hash to another level. 
Preserving Italy
Canning, Curing, Infusing, and Bottling 
Italian Flavors and Traditions 
By Domenica Marchetti 
(Houghton Mifflin Harcourt; $23) 
Books on preserving are many, but what 
makes this one a standout is its unique point 
of view. Domenica Marchetti, a longtime 
contributor to Fine Cooking, is Italian by 
heritage and visits Italy regularly. In this, her 
seventh book on Italian cooking, Marchetti 
delves deep into the Italian methods for 
preserving the kinds of ingredients beloved 
in that country—tomatoes, olives, eggplant, 
quince, beans, and basil—and how to then 
use them in scrumptious dishes. (Our 
feature on p. 68 is derived from her book.) 
Though mostly focused on fruits and veg-
etables, there are also chapters on curing 
meats and making fresh cheeses, liqueurs, 
and confections. And some recipes don’t 
even require advanced processing steps. 
Her aromatic Porchetta Salt is one such 
recipe, and her broths, pastas, and pestos 
can simply be frozen. Sidebars throughout 
the book show off Marchetti’s journalism 
skills (she used to be a reporter) and are fun 
to read, preferably while eating some toast 
slathered with her Apricot-Anise Jam.
A new pantry staple: Lemon Olive Oil. 
Easy to make, and oh so good.
Dandelion & Quince
Exploring the Wide World of Unusual 
Vegetables, Fruits, and Herbs
By Michelle McKenzie
(Roost Books; $35) 
If, like me, you are old enough to remember 
when arugula seemed exotic, you know 
that the sheer variety of fruits and veg-
etables available at supermarkets and, even 
more so, farmers’ markets has increased 
exponentially. But such abundance can 
leave one stymied: What to do with all 
those huckleberries? Is green garlic used 
like regular garlic? What do nettles taste 
like, anyway?  In her debut cookbook, 
Michelle McKenzie, head of 18 Reasons, a 
nonprofit community cooking school in San 
Francisco, answers these and many more 
produce-related questions. With 35 plant 
profiles—from Asian pears to sunchokes—
and more than 150 recipes, this informative 
collection will have you reconsidering celery 
leaves and bolted herbs, persimmon, and 
purslane. What McKenzie calls unusual is 
subjective, which is why shallots made the 
cut. As she’s discovered in her cooking 
classes, there are people out there unfamil-
iar with this cousin of the onion. But once 
they try her Crispy Shallots and Shallot Oil, 
they’ll never forget them. 
Don’t Miss: Baby Dandelion Salad 
with Fresh Figs and Pancetta. The 
pancetta is optional but adds crisp tex-
ture, and its flavor marries the sweet fruit

Little lion heads
Like all sweet and hot chile peppers, 
shishitos (Capsicum annuum) are 
descended from South or Central 
American plants. It’s likely that Portu-
guese explorers brought chiles to Japan 
several centuries ago, and some were 
bred into mild shishitos. One in every 
10 or so is as hot as a mild jalapeño 
(there’s no way to know until you taste 
them). The Japanese named the pep-
pers shishi, or lion, either because they 
used to be much hotter or the puckered 
tip looks like the head of a lion.
Now, shishito peppers are grown in 
the U.S. as well—they’re prolific during 
late summer in any temperate climate. 
Look for wrinkles
Farmers’ markets and well-stocked 
grocery stores carry shishitos. They’re 
about 3 inches long and narrow with 
firm, glossy skin. They have vertical 
wrinkles and are a bit twisty—that’s 
how you distinguish them from similar 
but spicier Padrón peppers. Although 
they’re usually picked while still green, 
you may find the occasional red or 
orange ones, too. They can be refriger-
ated in an open paper bag for up to  
two weeks. 
Char to enhance flavor
Shishito peppers can be eaten raw, 
seeds and all, but they’re usually 
grilled, roasted, or sautéed so the out-
side gets blistered but the flesh remains 
firm—the char brings out the peppers’ 
sweet heat. Avoid overcooking, though, 
because shishitos quickly turn bitter. 
They’re often served with Japanese 
ingredients, such as sesame seeds, 
toasted sesame oil, and soy sauce,  
as in the dish at right. Citrus like 
lemon or yuzu is a good match, as are 
nutty flavors like browned butter and 
toasted almonds.
—Layla Schlack

Pasta is one of the most popular foods in the world, and 
along with this widespread popularity come many questions about 
buying and cooking it: Is a $5 bag of Italian artisanal dried pasta 
any diﬀ erent from a 79¢ box of pasta made in America? Is your 
spaghetti done when it sticks to the wall? Science holds the answers. 
Is fresh pasta better 
than dried?
No. One pasta style isn’t quantita-
tively better than another; they’re 
just diﬀ erent types of pasta. 
Aside from the drying, the 
main diﬀ erence is ﬂ our. Fresh 
pasta is usually made with 
varieties of Triticum aestivum, 
a species of wheat that’s ﬁ nely 
ground and reﬁ ned into white 
ﬂ our that has about 10% to 12% 
protein. T e small particle size 
and moderate protein content of this ﬂ our—along with the common 
addition of eggs—helps to give fresh pasta its ﬁ ner, sof er, smoother 
texture. Dried pasta is almost always made with a harder species of 
wheat, Triticum durum, which is more coarsely ground into a type 
of ﬂ our called semolina. T e larger particle size and relatively high 
protein content (about 13%) of this ﬂ our—along with the drying 
process—gives dried pasta a ﬁ rmer, chewier texture. 
For either type, various other factors—ingredient quality, mixing 
and shaping methods, drying processes (for dried pasta), and cook-
ing techniques—inﬂ uence the ﬁ nal ﬂ avor and texture. In the end, 
high-quality dried pasta can taste better than low-quality fresh pasta.
Is expensive dried pasta worth the money?
Yes, if the pasta is well made. Two key factors aﬀ ect the quality of 
dried pasta: how the pasta is shaped, or extruded, and how it’s dried. 
To make dried pasta, semolina is mixed with water—typically 
70% semolina to 30% water by weight. T e dough is then kneaded 
and extruded (forced) through the dies (holes) of a pasta machine, 
creating various pasta shapes. Pasta extruded through bronze dies 
has a rougher surface, making it more porous and allowing more 
water to ﬂ ow in and sof en the starch during cooking. As a result, 
it tends to have a pleasingly sof  yet ﬁ rm texture when cooked. 
T e rougher surface also helps the sauce cling to the pasta. Most 
mass-market pastas are extruded through dies coated with Teﬂ on, 
which is faster and easier to clean than bronze. Teﬂ on reduces the 
surface tension during extrusion and gives the pasta a more uni-
form, slick surface that won’t let in as much water during cooking, 
so the cooked pasta stays ﬁ rmer.
Drying time and temperature make an even bigger diﬀ erence. 
Some mass-market pasta is dried at high temperatures of 190°F 
or more for only a few hours. High heat toughens proteins, so the 
pasta’s gluten (protein) network becomes so tight that it traps the 
starch granules. T is inhibits the cooking water from reaching the 
starch and prevents the pasta from getting sof  (a process called 
gelation); when cooked al dente (see p. 44), the pasta instead has a 
rubbery, bouncy texture. With continued cooking, the pasta may 
eventually become mushy.
By contrast, more expensive pastas are typically dried for up to 
ﬁ ve days at lower temperatures of about 100°F. T e pasta develops 
a looser gluten network, leaving more starch available to bond 
with water during boiling, yielding a sof er, more pleasant texture 
when the pasta is cooked al dente. Low-temperature drying may 
also preserve some of the volatile aroma compounds in durum 
wheat, improving the pasta’s ﬂ avor. 
Using bronze dies and drying slowly require more time and 
money, so many expensive brands advertise the use of these methods 
on their packaging to help explain the higher price tag. Pasta that’s 
been extruded through bronze 
dies appears sandy and opaque 
(as opposed to smooth and shiny) 
on the surface.

Italian flour the best? 
Not necessarily. T e country 
of origin matters less than the 
freshness of the ﬂ our.

Why should pasta be cooked in salted water?
Most store-bought pasta—both fresh and dried—doesn’t contain 
salt. As pasta cooks and hydrates, it absorbs the salty water, which 
seasons the pasta. Salt also tightens the gluten proteins, and there’s 
some evidence that it limits starch gelation, which may help pre-
vent cooked pasta from becoming mushy. 
However, salting the water won’t raise the boiling point and 
slow down the time it takes to reach a boil or speed up the cook-
ing, as some believe. One ounce of salt added per quart of water 
raises the boiling point by only 1°F, and that amount of salt is 
roughly ﬁ ve times more than we recommend adding to pasta 
cooking water. As a rule of thumb, add about 1½ teaspoons kosher 
or sea salt per quart of water.
Does adding oil to the cooking water prevent sticking?
No. Oil doesn’t keep pasta from sticking—movement does. 
Oil ﬂ oats on the surface of the water and has no eﬀ ect on the 
pasta cooking below. At the beginning of boiling, the surface of 
each noodle has barely been moistened. If one noodle should rest 
upon another, the small amount of water between them will be 
absorbed, and the partially gelated starch on the surface will glue 
them together. Stirring frequently at the beginning of cooking keeps 
the noodles separate from one another. Once the water returns to a 
rapid boil, convection currents help to keep the pasta moving. Start-
ing with lots of rapidly boiling water—about 5 quarts per pound of 
pasta—also helps by giving the pasta plenty of room to move once 
the convection currents take hold. Sticking af er cooking comes 
from surface starch that cools and dries as the noodles drain. Wet-
ting and warming the cooked noodles by immediately tossing them 
with hot pasta sauce separates them. 
What does “cook until al dente” mean?
T e Italian term al dente means “to the tooth”; it’s the sweet spot 
where pasta is neither too sof  nor too hard. When noodles are boiled, 
their protein and starch absorb water and expand. On the surface, the 
protein expands so far that it ruptures, and the starch ﬂ ows into the 
cooking water. Inside the pasta, less water is available, so the protein 
and starch stay more intact. When cooked al dente, the surface of the 
noodle contains about 85% water, and the center about 50%. When 
cooking pasta, use the package timing as a guide, and take a bite to 
test for doneness. T e center should be chewy but not hard and no 
longer opaque. T e old test of throwing a strand of cooked pasta 
against a wall to see if it sticks may reveal that the surface starch has 
gelated, but it says nothing about the doneness of the interior.
Should I rinse pasta after cooking?
It depends on what you’re making. Rinsing removes surface starch 
that helps sauce stick to hot pasta. But for a cold pasta salad, rinsing 
oﬀ  that surface starch prevents a gluey texture in the ﬁ nished dish.
Why should I save the pasta cooking water?
It contains starch, which can help thicken pasta sauce. Restaurant 
chefs of en slightly undercook pasta in boiling water, and then ﬁ nish 
cooking it right in the sauce over high heat with some of the pasta 
water, all while shaking the pan vigorously. T e shaking releases 
even more starch from the surface of the pasta and helps to further 
thicken the sauce. And if the pasta absorbs so much of the sauce that 
it seems dry, another splash of the cooking water is the perfect ﬁ x.

BRINE quickly
Brining is a foolproof method for preventing 
chicken from drying out. At its most basic, 
brining means soaking in a salt and water 
solution, but sugar is usually added to bal-
ance the salt flavor and promote browning. 
As salt enters the meat cells, it alters the 
structure of the muscle fibers and proteins, 
swelling their water-holding capacity. It also 
draws in the brine’s flavors, seasoning from 
the inside out. (Don’t season with additional 
salt, or it will taste too salty.) 
Brining can take hours for a larger piece 
of meat, but chicken breast needs just 
20 minutes; too long and it will become too 
salty. For the most basic brine, dissolve 
½ cup salt and ½ cup sugar (granulated or 
brown) in a gallon of water. Let it cool, and 
then soak the chicken. The result is pure 
chicken flavor. Brines are also a great way 
to add additional subtle flavor, as in the 
sweet-tea brine at left, which gets extra 
sweetness from the lemonade.

Salade Niçoise
This classic French salad may not be exactly  
what you think it is.   b y Ja m i e S c h le r
thirty years ago, after arriving in 
France at the height of summer, 
one of the first memorable dishes 
i had was a salade Niçoise. The com-
posed main-course salad was beautiful: a 
carefully plated mix of fresh vegetables, 
briny black olives, juicy tomatoes, silken 
canned tuna, and hard-cooked eggs. 
Brimming with a wild variety of flavors 
and contrasting textures, this new-to-me 
salad—named after the city of Nice on the 
French Riviera—was both satisfying and 
refreshing. These days, I’m still in France, 
married to a Frenchman and busy run-
ning our hotel in Chinon. And on a hot 
summer day, I still crave a salade Niçoise 
(nee-SWAHZ).
Traditionally, there’s little cooking 
involved in preparing the salad: The fish 
comes from a can or jar, and ideally, the 
eggs are cooked earlier in the cooler part 
of the day. Steamed green beans and 
boiled potatoes have made their way into 
American and Parisian bistro versions, but 
what you’ll find in Nice is usually a salad of 
crisp, just-picked garden vegetables, most 
likely favettes, the tender tiny fava beans 
popular in the Mediterranean region, or 
thin, raw slices of the gorgeous purple 
artichoke known as Violet de Provence. 
Indeed, as long as you include the basic 
elements, which most agree are tomatoes, 
olives, eggs, and anchovies or tuna (or 
both), and you don’t toss it (a big no-no), 
there really is no single right way to make 
this salad. Just be sure to serve it with a 
chilled glass of wine and a crusty loaf of 
bread, bien sûr.

Tailor your salad 
This recipe for salade Niçoise sticks close to the 
style served in and around Nice, but you can 
change it up to suit your taste. Here are some 
suggestions for doing  just that. 
Vary the vegetables. In place of favas, try 
blanched, cooled green beans. Boiled, cooled, 
and sliced new potatoes are a popular addition in 
bistros in Paris, while lettuce, celery, or cucumbers 
would add refreshing crunch.
Pick your preferred olive. Niçoise olives, small 
olives with an intensely savory flavor, are—perhaps 
obviously—the classic choice. They’re not easy to 
pit, so they’re almost always sold and served unpit-
ted. Feel free to swap them for other brine-cured 
olives, such as Kalamatas, if you prefer.
Simplify the dressing. I like a mustardy vinaigrette 
paired with the salad, but it’s also common to 
simply drizzle it with some extra-virgin olive oil and 
maybe a squeeze of fresh lemon juice.
Omit the whole anchovies. I love anchovies, 
and they’re even more traditional than tuna. But 
they’re polarizing, and lots of people are squea-
mish when faced with a fillet. You can skip serving 
them whole, but keep the deep savory flavor they 
provide by mashing a fillet or two to a paste and 
whisking it into the vinaigrette. 
Use fresh tuna instead of canned. Though  
many would argue that the bold flavor of canned 
tuna is integral to a Niçoise salad, there’s no law 
saying you can’t use slices of grilled or seared fresh 
tuna instead.

the Italian Way
A sweet-and-sour brine known as “agrodolce” gives 
vegetables—and fruit—an appealing kick.
B y Dom enic a M a rc h e t ti
Pickles may not be the first thing that comes to mind when you 
think of Italian food. But browse the aisles of any supermercato or alimentari 
(delicatessen) in Italy, and you’ll find shelves stacked with jars of pickled produce: 
artichokes, carrots, eggplant, mushrooms, peppers, tomatoes, zucchini, and more. 
I honestly can’t imagine the Italian table without an array of these colorful pickles 
set out as part of an antipasto platter or as accompaniments to a main course, such 
as pollo arrosto (roast chicken) or bollito misto (Italian boiled dinner).
My favorite Italian-style pickles are those 
made in the style of agrodolce. Agro means 
sour and dolce means sweet, so any Italian 
dish prepared in this style will exhibit both 
of these flavors. In the case of these pickles, 
the sweet-and-sour element comes from a 
brine combining vinegar and sugar, and often 
infused with herbs and spices. 
Peppers in agrodolce were a staple in our 
house when I was growing up, and I still rely 
on them to add zing to dishes. The first step in 
making them is to grill or roast the peppers to 
soften and sweeten them. Next, they’re mari-
nated in agrodolce brine for an hour, then 
submerged in extra-virgin olive oil, which 
helps to enhance their silky texture, and  
cured in the refrigerator for about a week to 
give the flavors time to meld and mellow.
Other vegetables require less manipulation. 
Carrots, fennel, beets, and others that are high 
in natural sugar take well to a sweet-and-sour 
bath; the vinegar sharpens their flavors and 
the sugar boosts their natural sweetness. Even 
fruit—especially ripe, refreshing melon—is 
delicious when left to steep in a chile-spiked 
agrodolce brine. I cure these other vegetables 
and fruit right in the brine, rather than in oil, 
to help retain their crisp texture. 
On the following pages, you’ll find four of 
my favorite agrodolce pickles, plus suggestions 
for ways to showcase them in your cooking. 
Feel free to play around a bit with the quantity 
of sugar in your brine, but don’t be too stingy; 
it’s the balance of sweet to sour that gives these 
pickles that extra layer of flavor and distinctive 
Italian appeal.

These rustic tarts are simple to 
make yet super impressive for  
dessert or dinner.   By Mindy Fox
You know the phrase “easy as pie”? 
In my house, it’s more like “easy as galette.” That’s because 
these free-form tarts, with their flaky, buttery crusts and 
fillings that feature the best of the season, are both easy 
to eat and easy to make. I turn to galettes when I want to 
make something impressive but fuss-free, which, to be 
honest, is most of the time. 
I like to think of a galette as the carefree French cousin of 
American pie. The main difference lies in their construc-
tion. For a galette, the filling gets placed on a round of 
rolled-out dough. The edges of the dough are folded up 
and pleated over the edge of the filling to hold it in, and 
then it’s baked on a baking sheet. There’s no fitting the 
dough into a pie plate, no need for fancy crimping, and no 
blind baking. Any small tears in the galette dough can be 
pinched together, though if you miss one or two, it’s OK: A 
little oozing filling is part of a galette’s rustic charm.
Though most people who know galettes think of them 
as dessert, they can also feature savory fillings perfect for 
lunch or a light supper. For both sweet and savory fillings, 
I’m inspired by what looks best at the farmers’ market. 
This time of year, berries and stone fruits like peaches 
and plums make luscious fillings for sweet galettes. Fill-
ing options for savory galettes are just about endless. In 
summer, corn and tomatoes are natural choices and are 
at their peak, too. Cheese is almost always a welcome 
addition, and some chopped bacon or other cured meat 
is also tasty.
I use the same easy-to-work-with dough for both sweet 
and savory galettes, but I like to customize it a bit  
depending on the filling. For example, I replace some of 
the flour with cornmeal in the dough for my corn and 
Swiss chard galette (p. 83) both to reinforce the corn 
theme and to add an appealing crunch to the crust. A bit 
of black pepper in the dough for a tomato galette (p. 84) 
counters the sweetness of the tomatoes, while poppyseeds 
embedded in the crust for a plum galette (p. 82) add 
flavor and texture. 
Despite its humble nature, a galette always elicits oohs 
and aahs when you offer one up for guests—and no one 
needs to know that making it was actually easier than pie.
Food writer Mindy Fox co-wrote the The Craft of Baking 
with pastry chef Karen DeMasco and is also the author of The 
Perfectly Roasted Chicken and Salads: Beyond the Bowl. Her 
newest book, Short Stack Editions: Ginger, debuts in December.

If, like me, you thought spring onion was just another way of saying scallion, you’re 
wrong, sort of. Spring onions are bulb (storage) onions harvested early when 
they have a small, tender bulb. T ey aren’t dried for storage and are milder than 
full-size dried storage onions. You’ll usually ﬁ nd them at farmers’ markets and 
occasionally at the supermarket. If picked very early, their bulbs are barely formed; 
such spring onions may be sold as green onions or (incorrectly) as scallions. While 
these very immature onions can be used interchangeably with scallions, a true 
scallion is actually a separate cultivar of the bulb onion, one selected to be tender, 
mild, and not produce a bulb. T e white bottom part of a scallion stays straight 
and does not bulge outward. Both the whites and greens of scallions are used for 
cooking, and the greens are of en used raw as an herb. T e greens of spring onions 
can be tough and may be best reserved for the stockpot.                                       —J.S.

grainy vs. whole-grain mustard 
our recipes often call for whole-grain or grainy mustard. 
They may sound like the same thing, but they’re not. 
All prepared mustards start with mustard seeds. for 
smooth mustard, the seeds are finely ground and mixed 
with water, vinegar, wine, or beer as well as seasonings. For 
whole-grain mustard, the seeds are left whole, which gives 
the mustard a pleasing, nubby texture. Falling somewhere in 
between is what we call grainy mustard, in which the seeds 
are partially ground. Depending on the brand, this style may 
be called stone ground, coarse ground, or country style. 
Choosing is mostly a matter of textural preference.  —J.S.

What is ventresca tuna?
For her Niçoise salad on p. 67, author Jamie Schler 
suggests ventresca tuna, which comes from the 
tuna’s belly (when fresh, it’s what’s known as toro at 
Japanese restaurants). Packed in oil, it has a smooth, 
almost velvety texture and a deep, rich flavor. You can 
find it canned or jarred at many supermarkets and 
specialty food stores or online (see Sources, p. 96). 
Ortiz, which catches all of its tuna by line, offers 
two kinds of ventresca tuna: bonito (known in America 
as albacore), which is more mellow; and yellowfin, 
which is more intensely fishy. Because ventresca’s 
special flavor and luxurious texture come at a price 
(upward of $15 for a 3½-oz. can for premium brands 
like Ortiz), use it when the tuna is the star, such as in a 
salad (like that Niçoise) or as part of a tapas or anti-
pasto spread.                                                                             —J.S.

The tough truth about chewy chicken
If you’ve ever cooked a chicken breast and had it turn out tough and chewy, it might not have been your fault. Some chickens 
are aﬀ ected by a condition called “woody breast,” which is the result of hardened muscle ﬁ bers. Th e exact cause is unknown, but 
it’s linked to chickens that have been bred to get as big as possible as quickly as possible. Most producers check for it by feeling 
for knots in the breasts, and either grind or discard those that have them, but it’s not always possible to detect before cooking. 
Woody breast is believed to aﬀ ect 5% to 10% of the chicken breasts on the market, and though not a health threat to humans, it’s 
certainly not pleasant to eat. To lessen the chance of getting a woody breast, buy your chicken from smaller farms.  —Layla Schlack

What are pimientos?
Years ago, the only thing I knew about pimiento 
peppers (also commonly spelled pimento) was 
that they were stuffed in green olives. Then I 
learned pimientos, which are red, sweet, and 
mild, can be used for so much more. They lend 
their color and flavor to their namesake south-

thicker flesh. Most supermarkets carry pimien-

are sold.                                                                           —J.S.

A good machine

Spiralizing vegetables—that is, cutting them so they look like 
long noodles—has become a thing lately. T e faux noodles are 
popular for those on gluten-free diets and are just a fun way to

with either three or four blades, which create noodles of varying 
thickness as well as shreds or ribbons. T ey’re available at kitchen 
stores, or see Sources, p. 96.                                                   —D.A.

Stop oiling 
hot grill grates
“Oil the food, not the grate” is a bar-
becue motto that I live by. If you put 
oil on the grate of a grill hotter than 
the oil’s smoke point (above 400°F), 
as so many recipes instruct you to do, 
the oil burns instantly. T e result-
ing carbon layer can make sticking 
worse and doesn’t taste good, either. 
Because food that’s oiled is cooler 
than the grill grate, it cools down 
the grate, and the oil doesn’t burn 
on contact. Also, oiling the food isn’t 
wasteful because you use just what’s 
needed to cover the food. 
—Elizabeth Karmel
