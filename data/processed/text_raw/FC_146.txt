NOTE FROM THE EDITOR
8  FI N E CO O K I N G • AP R / M AY 2 0 17
Montana is special. Not just its majestic 
mountains, crystalline rivers, and expansive 
views, but also the spirit and camaraderie 
you feel from the people who farm, ﬁ sh, and 
cook there. T ey all seem to share a strong 
sense of place and a resolve to overcome any 
challenge that the environment may toss 
their way. T e popular song refrain “If I can 
make it there, I’ll make it anywhere” could 
easily apply to living and working in this 
beautiful, rough-hewn landscape.
We ﬁ lmed two episodes of Moveable Feast 
with Fine Cooking in Big Sky Country this 
season, and I’m delighted to share these 
extraordinary dining adventures with you 
(p. 72). From a chuck wagon dinner, com-
plete with real cowboys, to a stunning out-
door feast for 50 in the foothills of the Rocky 
Mountains, the food served at these meals—
grilled whole trout, tender elk and beef, fresh 
huckleberry croustades—is straightforward, 
deeply satisfying, and as unpretentious as the 
chefs, ranchers, and farmers behind it. 
T at down-to-earth theme resonates 
throughout the rest of this issue as well. Chef 
Jenn Louis reminds us that it takes only a 
few basic ingredients to create incredible 
homemade gnocchi (p. 58), and additions 
such as earthy beets and fresh spinach 
ramp up the ﬂ avor and color. Making these 
humble dumplings is easier than you might 
think, too.
We also celebrate shrimp and grits (p. 68), 
a beloved Southern dish with modest begin-
nings. Choose between two exceptional ver-
sions, one on the classic side and the other 
with a Latin twist. Warning: You’ll have a 
hard time deciding between them; you may 
just have to try both!
To add a sweet note, we oﬀ er a feature 
on one of the easiest desserts you may ever 
make: skillet cookies (p. 82). T e dough is 
simple and quick to put together, and you 
can even eat the cookie right from the pan 
(just grab some spoons). Your guests will be 
smitten with the dessert’s homespun, rustic 
appeal, as well as the creative ﬂ avor combi-
nations—chocolate and tahini, anyone?
As always, we hope you enjoy cooking 
from this issue as much as we did testing—
and tasting—each dish. Please tell us 
which recipes you like best. (Even better, 
post a photo on Instagram using #fcfavs or 
@ﬁ necooking on Facebook.)
—Kathy Kingsley, editorial director

Samantha Seneviratne (“Small-Batch 
Cookies from the Skillet,” p. 82) lives in New 
York City. Her first cookbook, The New Sugar 
and Spice, was nominated for a 2016 James Beard Award. She’s also 
the author of Gluten-Free for Good and a contributor to The New York 
Times. Sam is currently working on her third cookbook.
• My ingredient splurge is... blanched Sicilian pistachios. They are 
vibrantly green and gorgeous. I use them sparingly as a garnish.
• In spring, I can’t wait to bake... something with apricots and rhubarb. 
Apricot-rhubarb upside-down cake is heavenly.
• My culinary inspiration is... everywhere! Every day, I learn from the 
talented people I work with, my friends and family, and chefs and 
food writers I follow.
Bruce Aidells (“A Most Perfect Cut of Pork,” 
p. 46) is the go-to guy for meat and meat 
cookery topics. The founder of Aidells Sausage 
Company and author of 12 cookbooks, Bruce lives in the San Francisco 
Bay Area with his wife, chef Nancy Oakes.
• The most underrated cut of meat is… neck meat, including lamb, 
pork, and beef.
• My go-to breakfast is… homemade heirloom pork sausage patties 
made with fresh sage, fennel, and thyme.
• The food destination I most want to travel to is… Turkey. The Turkish 
food I’ve eaten and cooked is very appealing. I’d love to try more and 
learn more. 
 
 
Mindy Fox (“A Fresh Look at Leeks,” p. 52) is 
the author of several books, including Salads: 
Beyond the Bowl, chosen as a New York 
Times best cookbook of the year. Her most recent cookbook is Short 
Stack: Ginger.
• The food trend that needs to end is… orange wine. I’ve tried a few, 
but haven’t yet been able to understand the draw.
• When cooking, I like to listen to… the sounds of the kitchen: from 
mixers and blenders to chopping to ingredients as they gently bubble 
in a pot or cook in a skillet.
• My latest kitchen purchase was… a pair of mini spatulas.
Anthony Lamas (“Shrimp & Grits,” p. 68) 
is chef and owner of Seviche, a Latin restau-
rant in Louisville, Kentucky. He’s also the 
author of Southern Heat: New Southern Cooking, Latin Style.
• The dish I want to learn to make is... lamb vindaloo. I love 
Indian flavors!
• My favorite food town is... Chicago. The diversity and caliber of 
restaurants is incredible.
• If I weren’t a chef, I’d be... a drummer in a band (a guy can 
dream). More realistically, I would explore agricultural science.
Mike Lata (“Shrimp & Grits,” p. 68) is 
chef/partner of FIG Restaurant and The 
Ordinary, an oyster bar and seafood hall 
in Charleston, South Carolina.
• I love Charleston for its... food and beverage community, life-
style, natural resources, and weather.
• My next kitchen purchase will be... a pasta extruder. All  
the cool kids have them these days. 
• My guilty-pleasure snack is... sausage, egg, and cheese on a 
toasted poppyseed bagel. 
Jenn Louis (“Gnocchi, in Living Color,” 
p. 58) is executive chef and owner of 
Lincoln Restaurant in Portland, Oregon, 
where she lives with her two cats. She’s also the author of Pasta By 
Hand and the recently published The Book of Greens. You can find 
her on Instagram and Twitter @jennlouis.
• The kitchen utensil I can’t live without is… my Kramer  
10-inch chef’s knife.
• My favorite spring produce is… the first wild greens of the year. 
I have a wonderful forager who finds a great collection of wild 
greens, which I use in my favorite salad mix: lemon balm, miner’s 
lettuce, wild watercress, and more. I’m also a sucker for wild 
spring nettles.
• My guilty-pleasure snack is… homemade cookies, usually 
involving chocolate.

BOOKS THAT COOK
The Reading List
Vegetarian books that everyone will love 
BY JOAN N E S MART
Vibrant India
Fresh Vegetarian Recipes 
from Bangalore to Brooklyn
By Chitra Agrawal (Ten Speed Press; $24.99) 
In the introduction of her debut book, Vibrant 
India, which focuses on the vegetable-centric 
cuisine of South India, Chitra Agrawal quotes 
chef, world traveler, and lover of meat Anthony 
Bourdain about eating in India: “This is one of 
the few places in the world where I could eat 
vegetarian every day and still be happy.” I know 
I would feel the same. With so many exciting 
herbs and spices in the Indian pantry, plus an 
emphasis on protein-rich lentils, who needs 
meat? Raised in the suburbs of New Jersey, 
Agrawal took many long summer trips to visit her 
mother’s family in Bangalore and grew up eating 
the vegetarian dishes from her parents’ native 
country. After college in California, she moved 
to Brooklyn and started cooking and writing a 
food blog. That led to teaching food classes 
and, eventually, launching Brooklyn Delhi, the 
Indian condiments company she co-owns with 
her husband. Though many of the recipes in 
her book are not difficult to make (try Ben’s 
Curry Leaf Popcorn), she doesn’t shy away from 
more challenging dishes. I’ve only had dosas, 
those ethereal crepes made from a batter of 
fermented rice and lentils, at Indian restaurants, 
but I may try making them at home thanks to her 
detailed directions. 
A sure hit: Apple, Ginger, and Coconut Hand 
Pies A take on a traditional Indian dumpling 
influenced by the author’s love of apple pie. 
In My Kitchen
A Collection of New 
and Favorite Vegetarian Recipes
By Deborah Madison (Ten Speed Press; $32.50) 
Deborah Madison, a longtime and revered pro-
ponent of vegetarian cooking, has contributed 
many recipes to Fine Cooking. A favorite is one 
I fondly call “beans and greens on toast” (but 
online you’ll find it as Broccoli Raab & Cannellini 
Beans over Garlic Bread). The dish exemplifies 
the types of recipes—drop-dead simple but so 
delicious—featured in her newest cookbook. 
Madison acknowledges that more involved dishes 
still have their place (and there are a few of those 
here), but she believes that most (including her 
own) can be simplified with little to no loss of 
flavor. Whether it’s her take on scrambled eggs 
(smothered with crispy breadcrumbs) or a hearty 
Potato and Green Chile Stew, these are her 
favorites, the dishes she turns to again and again. 
Fans may recognize dishes from earlier incarna-
tions, but chances are they’ve been lightened or 
tweaked to boost flavor. A chard and saffron tart, 
for example, is now lined with ground almonds in 
place of a butter crust. The book is organized (I 
use that word loosely) in an unusual way—without 
chapters and with recipes in alphabetical order 
by main(ish) ingredient. Some may find it frustrat-
ing that a broccoli side follows a recipe for bread, 
but I found it encourages browsing and discovery. 
A keeper: Smoky-Spicy Butter In her earlier 
recipes, this might have been one component. 
Here, it’s the star, slathered on roasted veg. 
The First Mess Cookbook
Vibrant Plant-Based Recipes 
to Eat Well Through the Seasons 
By Laura Wright (Avery; $30)
If you can get past the odd title (more on the 
title later) and a grammatically tortured subtitle 
(the less said, the better), The First Mess is a gor-
geous book filled with recipes that delight and 
surprise. I’m not vegan, but the book’s photos, 
shot by the author, an award-winning blogger, 
are so enticing that I’m really eager to try making 
her Seedy Sesame Granola Bars, among many 
others dishes. The recipes emphasize seasonal 
and local produce, and there is nothing  strident 
about her approach to eating. Her veganism 
is presented matter of factly, and her tone is 
friendly and lively. An example: A recipe called 
Go-To Kale Salad with “Master Cleanse” Dressing 
pokes gentle fun at detox diets. Wright admits 
she has never tried the Master Cleanse, a fast 
that involves drinking a mixture of water, maple 
syrup, lemon juice, and cayenne, but she did 
enjoy a martini inspired by it, and loved the com-
bination of sweet, spicy, and tart. And what of

Slice and Dice
An illustrated guide to basic knife skills and common cuts.  b y Li s a L a h ey
Kitchen Basics
The idea of precision knife cuts may be 
thought of as too persnickety for many 
home cooks. It’s not just the beauty of a 
dish that benefits from good knife skills, 
though, but also the flavor and texture. 
Think of it this way: If you're making 
soup and you cut your vegetables big, 
you'll be able to get only one hunk of 
carrot on your spoon. If you cut the veg-
etables into small or medium dice, you'll 
have an array of flavors and textures in 
each bite. Plus, uniformity of cuts also 
allows for uniformity of cooking time 
and doneness. That said, no need to 
bust out the ruler to be exact on sizes; 
approximate is just fine. 
A key tool in your kitchen is a good-
quality chef's knife. It should feel bal-
anced in your hand, with the weight of 
the blade equal to that of the handle. 
Almost more important, it should feel 
right in your hand: the handle, the size 
of the blade, and the weight of the knife. 
Once you have your knife, be sure to 
maintain it with regular sharpening and 
honing. A sharp knife is safer than a dull 
one, having less tendency to slip or slide 
rather than to cut as it should. It will 
also give you better and more beautiful 
results; a dull knife can tear and damage 
foods rather than chop or slice.  
Once you become accustomed to 
cutting vegetables with the techniques 
shown here, you'll work with more 
speed and confidence in the kitchen.

An 8- to 12-inch chef's knife is the most often used knife in the kitchen and the perfect 
tool for most chopping needs.
How to hold a knife
For best control, choke up your hold 
so that your hand is grasping both the 
handle and blade of the knife. (Holding 
a knife by just the handle can make the 
blade unwieldy.) Grasp the handle in 
the palm of your hand so that your index finger wraps over the bolster and rests on the side 
of the blade. Your thumb will rest on the opposite side so that pressure between your thumb 
and forefinger will steady the blade. The power or force of the cut comes from the palm of 
your hand, and your thumb and forefinger stabilize and guide the blade as you cut. 
What to do with your other hand
When slicing or dicing, your other hand secures the ingredient 
as you cut and helps guide the knife to the thickness of each 
cut. Always curl the fingertips of this hand under so that they 
are tucked away from the sharp edge of the knife. The blade of 
a larger knife, such as a large chef’s knife, will be guided by the 
flat surface of your fingers so that each slice can be made with 
steady precision.

skillet shepherd’s pie
This kind of dish is perfect for a still-cold spring night. 
It’s hearty and filling, but the peas and mint hint at the 
warm weather to come.  Serves 4
	
	 Kosher salt
	
2	 large russet potatoes (about 1½ lb. total),  
peeled and cut into 1-inch pieces
	
1/2	 cup mascarpone
	
2	 Tbs. unsalted butter
	
	 Freshly ground black pepper
	
1 	 Tbs. olive oil
	
½ 	 cup finely chopped sweet onion
	
1 	 Tbs. finely chopped garlic
	
1	 lb. ground lamb
	
1 	 Tbs. finely chopped fresh rosemary
	
2 	 Tbs. all-purpose flour
	
1 	 cup lower-salt beef broth
	 1½ 	 cups thawed frozen peas
	
1	 Tbs. finely chopped fresh mint
	
3 	 oz. grated sharp Cheddar (about 1 cup)
	
	 Hot sauce, such as Cholula, for serving (optional)
Position a rack in the center of the oven and heat to 400°F.
Bring a 4-quart saucepan of well-salted water to a boil. Add 
the potatoes and cook until tender, 12 to 15 minutes. Reserve a 
little of the cooking water, drain the potatoes, and return to the 
pot. Add the mascarpone, butter, ½ tsp. salt, and ½ tsp. pepper, 
and mash until smooth. If necessary, add a little of the reserved 
water to make the potatoes spreadable but not too loose.
Meanwhile, in a 10-inch ovenproof skillet, preferably cast 
iron, heat the oil over medium-high heat. Add the onion and 
cook, stirring occasionally, until tender and browned, about 
2 minutes. Add the garlic and cook, stirring, until fragrant, about 
30 seconds. Add the lamb, rosemary, 1/2 tsp. salt, and 1/2 tsp. 
pepper, and cook, stirring occasionally and breaking up the 
meat, until browned, 6 to 8 minutes. Spoon off most of the fat. 
Sprinkle the flour over the lamb, and stir until combined. Add 
the broth and cook, stirring and scraping up the bits on the bot-
tom of the pan, until thickened, 2 to 3 minutes. Remove from 
the heat.  
In a small bowl, combine the peas and mint. Layer the peas on 
top of the lamb. Dollop all over with the mashed potatoes, and use 
damp, flattened hands to spread them to the edge of the skillet.
Bake for 5 minutes, then top with the cheese. Turn the broiler 
on medium and broil until the cheese melts and is golden 
brown, 4 to 6 minutes.  Allow to cool slightly before serving with 
some hot sauce, if you like.                                                    —Ronne Day

Whipped Cream
What’s happening as you whisk those soft, lofty, delicious peaks?
BY DAVID JOACH I M A N D A N DR EW S C H LO S S
FOOD SCIENCE
End just right
If you plan to pipe or spread whipped cream or fold 
it into another mixture, whip the cream to medium, 
not stiﬀ , peaks (see above right). T e actions of piping, 
spreading, and folding continue to apply mechanical 
force to whipped cream, which will drive fat globules 
closer together and create a grainy texture. T e same 
thing happens when you overwhip cream, which is 
why some prefer to whip cream by hand—or at least 
by hand toward the end of whipping—to gain more 
control over the ﬁ nal texture.
Liquid to foam
Whipped cream is a foam created by forc-
ing air into liquid cream, which increases 
the cream’s total volume as the air bubbles 
and liquid are trapped and suspended in a 
network of fat. 
Start slow
When you begin to whisk cream whether by hand 
or electric mixer, air appears ﬁ rst as large frothy 
bubbles. T ese bubbles pop easily because nothing 
is holding them in place, making the foam deﬂ ate 
quickly. To build stabler foam, it’s best to start 
slowly, whisking to create an even distribution of 
smaller bubbles that can be more easily held in 
place as the fat in the cream stabilizes them.
Keep it up
As more air is beaten into the cream, the force of whisking 
gradually shears away part of the protective membrane coating 
the surface of the fat globules in the cream. T ese now-exposed 
fat globules align themselves around the air bubbles. Because fat 
and water will not combine, the fat globules in the cream natu-
rally turn away from the water to face either the interior of an 
air bubble or each other, where they will connect and surround 
the air bubbles, making them more stable. Ultimately, they cre-
ate a network of fat that holds both the water and air in suspen-
sion. At this point, the liquid cream has transformed into the 
semisolid foam we call whipped cream.

Peak stages
Lightly whipped, or sof  peak, cream has a ﬂ uﬀ y consistency that 
won’t hold a peak and barely clings to the whisk when lif ed. Cream 
whipped to medium peaks takes on more air and holds a sof  peak at 
the top, but spreads gently at the bottom of a dollop. Whipped cream 
at stiﬀ  peak has roughly doubled in volume, holds ﬁ rm, and has a 
sharp edge when piped or spread.
Which cream? 
Cream or milk with less than 30% 
fat can be whipped into a foam, 
but it won’t last long, because 
cold fat strengthens the foam. 
For whipped cream that holds for 
several hours, choose cream with 
at least 30% fat (see chart above).
Unhomogenized cream whips 
faster than homogenized cream 
because the fat globules are larger 
and align themselves faster. How-
ever, this foam will be less stable 
because the larger fat globules 
form a looser network that is less 
able to hold water in suspension; 
it then weeps from the cream and 
deﬂ ates the foam. 
Homogenized cream creates 
ﬁ ner-textured, more stable foam 
because the fat globules are smaller 
and more widely and evenly dis-
tributed throughout the liquid.
Raw, unpasteurized cream 
whips faster and holds stiﬀ er 
peaks than pasteurized cream. T e 
heat of pasteurization denatures 
(unravels) protein in the cream and 
destroys an enzyme that encour-
ages fat globules to clump together, 
both of which weaken the stability 
of whipped cream. For the same 
reasons, pasteurized cream whips 
faster and holds better peaks than 
ultrapasteurized cream.
Whisk away
You could whip cream with a 
fork, but a whisk is the best tool 
for the job. T e many wires of a whisk 
multiply the aerating eﬀ ect of whipping, 
accomplishing the task more eﬃ  ciently. A chilled metal whisk 
helps keep the cream cold so that it whips quickly (see right).
Keep it cool
All fat solidiﬁ es as it cools and melts 
when it’s heated. T e fat in cream 
(milk fat or butterfat) is solid at 
temperatures below 40°F, ﬂ exibly 
ﬁ rm at temperatures between 40°F 
and 60°F, spreadable between 60°F 
and 85°F, and liquid at temperatures 
above that. For the fat in cream to be 
ﬁ rm enough to stabilize the network of 
air bubbles in whipped cream, the cream 
ideally should be between 40°F and 50°F. 
Refrigerating cream for at least 12 hours 
makes its milk fat uniformly chilled, resulting in 
whipped cream that is smoother and more stable. 
Cream that has been out at room temperature does not 
gain as much volume as chilled cream and more easily 
turns granular and watery as the whipped cream sits.
Beaters and bowls should be colder than 40°F, since 
air and the friction created by whipping will quickly 
warm them up.

RECIPE
Sweeten the deal
Adding sugar is the most common way to 
sweeten whipped cream. Besides adding 
sweetness, sugar attracts and holds on to 
some of the water in the foam, making the 
whipped cream less likely to weep. 
T e ﬁ ner the granulation of the sugar, the 
more easily it dissolves in the liquid cream. 
If you are using more than a few table-
spoons of sugar to sweeten your whipped 
cream, as you might for a cake topping, use 
superﬁ ne, ultraﬁ ne, or confectioners’ sugar 
to avoid any possibility of a gritty texture 
from undissolved sugar granules. 
Liquid sweeteners, like honey or agave, 
thin the cream, decreasing its ratio of fat, 
thereby making the cream more diﬃ  cult 
to whip. T ese sweeteners should be added 
near the end of beating, af er the cream is 
thick enough to hold a shape. 
What’s in the can?
As with all food, fresh is 
best, and store-bought 
canned whipped cream 
is not as fresh as cream 
whipped on the spot. 
Bubbles form a little 
differently in whipped 
cream dispensed from 
a can and the whipped 
cream dispensers many 
chefs favor. In these 
containers, nitrous oxide 
(N20) gas is dissolved in 
the cream and dispersed 
throughout the fat under 
pressure. When the cream 
is dispensed, the pressure 
is released, and the gas 
moves rapidly out of solu-
tion, quickly expanding 
and forming bubbles 
in the cream. 
While cream whip-
ped by hand aerates to 
roughly twice its origi-
nal volume, aerosolized 
creams expand to about 
four times their original 
volume due to rapidly 
expanding N20 gas. As a 
result, dispensed whipped 
cream is lighter in weight 
than an equal volume of 
hand-whipped cream.

Can broken whipped 
cream be saved?
IF YOU OVERBEAT CREAM and

A Most 
Perfect 
Cut of 
Pork
Tender, juicy, and quick to prepare, 
pork tenderloin answers the weeknight 
question: What’s for dinner?
by B ruce Aidel l s
I consider pork tenderloin the chicken breast of 
the pork world: It doesn’t get a lot of attention at fancy 
restaurants—chefs prefer more impressive-looking chops—but 
home cooks love it. And there is lots to love. Pork tenderloin 
takes almost no time to prep and cooks quickly, making it an 
especially good choice for weeknight dinners. While it may 
seem pricey compared to other cuts, there’s little fat and no 
waste; you don’t even need to trim away the silverskin (that 
thin membrane on top) as its not detectable once cooked. Yes, 
pork tenderloin has a mild flavor, but as with chicken, that just 
gives license to jazz it up with all kinds of flavorings. Naturally 
tender, it takes well to dry-heat cooking methods, such as sear-
ing, grilling, sautéing, and roasting.
The recipes that follow show the versatility of this piece 
of porcine pleasure: roasted with a fennel-and-garlic rub; 
grilled with Tex-Mex flavors; flattened, stuffed, and rolled for 
a dinner-party-worthy entrée; and brined to keep it extra juicy 
while its exterior develops a lovely char. No matter which way 
you cook pork tenderloin, the key is to not overcook it. Gone 
are the days when, for safety reasons, cooked pork had to show 
no traces of pink (see right). Once cooked, give it a short rest 
before slicing. You’ll be rewarded with slices that are pink and 
juicy—in other words, perfect. 
Meat expert Bruce Aidells has written many award-winning  
cookbooks, including The Complete Meat Cookbook.
Think pink
Years ago, pork was cooked until 
there was no sign of pink, and 
the results, as may be expected, 
were dry. These days, thanks 
to improved pork production, 
there’s no need to cook it to 
that extreme. The USDA still 
suggests it be cooked to 145°F 
(down from 160°F), but you’ll 
get juicier results cooking it to 
between 130°F and 140°F.

of en the unsung hero that makes a dish special. Case in point: 
My Uncle T om and his wife, Bonnie, credit me with turning 
them on to leeks via a soup recipe from my chicken cookbook; 
now, like me, they’re fans of this unassuming allium. 
Though often associated with spring, as that’s when 
small, young ones ﬁ rst start to show up at the market, leeks are 
farmed commercially almost year-round in areas of the United 
States like the temperate Paciﬁ c Northwest. When buying leeks, 
look for fairly ﬁ rm whites and sturdy greens, free from blem-
ishes and withering. Leeks stay freshest when a portion of their 
roots remains attached, so if you can, buy them that way.
Almost all leek recipes begin with trimming away the 
dark green leaves of the plant, which are too ﬁ brous to 
cook up tenderly. (T ey can be added to a stock for ﬂ avor, 
however.) Any tough outer layers of the white and light-
green parts should also be removed. For bigger leeks, this 
can mean that a few layers have to go. Leeks that have over-
wintered or have been exposed to a lot of cold in the ﬁ eld 
can have a whitish, ﬁ brous seed stalk running through the 
center, which you’ll want to discard, too. 
Cleaning leeks is fairly easy. Sliced or chopped leeks may 
be easily rinsed in a strainer under running water. For leeks 
to be cooked halved but intact, rinse the halves under run-
ning water, fanning out the layers as you go. To be sure all of 
the grit is gone, submerge them in a bowl of lukewarm water, 
and swish them vigorously. Leave the leeks ﬂ oating in the 
water for a few minutes so that any dirt settles to the bottom, 
and then lif  them out carefully.
T e recipes on the following pages showcase leeks either 
grilled on their own for a stunning side dish or sliced and 
featured in abundance in a soup, pasta, and bread pudding. 
But you don’t need a recipe to cook with leeks; just use them 
as you might an onion in a ﬁ lling for an omelet or quiche, to 
top pizza, or to boost the ﬂ avor of a stew or braise. I also like 
to slowly sauté sliced leeks until they’re sweet and silky (see 
sidebar at right), or give them an easy fry to make frizzled 
leeks (see sidebar at right), which are the perfect garnish, add-
ing both ﬂ avor and crunch. 
T e next time you’re at the market, give leeks a chance.

B Y J E N N LO U I S
While traveling through Italy and researching 
handmade dumpling-style pasta for my book Pasta 
By Hand, I came to understand that the world of 
gnocchi (NYOH-kee) is much larger than I’d previ-
ously thought. I was familiar with potato gnocchi, 
the kind oft en described as “light as air,” but soon 
came to learn that gnocchi are also made from ricotta 
(sometimes known as gnudi) and with semolina in 
a manner similar to polenta (gnocchi alla Romana). 
Th ey can be fashioned from stale bread or even 
pâte à choux, which is how the French make their 
gnocchi Parisienne.  
What really surprised (and delighted) me 
was how much the flavors—and even the 
colors—can vary. To demonstrate some of the 
myriad ways to fl avor gnocchi, I’m focusing here 
on potato-based gnocchi to keep the basic process 
more or less the same. Hailing from northern Italy, 
potato gnocchi are made by mixing cooked potatoes 
with fl our and sometimes egg to form a thick dough. 
Th e dough gets rolled into ropes, then cut into indi-
vidual pieces. To serve, it’s boiled like pasta. Th e varia-
tions come from what I add to the dough, whether 
a diﬀ erent kind of fl our that changes the look and 
texture of the gnocchi, a vegetable purée that adds 
deep color and earthy fl avor, or a soft  cheese that 
makes it feel light. 
For plain potato gnocchi, a super-light consistency 
is the biggest selling point, and when well made, each 
bite feels ethereal. My Potato and Ricotta Gnocchi 
on p. 67 are that airy and supple, but one thing I also 
learned while sampling various gnocchi in Italy is 
that some, such as the Chestnut Gnocchi on p. 66, are 
actually supposed to be a bit more dense.
Though the process can look intimidating, 
these little dumplings are fun to make, once 
you get the hang of it. If you’ve ever enjoyed shap-
ing Play-Doh, making gnocchi is right up your alley. 
And the payoﬀ  for making your own—that lovely 
mouth-feel and the ability to tailor the fl avor and 
color as done here—is huge. If the idea of giving each 
dumpling ridges gives you pause, know it gets easier 
with practice. Besides, it’s perfectly fi ne to leave your 
gnocchi ridge-free. Served with a chunkier sauce, the 
ridges can help catch fl avorful bits, but they’re not as 
necessary when served with a butter or cream sauce. 
Speaking of sauce, don’t simply top the 
gnocchi with it. Instead, toss gently with the warm 
sauce. Th at way, each dumpling gets a chance to 
absorb some of the sauce’s fl avor, and each gets well 
coated. Like pasta, gnocchi may be served with a 
wide variety of sauce styles, from a meat ragù to 
marinara to simple melted butter. Use your own 
favorite sauce or one of my sauces on p. 67. Th e only 
trouble you’ll have is deciding which fl avor of gnoc-
chi to make.

Pick the right potato. 
Starchy russets give gnocchi a 
light texture. Boiling potatoes 
whole and unpeeled helps to 
keep them from absorbing 
excess water. Remove the peel 
as soon as you can so that 
steam is released rather than 
absorbed into the flesh. 
Use a ricer. Passing boiled 
potatoes through a ricer, a 
tool that looks like an oversize 
garlic press, instead of mash-
ing ensures a fine texture—no 
one wants lumpy gnocchi—
and aerates them as well. 
Make the dough with 
still-warm potatoes.  
This will encourage the egg to 
bind the dough.
Be stingy with the flour. 
The exact amount will vary 
depending on the flour,  
potatoes, and humidity  
in your kitchen, but add  
additional flour sparingly. 
You want enough to hold the 
dough together, but not so 
much to cause the gnocchi  
to become heavy.
Handle gently. Overwork-
ing develops gluten, which 
can make gnocchi tough. Mix 
and shape with a light touch. 
Test for texture. Before 
you shape all of your gnocchi, 
make and cook just a couple. 
If the gnocchi fall apart, add 
more flour to the dough. 
Freeze ‘em. Frozen gnocchi 
are easier to handle than fresh 
and hold their shape better 
during cooking. (To loosen 
frozen gnocchi from a baking 
sheet, give the pan a shake.)
Don’t overcook. If your 
gnocchi cook for too long, 
they can absorb too much 
water and become dense  
and chewy. 
Forgo the colander. Deli-
cate gnocchi can get squashed 
if drained. Remove them 
from the pot with a skimmer 
or slotted spoon and toss

Succulent shrimp served over creamy grits may be one of the most 
popular dishes on restaurant menus in the South and, increasingly, through-
out the United States. But its beginnings were humble: In the Lowcountry of 
South Carolina, the dish was eaten mostly at home, primarily for breakfast 
and mainly during shrimp season. In the 1980s, chef Bill Neal began serving his 
version—made with mushrooms, bacon, and red-eye gravy, another South-
ern staple—for dinner at his Crook’s Corner restaurant in Chapel Hill, North 
Carolina. Neal’s dish gained wider attention after Craig Claiborne, then a New 
York Times food editor, wrote about it in 1985. Neal died just six years later, 
but his style of regional American cooking influenced a whole generation of 
chefs. On the following pages, two Southern transplants who have embraced 
the region’s foodways reflect on how they came to know this classic dish and 
share their unique takes on it.

southern-style shrimp and grits
What are grits, anyway?
“The unimaginative definition of grits emanates from the USDA, where grits is just 
another iteration of cornmeal,” says Glenn Roberts, founder of Anson Mills in Colum-
bia, South Carolina. He adds that the more interesting definition hails back to just 
after the Great Depression: when sifted, stone-milled grits was “what stayed on top 
of a door screen and coarse cornmeal is what fell through.” This means grits particles 
can measure 1/6 to 1/16 inch while cornmeal begins at 1⁄24 inch. Grits is not, however, 
hominy, which is made from dried corn kernels cooked in a lime solution and used 
whole in the Mexican dish posole and ground to make masa. The products can look 
similar, which is why a prepared dish of grits used to be called (and may still be called) 
“hominy” by folks in the South. For sources for these chefs’ favorite grits, see p. 96.

When he thought about putting

Lamas’s spicy rendition would be 
quite the wake-up call.

One Saturday evening, I found myself 
tasked with bringing a dessert to a 
dinner party and had practically no time to 
prepare one. Since I’m a baker, I’m always happy to 
make the ﬁ nal bite for my friends, but this particular 
Saturday, I hadn’t made a plan; I hadn’t even gone to 
the supermarket. Instead of stopping on the way to the 
party for a store-bought solution, I tried to imagine 
something simple but still spectacular that I could 
whip up quickly. 
As I rummaged through my cupboards, it came to 
me: a skillet cookie! It has all the beneﬁ ts of a freshly 
baked cookie without the hassle of portioning dough, 
baking in batches, rotating pans, or cooling on racks. 
Enhanced with ingredients like nuts, coconut, cin-
namon, cayenne, lime, or even a swirl of tahini, the 
cookie could become sublime, even more so when 
topped with ice cream. 
To bake this treat, all you need is an 8-inch oven-
proof skillet. Both stainless and cast-iron pans work 
well, though baking times may vary as cookies made in 
cast-iron pans bake more quickly (see p. 91 for why). T e 
cookies can be sliced for serving or scooped, which is the 
way to go if you’re serving them very warm.
For that dinner party, I assembled a cookie dough in 
10 minutes, spread it in my skillet, wrapped it up, and 
brought it with me, unbaked. Af er dinner, I popped 
the skillet in the oven, and as my friends and I sipped 
some more wine, the smell of caramelized sugar and 
melting chocolate ﬁ lled the air. I brought out the hot 
cookie with its crisp, browned edges and delightfully 
gooey core. T en I heaped on some ice cream and fresh 
whipped cream. It looked so good we didn’t even bother 
with plates—just four friends with four spoons around 
one skillet, enjoying the coziest dessert we could have 
imagined. Mission accomplished.

How to rake

If you’ve never cooked spaghetti squash,

for culinary as well as medicinal uses, maitake 
are called by many other names, too, including 
ram’s head or sheep’s head mushrooms, king of 
mushrooms, cloud mushrooms, and signorina (or 
unmarried woman). 
Maitake are native to the United States, Europe, 
and Japan, and in the wild, they favor the base of 
oak and maple trees, as well as coniferous trees and 
stumps, where they can be found in late summer 
through early fall. Today, however, thanks to the 
Japanese who pioneered the eﬀ ort, the mushroom 
is widely cultivated and can be found both fresh 
and dried in Asian markets and increasingly in 
supermarkets, where they are sold packaged most 
of en with their Japanese name, maitake. 
With their beery aroma and meaty texture, 
maitake are delicious eaten raw, seared, roasted, 
deep-fried, grilled, or sautéed. T e recipe at right 
incorporates roasted maitake into a hearty vegetar-
ian noodle bowl.                                           —R.D.

flavors, warm udon noodles, and vibrant, crisp 
vegetables.  Serves 4

Position a rack in the center of the oven and heat 
to 450°F.
Put the fresh maitake on a large rimmed baking 
sheet, toss with the olive oil, and season with ½   tsp. 
salt and ½   tsp. pepper. Roast until golden and crisp on 
the edges, 12 to 15 minutes. Reserve a handful of the 
larger pieces and transfer the rest to a 4-quart pot. 
Add the dried mushrooms, garlic, ginger, 
coriander, peppercorns, star anise, and 8 cups of 
water to the pot. Bring to a boil. Lower the heat, 
and simmer until reduced by half, about 1 hour. 
Strain the broth through a fine-mesh strainer

bowls. Season the broth to taste, and ladle over 
the noodles.

pieces and add to the bowls along with the rad-
ishes, scallions, carrots, and cilantro. Drizzle each 
with a little sesame oil, and serve.       —Ronne Day

Not all black peppercorns 
taste the same 
I recently found myself grinding more and more black pep-
per onto my scrambled eggs. For some reason, I just wasn’t 
getting the pepper ﬂ avor I expected. No, I didn’t have a 
cold. T e culprit, I found, was that I had inadvertently 
bought “regular” (Malabar) black peppercorns instead 
of my usual Tellicherry. T ough both grow on the same 
vines of the same plant (Piper nigrum) in the same region 
of India, once the berrylike fruit gets dried and sorted, the 
larger (at least 4.25 millimeters) are deemed Tellicherry and 
the even larger still (at least 4.75 millimeters) are deemed 
Tellicherry Special Extra Bold. Tellicherry peppercorns 
have a fruitier ﬂ avor and are much more aromatic, but 
because they make up much less of any given harvest, they 
cost a little more. Af er using regular black pepper on my 
eggs and not getting that same complex ﬂ avor, the slightly 
higher price is something I will gladly pay. See p. 96 for 
sources.                                                            —Joanne Smart

Fresh Raspberry-Lemon Cheesecake Bars
Prep Time: 15 min. 
Total Time: 6 hours 5 min. (incl. refrigerating) 
Makes: 18 servings
What You Need
2 cups graham cracker crumbs
1 
cup plus 2 Tbsp. sugar, divided
6  Tbsp. butter, melted
3 cups (12 oz.) raspberries, divided
1 
Tbsp. each zest and juice from 1 lemon
4 pkg. (8 oz. each) PHILADELPHIA Cream Cheese, softened
4 eggs
Make It
HEAT oven to 325ºF. 
LINE 13x9-inch pan with foil, with ends of foil extending over 
sides. Combine graham crumbs, 2 Tbsp. sugar and butter; 
press onto bottom of prepared pan. Bake 10 min. 
RESERVE 1/2 cup raspberries and 1 tsp. lemon zest for later use. 
BEAT cream cheese, lemon juice, remaining zest and remaining 
sugar in large bowl with mixer until blended. Add eggs, 1 at 
a time, mixing on low speed after each just until blended. 
Gently stir in remaining raspberries; pour over crust. 
BAKE 35 to 40 min. or until center is almost set. Cool completely. 
REFRIGERATE 4 hours. Top with reserved raspberries and 
lemon zest. Use foil handles to remove cheesecake from 
pan before cutting into bars.
