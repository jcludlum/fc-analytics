Cook Like a Pro
Elevate your cooking with convection.
Flaky, golden, evenly baked blackberry pies. Crisp-skinned, succulent roast 
capons. Beets that caramelize faster and develop deeper, richer ﬂ avors. For 
professional results at home, chefs in the oﬀ -hours turn to convection cooking.
YOU CAN COOK just about 
anything in a convection oven, and 
while learning to use one certainly 
isnʼt a big deal, the results you 
get—evenly baked cookies and juicy, 
well-browned meats—are.
Unlike conventional radiant (also 
called thermal) ovens, convection 
ovens have a fan that continuously 
circulates air through the oven 
cavity. When hot air is blowing 
onto food, as opposed to merely 
surrounding it, the food tends to 
cook more quickly. 
This acceleration eﬀ ect is one 
reason for the superior results you 
get from convection. The rush of 
heat speeds up the chemical 
reactions that occur when food 
cooks. For example, the butter in a 
piecrust releases its steam quickly, 
creating ﬂ aky layers, or the skin of a 
roasting chicken renders its fat and 
browns more quickly, so the meat 
cooks faster and stays juicier. Overall, 
food cooked in a convection oven is 
often done about 25 percent faster 
than it is in a conventional oven.        
— Susie Middleton, cookbook author, 
The Fresh and Green Table
What is Convection 
Cooking?
1.  Evenly Baked 
Cookies
“Multiple racks of cookies bake 
evenly in a convection oven—no 
burnt bottoms, no pale spots, and 
you donʼt need to turn the sheets 
halfway through baking. Always 
set the oven 25°F lower than the 
recipe calls for, and since they 
bake faster in a convection oven, 
start checking the doneness much 
sooner than the recipe indicates.”
—  Diana Andrews, food editor/
test kitchen manager, Fine Cooking

2.  Caramelized
Roast Onions 
“To get nicely caramelized roast 
onions, toss a mix of small peeled 
pearl onions and cipollini on a 
baking sheet with olive oil, salt, 
and pepper. Roast at 400°F in 
a convection oven for 25 to 30 
minutes; stir occasionally. Use the 
onions warm in salads or as a side 
dish with meats and poultry.” 
— Kate Winslow and Guy Ambrosino, 
authors of Onions, Etcetera: The 
Essential Allium Cookbook

8.  Even, Golden 
Toasting
“For toasting nuts, youʼll get an 
even, golden hue with far less 
tossing and turning if you use a 
convection oven. Nuts should be 
toasted in a shallow baking pan 
so that air can circulate around 
them. Toast at 350°F, but check 
the nuts for browning after 
3 minutes.” 
— Lisa Lahey, food editor, 
Fine Cooking
« 
4.  Grill Flavor 
in a Flash
“Make food ahead, even when youʼre 
grilling. Prepare and grill your meats 
and vegetables, conventionally or on 
an indoor grill; then, just before 
serving, quickly heat them in a 425°F 
convection oven. The food tastes 
like it just came oﬀ  the grill. If youʼll 
have more than 30 minutes between 
grilling and serving, refrigerate, then 
bring back to room temperature be-
fore ﬂ ash-heating.” 
— Ronne Day, senior food editor/stylist, 
Fine Cooking 
6. Better Biscuits
“For ﬂ uﬀ y, tender biscuits, bake 
them in a convection oven on a ﬂ at 
cookie sheet. The convection heat 
will circulate hot air, giving the 
biscuits a golden brown top with a 
soft, moist interior.” 
— Lisa Lahey, food editor, 
Fine Cooking
3.  Dried Fruit Without 
Special Equipment
“Dehydrate vegetables and fruits (you 
can even make fruit ʻleatherʼ) using 
your convection oven. For apples, 
thinly slice, stem, and seed the fruit 
(the thinner they are, the shorter 
the dehydration time), and place 
on sheet trays, Silpats, or on Wolf 
dehydration racks (sold separately) 
in a 135°F oven in convection mode 
for 6 to 7 hours.” 
—  Diana Andrews, food editor/test 
kitchen manager, Fine Cooking 
 5.  Crisp Roast 
Chicken
“For extra-crispy skin and a nice 
brown coloring when roasting 
chicken and turkey, use a 
low-sided roasting pan and 
convection heat. The low sides 
on the pan will allow for better 
air circulation around the food, 
and that will help foods become 
more brown and crisp.”
— Ronne Day, senior food editor/stylist, 
Fine Cooking

Tender Fish

NOTE FROM THE EDITOR
8  F I N E CO O K I N G • J UNE / JULY 2 0 17
The other day as I was making 
gumbo—enough gumbo, as it turned 
out, to feed an army—it hit me how 
immensely satisfying I found the 
process. From mincing the garlic and 
chopping the vegetables to slowly 
cooking the roux until it was a deep 
mahogany hue, the sights, sounds, and 
smells were intoxicating. Cooking has, 
for me at least, never been purely about 
the end result, but the hands-on craf  
along the way. It’s the small details that 
make it such a pleasure. I’m sure many 
of you feel the same way, and I know 
our authors do. 
Take chef Marcus Samuelsson, for 
example. We recently interviewed him 
(p. 56) at one of his restaurants, Red 
Rooster Harlem. While we were in the 
kitchen, he shared his thoughts about 
food as a personal story and connection, 
all while demonstrating how to cook his 
amazing Steamed Salmon with Fennel 
and Citrus (p. 60). T e care he put into 
every detail of cooking was evident, but 
what stood out more was how content 
and relaxed he was—like he was spend-
ing time with an old friend. And, by the 
way, his salmon is exceptional.
For food writer and stylist Erin 
McDowell, joy comes from the simplic-
ity of assembling a fresh fruit cobbler. 
She’s created a rich, buttery biscuit 
dough, and lucky for us, she shares her 
master recipe so that you can create
your own amazing cobbler (p. 84) 
paired with your favorite fruit.
T ese pages are packed with recipes 
as satisfying to prepare as they are to eat. 
Fine Cooking’s food editor, Lisa Lahey, 
shows you how to make pesto beyond 
basil (p. 66) with ingredients like smoky 
roasted peppers, ripe tomatoes, ﬁ gs, and 
even grapefruit. Making these vibrant 
sauces will deﬁ nitely excite your senses 
and creativity as you consider all the 
ways to serve them. And those who like 
to grill must try the small-bird recipes 
(p. 72) developed by another Fine Cook-
ing staﬀ  member, senior food editor/
stylist Ronne Day. She oﬀ ers up a great 
method for producing the juiciest, most 
ﬂ avorful results. T ese dishes are perfect 
for entertaining outdoors.
T is brings me back to that big pot 
of gumbo. I’ve made the stew dozens 
of times, and each batch comes out 
slightly diﬀ erent. Maybe it’s the length 
of time the roux has cooked, the type 
of Andouille sausage, or the mix of 
peppers, but what never changes is the 
endless delight I get from creating it and 
giving those around me something good 
to eat. For us at Fine Cooking, that’s what 
it’s all about. 
Enjoy!
—Kathy Kingsley, editorial director

Get to know 
avocados
•  Mexico is by far the largest 
producer of avocados, but 
they’re also grown in many 
other countries, including 
the United States. 
•  There are at least 15 differ-
ent varieties, but the Hass 
accounts for 80% of the 
avocados grown around the 
world. It has a rich flavor and 
wonderful creamy texture.
•  Avocados ripen only once 
picked. Perfectly ripe ones 
give a little when  pressed. 
If hard, leave at room tem-
perature to ripen (put them 
in a bag with a banana if you 
want to speed the process 
along). Once ripe, refrigerate 
to prevent overripening. If 
you feel air pockets, the fruit 
is past its prime. 
•  Nutrients are concentrated 
close to the peel, so use the 
“nick and peel” method for 
removing the thick skin (see 
p. 98) to ensure that you’re 
getting the most from your 
avocado. 
•  Once cut, avocados oxidize 
quickly (the flesh turns 
brown). Prep just before 
using, if possible. Otherwise, 
cover with plastic wrap, or 
top with a squirt of lemon or 
lime juice to slow oxidation.

FOOD SCIENCE
Maceration is a process of breaking down 
and sof ening various substances. In food 
preparation, the process most of en occurs 
when soaking fruit in sugar, alcohol, or other 
ﬂ avored liquids with the goals of sof ening 
and ﬂ avoring the fruit.
Why macerate?
Maceration changes a fruit’s ﬂ avor and texture and is useful for 
improving the texture of hard, underripe fresh fruit as well as for 
ﬂ avoring fruit at the peak of ripeness. When fruit is macerated, 
it sof ens and releases some of its ﬂ avor and aroma compounds 
through its plasmodesmata, which are small channels in the wall of 
a plant cell that allow molecules 
and substances to pass through 
(both in and out) as needed. 
Af er maceration, fruit becomes 
something new, a complex 
mingling of ﬂ avors and textures. 
T e sof  fruit and liquid have 
many uses: a tasty dessert on 
its own topped with a dollop 
of whipped cream; a sauce 
for ice cream or cake; or a 
ﬁ lling for pie or a cake where 
it adds not only ﬂ avor, but 
color and moisture.

Sweet spot
Sugar is hygroscopic, mean-
ing that it attracts and bonds 
with water. When you macer-
ate with sugar, the water in 
the fruit is drawn out into the 
surrounding sugar (through 
osmosis; see above right). As 
water leaves the fruit, its cells 
lose volume, reducing the 
internal pressure on the fruit’s 
cell walls, which then relax, 
causing the fruit to soften.  
MEDIUMS FOR MACERATION
Liquids for maceration include liquors, liqueurs, 
wine, fruit juice, vinegars, and water that may 
(and in the case of water must) be infused with 
all sorts of flavorings, like spices, herbs, tea, and 
coffee. When it’s sprinkled on fruit, sugar draws 
out moisture, creating its own juicy medium for 
maceration. Liquids for maceration are sometimes
heated to hasten the process or to infuse flavors
prior to maceration, but maceration does not 
require the application of heat.
Osmosis has a role
Osmosis is a process in which a fluid flows through 
a semipermeable membrane (such as a cell wall) 
from an area of lower concentration to one of higher 
concentration. Salt and sugar are two of the most 
common catalysts for osmosis, so putting either 
ingredient into your maceration mixture will trigger 
the process. When either comes in contact with food, 
it works to reach a state of equilibrium with the water 
content of the food itself to balance the concentra-
tion of the salt or sugar in the solution, so beginning 
the process of osmosis; available water contained 
within the cells of the fruit is drawn out. The loss of 
water from the fruit causes it to soften; it also con-
centrates its natural flavors.
Osmosis works pretty much the same way when 
fruit is macerated in alcohol because alcohol, like 
sugar, is hydrophilic and will “steal” water from the 
fruit, with other flavor molecules along for the ride.

Flavor boost
Liquids that have already been infused with flavor 
provide the fastest flavor transfer to the macerat-
ing fruit. For instance, it’s best to macerate fruit in a 
cinnamon-infused liquid rather than adding a cin-
namon stick directly to a cold water-based macera-
tion liquid. However, alcohol extracts flavor really 
effectively. When using a liquid with a high alcohol 
content, such as whiskey, you can add any mix of 
whole spices, aromatics, or other flavorful solids be-
cause their flavors will be extracted without heating.

Sublime Sake
It’s not beer and it’s not wine, but a diﬀ erent kind of brew 
with a clean taste that pairs perfectly well with all kinds of food. 
TEX T BY NA NE T TE M A X I M ; R EC I P E S B Y D I AN A AN D REWS
Most sake bottles include the 
type or grade, ingredients, 
rice polishing ratio (indicat-
ing how much of the grain 
remains after polishing), 
production date, and maybe 
even the city and prefecture 
where it was brewed. Some 
labels also have a Sake Meter 
Value, which is an indicator 
for gauging dry versus sweet. 
The higher the value, the drier 
the sake.
Here is a guide to the major 
types and classifications of 
sake.
•  Junmai: The word means “pure 
rice,” and this type of sake has 
no added alcohol. The polishing 
rate is usually 70%, and they 
tend to have a rich full body with 
a slightly acidic flavor.
•  Honjozo: A type of sake that 
also has been polished to a mini-
mum rate of 70%, with a small 
amount of distilled brewer’s 
alcohol added. Honjozo sakes 
are often light and easy to drink.
•  Ginjo: Sake that uses rice that 
has been polished to at least 
60% and brewed using special 
yeast and fermenting tech-
niques. The result is often a light, 
fruity, and complex flavor that is 
usually quite fragrant. 
•  Daiginjo: This is a super-premium 
sake and requires precise brewing 
methods. Look for a 50% polishing 
rate on the label. Daiginjo sakes are 
light, complex, and fragrant tasting, 
and best served chilled. 
•  Futsushu: This is considered a 
basic or table sake and is almost 
always served warm. The rice 
has barely been polished (93% 
to 70% polishing rate). 
•  Namazke: This type of sake is 
unpasteurized and needs to be 
kept refrigerated. It usually has a 
more vibrant, livelier taste than 
pasteurized sake. (Most sakes 
are pasteurized twice.) 
•  Nigori: A cloudy, white, coarsely 
filtered sake that may contain 
very small bits of unfermented 
rice.  It’s usually sweet and creamy, 
and best shaken before using. 
•  Koshu: Sake that has been aged 
at least three years. It may taste 
rich, nutty, or slightly sherrylike.  
•  Sparkling: This style of sake is

For many of us, the term sake conjures

Sake is almost always made from four

called sakami), water, a mold called koji-
kin, and yeast. Some are also fortiﬁ ed with 
a distilled alcohol (see below). T ough 
of en called “rice wine,” it’s brewed from a 
grain, similar to beer. 
T ere are many variables that aﬀ ect

the grains are polished before brewing. 
T e more polished, the less protein and fat 
are present, which leads to a cleaner fer-
mentation. T erefore, sake made with rice 
polished to 50 percent of its original grain 
is considered of higher quality than one 
with only 30 percent removed. T e miner-
als found in the water used also play a 
role in the ﬂ avor of sake; for example, too

Why Marcus Samuelsson
LOVES SALMON
Growing up in Sweden, Marcus Samuelsson 
ate a lot of salmon. As he explains in Marcus 
Of Duty, his cookbook about what he cooks at 
home, salmon in Sweden was a “weeknight dish 
that every family had almost every week.” One 
might think that such regular exposure would 
make him tire of the rich, full-lavored ish. Quite 
the contrary; he still loves it. Over the years, the 
multiple James Beard Award-winning chef has 
consistently celebrated salmon on his menus, 
starting at Aquavit in New York City, where he 
made his name as a bold young head chef more 
than 20 years ago. Under his helm, the three-star 
Nordic restaurant at times featured no fewer than 
eight diferent salmon dishes. 
here’s more. Before he and his wife married in 
Ethiopia, where both were born (he moved to Swe-
den when he was adopted as a young boy),  
they had a big prewedding party in Smögen, a tiny 
island town he still regularly visits. Samuelsson 
admits he let most of the party planning to his wife

Most people are familiar with basil pesto, that wonderfully fra-
grant basil purée ﬂ avored with garlic, olive oil, pine nuts, and cheese. T e 
uncooked sauce, which hails from Genoa, gets its name from the Italian 
pestare, meaning to pound, because it’s traditionally made in a mortar
and pestle (see Test Kitchen, p. 93). You can ﬁ nd recipes for basil pesto 
everywhere, including some delicious versions at FineCooking.com. What 
I want to share with you are ﬂ avorful pestos you won’t ﬁ nd anywhere else, 
ones that feature ingredients such as smoky roasted peppers; ripe tomatoes 
and creamy ricotta; or ﬁ gs, ginger, and—whoa!—grapefruit. And don’t 
worry: You don’t need to haul out the mortar and pestle; these whip up fast 
in a food processor. 
Tame the garlic, skip the extra-virgin olive oil, and choose your 
nuts. T ese pestos include many of the same ingredients as the classic, 
including garlic for ﬂ avor and oil to smooth the texture and carry ﬂ avor. In 
a traditional basil pesto, the garlic goes in raw, but in many of these recipes, 
I tame its heat by blanching it in oil or roasting it to let the other ﬂ avors 
shine. Similarly, I use regular olive oil, not extra-virgin, because the latter 
can mask subtle ﬂ avors and tends to become bitter in the food processor. 
Since nuts are mild in ﬂ avor (they mostly add rich-
ness), you can use what’s on hand; if you don’t have 
the exact one called for, replace it with another. 
Enjoy your pesto right away, or save for future 
good eating. T ere are many ways to enjoy pesto; look 
on p. 69 for some ideas. All of these recipes make 
about 2 cups, and the pestos will keep, covered and 
refrigerated, for a few days. Even better, they can 
be portioned and frozen. When it’s time to grill 
a steak or make pizza, you have a secret weapon 
on hand for boosting ﬂ avor in an instant. Presto!

As someone who works behind the bar, I know how 
controversial craf ing a drink can be. Just look at the volumes 
compiled about the “proper” martini, a two-ingredient cock-
tail. And guests are constantly telling me about their interpre-
tations of how to make an old fashioned. But few drinks elicit 
such passionate distrust as sangria, that lightly spiced and 
sweetened wine cocktail traditionally consumed during the 
summer in Portugal and Spain. 
I think the reason so many people view this libation with 
skepticism is that they have tried only the sweet, syrupy versions 
served at mediocre restaurants. Well-made sangria, though, can 
be a wonderfully complex beverage as versatile with food as it 
is delicious on its own. Sangria pairs, perhaps obviously, with 
Spanish foods like paella, dried fruits, hard cheeses, oily 
almonds, cured meats, and chilled shellﬁ sh. It also goes 
well with all kinds of summertime dinner fare from Tex-
Mex to barbecue to burgers. 
A customizable drink 
Don’t believe anyone who tells you that there’s one speciﬁ c 
recipe for sangria and that anything else isn’t real. T e 
only requirement to making sangria is that it contains 
wine. Everything else is based on personal preference. I 
start with an all-purpose sangria base, basically a mix of 
simple syrup, fresh (always) orange juice, brandy, and a 
dash of bitters. To that, I add a bottle of decent wine (see 
“Which wine?” on the facing page) and fruit, always 
some citrus and then whatever else is at its seasonal 
best. Because I go light on the amount of syrup, the 
resulting drink is not at all cloying; instead, it’s reﬁ ned 
and refreshing. Don’t believe me? Follow the tips here, 
make my basic recipe—I go into more detail on the 
various components on the facing page—and discover 
for yourself just how sensational sangria can be.

Bring on the brandy
Why fortify your sangria with more 
booze? Why not? Aside from add-
ing some kick, brandy contributes 
depth of flavor that wine alone 
can’t match. Try heavier European 
brandies, such as Cognac, for red-
wine sangrias and lighter California 
brandies for white.
Part of the joy of making sangria is 
showcasing your favorite fresh fruit. I go 
heavy on the citrus, using assorted kinds 
to make up half of the total amount of 
fruit I’m using. For the other fruit, grapes, 
kiwi, and fresh berries are among my 
go-tos, but try apples, peaches, plums, 
mango, melon, or pineapple in yours. 
And never mind that old frat-boy adage 
that all of the alcohol eventually resides 
inside the fruit—that’s nonsense. After a 
reasonable period of time in the liquid, 
the alcohol content of the fruit will be 
exactly the same as the alcohol content 
of the sangria, and no more.

In a small saucepan, combine the

A very simple syrup
Sangria should be a little sweet. 
Because sugar doesn’t dissolve 
easily in cold liquid, a simple syrup, 
in which the sugar is dissolved in hot 
water and then cooled, works best. 
I make a rich simple syrup, which 
means a sugar-to-water ratio of 
2-to-1. Because you need to add less 
syrup, the wine’s flavor stays more 
pronounced. If you want to get fancy, 
add a stick of cinnamon or a vanilla 
bean to the mix. Use leftover syrup 
to sweeten iced tea, lemonade, or 
cocktails.
Fresh juice, please
Do not use orange juice from  a carton, bottle, 
or can in your sangria. If you don’t think you can 
spare the five minutes to juice fresh oranges, 
pick up a six-pack of beer instead.
Which wine?
For a classic red sangria, choose something 
light but with some character, like an inex-
pensive Spanish Garnacha. For a summery 
sangria blanca, opt for a citrusy and floral 
white, like a Spanish Albariño. If you’re look-
ing for something fun and not so traditional, 
grab a Spanish cava, which will give the drink 
a little fizz so long as it’s added just before 
serving. Whatever you choose, don’t think 
that spending $5 on a bottle of wine is going

Grilled chicken makes a delicious, if 
somewhat predictable, summertime 
meal. For something more exciting but 
also crowd-pleasing, I love to grill smaller 
birds whole. Because of their tiny size 
(see “From Tiny to Small,” facing page), 
birds such as game hens, quail, and pous-
sin cook up in a fraction of the time it 
takes to grill a larger chicken, with results 
that are amazingly tender and juicy.
Preparation is minimal and done in 
minutes. There’s very little to do to get 
small birds ready for the grill. Indeed, 
the trickiest part may be getting your 
hands on them in the first place. Cornish 
game hen is carried at most supermar-
kets (often frozen), but you might have 
to go to a specialty market for quail or 
poussin (or order online; see p. 100). 
You can also substitute a small chicken, 
preferably one 3 lb. or less, which will 
work with the grilling method and flavor-
ing recipes that follow.
Once your bird of choice is in hand, the 
next step is to butterfly, or spatchcock, 
it. This technique helps it to cook more 
quickly and evenly. That word may sound 
scary, but it’s simply a matter of cutting 
out the backbone, an easy task with a 
pair of scissors, and pressing the bird flat 
with your hands (see photos p. 74). 
Grill the birds over direct heat to 
start, then finish over indirect heat.  
Prepared the way most pitmasters grill 
barbecued chicken, these birds cook up 
best if they have a hot start 
and a cooler finish. By start-
ing the birds breast side 
down over high heat, the 
skin cooks nicely. While a 
barbecued bird will not 
be crisp, the high heat 
gives the skin nice grill 
marks and savory flavor. 
The birds are then 
moved to a cooler part 
of the grill to finish cook-
ing without drying out.
Add flavor in stages. 
When I cook, I add flavor 
at just about every oppor-
tunity. In each of the recipes 
beginning on p. 75, you’ll find 
a rub for seasoning before 
grilling, a glaze that gets brushed 
on during grilling, and a flavored 
butter slathered on afterward. This 
may sound like a lot of work, but each 
component comes together quickly and 
can be made ahead. The rub imbues 
flavor, the glaze adds color and sweet 
tanginess, and the butter adds welcome 
richness and a tantalizing aroma to the 
lean birds. All this flavor, plus the joy of 
something unexpected, makes these 
small birds something special.

basket.

farmed. Wild salmon is exclusively from Alaska and the Pacific

Speed up browning with baking soda
Caramelized onions can take a while to make. If you need them on 
a weeknight (as for the avocado tartine on p. 23), you may need to 
speed things up. If so, reach for the baking soda. Onions brown due to 
a chemical reaction between amino acids and reducing sugars. The 
reaction is affected by a number of variables, the 
most obvious being temperature, but pH also plays 
a role. A bit of baking soda increases the pH level, 
causing the onions to brown more quickly. Don’t 
add too much, though; it will adversely affect flavor. 
Try about 1⁄8  tsp. per large onion, and add it at the 
beginning of cooking.                                                     —R.D.
