NOTE FROM THE EDITOR
10  FI N E CO O K I N G • A UG / S E P T 2 0 17
Farmers’ markets and farmstands are more than just places to 
buy extraordinary heirloom tomatoes, sweet corn, and just-picked juicy 
peaches. For me, and I’m sure many of you, it’s where inspiration strikes 
and a meal is born. As I stroll by open-air stalls bursting with fresh, color-
ful fruits and vegetables, I can’t help but imagine all of the ﬂ avorful dishes 
practically begging to be made. 
Not only is all of the luscious produce we’ve been dreaming of for 
months at its peak right now, but these ingredients don’t require much in 
the way of preparation to make them shine. Summery tomato and bread 
salads (p. 56) are a perfect example of this idea. Susie Middleton, author 
of the recently published Simple Green Suppers and a former Fine Cooking 
editor, shows us that simplicity can be delicious. Ripe, juicy tomatoes plus 
bread require little to become a standout dish. Although Susie shares some 
of her favorite recipes, she also gives you the building blocks to tweak them 
depending on what looks good at the market. 
A roundup of favorite marinades from our very own staﬀ  (p. 62) pro-
vides other easy options for stress-free summer cooking. A soak in one of 
these seasoned liquids will impart bold ﬂ avors to meat, ﬁ sh, and vegetables, 
giving you easy and delicious options for grilled dinners all season long. 
Or if an impeccably grilled steak is what you’re af er, Food Science authors 
David Joachim and Andrew Schloss share everything you need to know to 
get it right—in other words, juicy and tender on the inside with a charred, 
ﬂ avorful crust (p. 42).
And don’t miss cookbook author Anjali Pathak’s late-summer menu 
(p. 68). She features the ﬂ avorful spice characteristics of Indian food, while 
focusing on ingredients that are known to Westerners, such as ribs, pota-
toes, green beans, and plums. It’s an exceptional yet casual meal that will 
leave your guests asking for more—and the recipes, too!
T is time of year, the best cooking is all about showcasing what’s fresh 
and seasonal with ease and simplicity, and that’s what you’ll ﬁ nd on these 
pages. Plus, this leaves you more time to enjoy the results of your (minimal) 
labor—preferably outside with your feet up, sipping a cool drink. 
Cheers!
—Kathy Kingsley, editorial director

Susie Middleton (“Simple, Summery 
Tomato-Bread Salads,” p. 56), the former 
editor of Fine Cooking, is the author of four 
vegetable-inspired cookbooks. She’s also special projects editor for 
The Vineyard Gazette Media Group on Martha’s Vineyard, where 
she has a farmstand. Visit Susie at Sixburnersue.com.
• My current ingredient obsession is... kimchi. Kimchi fritters, 
kimchi omelets—kimchi, kimchi, kimchi!
• My favorite place to eat fresh summer tomatoes is... right in 
the garden as I’m harvesting for my farmstand.
• My go-to summer dessert is... homemade “backyard berry” 
ice cream. We’re surrounded by wild blackberries and blueberries, 
and there is nothing better than homemade blackberry ice cream.
In addition to preparing Fine Cooking’s 
drinks pairings, Jill Silverman Hough is 
a cookbook author, food and wine writer, 
recipe developer, cooking teacher, and food photographer. To learn 
more, visit JillHough.com.
• My favorite summer beverage is... a homemade punch of 
rosé and ginger ale. It’s the perfect poolside quaff—slightly sweet, 
slightly tart, super refreshing, and you can drink it all day without 
getting more than perfectly, slightly buzzed.
• My go-to ingredient is... smoked paprika. It’s key to any self-
respecting summer spice rub.
• The wine that people need to discover is... lambrusco, a fizzy 
red wine that you drink cold. It’s great for summer wine pairing  
because it works with red-wine-deserving foods, like grilled steak or 
lamb, but unlike other reds, it’s cool and refreshing. Plus, bubbles!
Chef and food writer Anjali Pathak (“Menu 
from Mumbai,” p. 68) grew up fully immersed 
in food: Her family started the specialty-food 
brand Patak’s. The author of Indian Family Kitchen, Anjali is based in 
Mumbai, where she runs the city’s first luxury culinary school.
• I like being a cooking teacher because... it brings me enormous 
pleasure seeing the joy cooking brings to someone. I enjoy sharing 
the love I have for food by bringing the fun back into the kitchen.
• My next culinary travel destination is... South Korea. I’ve always 
been intrigued by Korean cuisine and culinary techniques.
• The spice people need to get to know is... black cardamom. 
Unlike its green sibling, black cardamom has a mesmerizing 
smoky, deep flavor and is amazing in grilled dishes.
Justin Shoults (“How to Cook Grilled 
Octopus,” p. 76) is executive chef of Bos-
ton’s Oak + Rowan, where he’s known for 
his locally sourced cuisine. Justin, whose culinary philosophy is 
“less is more,” was named one of Zagat Boston’s “30 Under 30.”
• I love Boston for its... atmosphere. The people are great, the 
food scene is up and coming, and I love the history of the area. 
• My guilty-pleasure snack is... Wheat Thins and Cheez Whiz. 
More Cheez Whiz than cracker is key.
• The three people I want to cook dinner for are... my family. 
In a dream world, I would love to travel back in time and cook a 
grand dinner for Escoffier and Antoine Carême.
Cheryl Sternman Rule (“Frozen 
Yogurt,” p. 86) is an award-winning food 
writer based in Silicon Valley, California. 
She is the author of two cookbooks, Ripe and Yogurt Culture, and 
the founder of the global all-yogurt website TeamYogurt.com.
• The three things always in my fridge are... whole milk, sour-
dough starter, and a block of Parmigiano-Reggiano.
• My favorite accompaniment for frozen yogurt is... toasted 
coconut and/or pistachios (preferably both). I wouldn’t kick dark 
chocolate shavings or cacao nibs out of bed, either.
• My most recent kitchen purchase was... a coffee grinder. 
After more years than I can count, I switched from being a tea 
person to being a coffee person. It’s a shocker, even to me.
Zoë François (“Crêpes,” p. 78) is a 
pastry chef and baker trained at the 
Culinary Institute of America. In addi-
tion to writing the cookbook series Artisan Bread in Five Minutes a 
Day, she creates pastry recipes for her websites, ZoeBakes.com and 
Breadin5, as well as for local restaurants and national magazines.
• My favorite crêpe filling is... cheese. I grew up eating blintzes at 
the Kiev Restaurant in New York City with my mom in the 1970s, 
so a simple cheese blintz brings back sweet memories.
• The next big food trend will be... strong spices and flavors. As 
the world gets smaller, the flavors will get bolder. Think Middle 
Eastern, Indian, African.
• My favorite kind of bread is... Potato Flour Milk Bread, a recipe 
I’m working on for a new book; I can’t get enough of it.

Back to (cooking) school reading  BY JOAN N E S MART
Salt Fat Acid Heat
Mastering the Elements of Good Cooking
By Samin Nosrat (Simon & Schuster; $35) 
Learning should be fun, right? Samin Nosrat 
must think so because her book, both informa-
tive and intimate, sciencey and slightly silly, 
makes it so. Here’s her thesis: Understand the 
four elements in her book’s title, and everything 
you cook will be delicious. While studying English 
at UC Berkeley, Nosrat saved up to eat at Chez 
Panisse. After writing Alice Waters personally, 
she got a job there in 2000 and spent the next 
four years learning how to cook. Though she 
went on to become a chef in her own right (she 
now mostly teaches and writes about cooking), 
she hasn’t forgotten what it was like be a novice 
in the kitchen, which makes her tome superap-
proachable. Those English classes paid off, too, 
because Nosrat writes very engagingly. When 
talking about the importance of fat, she sug-
gests the reader try and imagine cooking without 
it. “What would a croissant be without butter?” 
she asks before answering: “Pointless, that’s 
what.” And she deftly weaves in anecdotes 
from her life, both as the daughter of Iranian 
immigrants and an aspiring cook, to help make 
her points. Once you’ve read all about how acid 
works and how to balance salt, it’s on to her reci-
pes, which make up about half of the book’s 462 
pages and cover a lot of basics, deliciously.   
 
Don’t miss: Conveyor Belt Chicken
Not so much a recipe as a method to yield 
amazingly crisp skin-on boned-out thighs. See 
p. 325 in the book to learn how it got its name.
Bravetart
Iconic American Desserts 
By Stella Parks (W.W. Norton; $35)
There’s an unofficial two-part test around here 
that determines whether a new baking book is 
any good. First, copy editor and bakeophile Chris 
Hoelck “borrows” the galley and begins baking 
from it. If the book is good, he doesn’t stop. Such 
is the case with Bravetart, from award-winning 
pastry chef Stella Parks. Between the covers: 
recipes for just about every American dessert 
that ever existed, including chocolate chip and 
oatmeal cookies, classic cherry and Key lime pies, 
devil’s and angel’s food cakes, plus engaging 
explanations on how many of these desserts 
came to be. The blondies and brownies Chris 
shared were spot on, but he was even more 
smitten with Parks’ Red (Wine) Velvet Cake—the 
wine underscores the fruity chocolate—and her 
method for caramelizing white chocolate by 
“roasting” it in the oven to give it a complex, malty 
flavor. But what many readers will love best is 
Parks’ homemade versions of the branded treats 
they grew up on: Nilla Wafers, Oreos, Milky Ways, 
and 3 Musketeers. As Parks notes in her introduc-
tion, she’s not out to “fix” these faves but rather 
to make them taste as they’re meant to be. Her 
versions are not as overly sweet as the packaged 
goods (a plus), but they still conjure that feeling 
you got upon opening your lunch box to find a 
Hostess Cupcake inside. In other words, pure joy.  
A great idea: Snip Doodles. Adding a cup of 
milk to the dough transforms snickerdoodles 
into a fragrant coffee cake best enjoyed warm.
Six Seasons
A New Way with Vegetables 
By Joshua McFadden (Artisan; $35) 
In his debut cookbook, Joshua McFadden, chef 
of Ava Gene’s in Portland, Oregon, wants to 
change how cooks think about seasonality: For-
get four seasons, he says, and make it six. Before 
moving west to cook, he managed the innova-
tive Four Season Farm on the coast of Maine. 
Despite the farm’s name, McFadden came to 
the conclusion that summer isn’t just one sea-
son but three: early, mid, and late. These, plus 
fall, winter, and spring, make up the bulk of the 
chapters of his book. Within the chapters, peak-
of-the-season vegetables are highlighted with 
lessons on how to choose, store, and prep. The 
gorgeously styled and photographed recipes 
reflect the kind of right-from-the-farm dishes he 
serves at his restaurant. (While not strictly vege-
tarian, the bulk of the 225 recipes are meatless.) 
Thanks to help from Martha Holmberg (a former 
Fine Cooking editor), the recipes are also written 
in a friendly voice perfect for the relaxed cook 
who doesn’t fret over the precise measurement 
of a “glug of olive oil.” Exciting flavor combina-
tions in recipes like Roasted Cauliflower, Plums, 
Sesame Seeds, and Yogurt, plus Sugar Snap 
Peas with Pickled Cherries and Peanuts, mean 
this is no mere guide to vegetables but a primer 
on how to make them taste their exciting best.  
Not a typo: Burnt Carrots with Honey, Black 
Pepper, Butter, and Almonds. The blackened, 
somewhat bitter bits of the carrot get beauti-
fully balanced by rich butter and sweet honey.

it at cool room temperature

Grilling Steak
What you need to know to get 
perfect results: juicy and tender 
on the inside with a charred, 
flavorful crust
BY DAVID JOACH I M A N D A N DR EW S C H LO S S
FOOD SCIENCE
Know your cuts
Tender steaks come from the muscles that get the least 
amount of exercise, which run along the back of the cattle—
the rib, short loin, and sirloin. Steaks that are cut from 
here—tenderloin, T-bone, porterhouse, rib-eye, and strip—
have thin, diaphanous connective tissue that tenderizes 
easily on the hot, dry heat of a grill. 
Tougher cuts of meat come from the areas that move or 
support the body. T ey usually require low, slow cooking 
with moist heat to become tender. However, there are a few 
tougher steaks, such as skirt, hanger, ﬂ ank, and ﬂ at iron, 
that, when sliced against the grain af er grilling, yield tender 
meat with an added beneﬁ t: a richer ﬂ avor that comes from 
a more exercised muscle. 
Tender at the bone? 
T e honeycomb structure of bone 
slows heat transfer so that the parts 
of a steak bordering the bone cook 
more slowly. T e phrase “tender at 
the bone” refers to the succulence 
of meat near the bone that is rarer. 
When to salt
Salt your steak generously before cooking. Use coarse 
salt; the bigger crystals are easier to feel and see and, 
therefore, to control and distribute evenly.
Ten minutes after salting, the surface of the steak 
becomes moist as the salt draws out juices that are a 
mixture of water and savory liquefied proteins. If you 
grill your steak right away, this moisture will be lost to 
the heat of the grill. But after 1 hour under refrigera-
tion, some of this liquid will be reabsorbed with the 
salt to add flavor and moisture. Wait longer—up to 
8 hours—and more will reabsorb, while some will evap-
orate, leaving a slightly thickened liquid on the surface 
that will aid in making a more pronounced crust.

Heat plus smoke equals ﬂ avor
Searing at high heat allows browning (Mail-
lard) reactions, which begin at about 250°F, 
to take place on the surface of the steak, 
creating a ﬂ avorful outer crust; amino acids, 
sugars, and fats break down and react to 
create new ﬂ avor compounds such as furans 
and pyrazines. 
Maillard browning requires a hot, dry 
environment. Hardwood lump charcoal burns 
hotter and drier than gas, making it the best 
choice for browning and crust development. 
Hardwood also releases ﬂ avor compounds 
in its smoke, such as guaiacol and syringol, 
which produce noticeably tastier steak. If you 
have a gas grill, you can use wood chips to im-
part ﬂ avor, but you won’t get the same crust.
Keep it juicy
Contrary to popular belief, searing a steak does not seal in 
juices. The key to a juicy steak is to not overcook it. Meat 
consists of about 70 percent water. Around 120°F, meat 
protein starts to coagulate and squeeze water from the 
cells. At around 140°F, connective tissues surrounding 
protein fibers begin to shrink, squeezing yet more moisture 
from the cells. As a result, steaks cooked above 155°F 
(medium well) lose most of their moisture and become dry. 
The perception of juiciness also comes from melted fat 
(one reason why well-marbled steaks tend to taste juicier). 
Flip out
Steaks need to be ﬂ ipped for even 
cooking and to help develop an 
even crust on both sides. On a hot 
grill with no lid, all the heat comes 
from below, and frequent ﬂ ipping 
transfers heat equally to both 
sides of the steak, promoting even 
cooking. If you do use a lid, it will 
trap heat and send some to the top 
of the steak, so you may be able to 
ﬂ ip less of en. 
Grills tend to have hot spots, 
so it also helps to rotate the steak, 
or move its location on the grill 
periodically to create an evenly 
browned surface.
The right temp 
Contrary to popular opinion, 
it is unwise to bring a steak to 
room temperature before grilling. 
While you want the surface of 
a steak to get super hot during 
cooking to create a thick browned 
crust, you don’t want to overcook 
the inside, which makes it beneﬁ -
cial to start with a cooler interior. 
It’s also unwise for another 
reason: Most harmful bacteria 
on a steak are on the surface and 
multiply when the surface tem-
perature rises above 40°F.

Better with age
Aging beef makes it more tender and ﬂ avorful. 
Dry-aging between 34°F to 38°F in high humidity 
(70 to 80 percent) allows enzymes in the meat to 
break down proteins into more ﬂ avorful amino 
acids; glycogen into sweet sugars; and fat into

Collagen in connective tissue gelatinizes more 
readily, allowing meat to tenderize more quickly 
and at lower temperatures; and connective tis-
sues surrounding muscle ﬁ bers relax so that less 
juice is squeezed during cooking. T e result: 
tender, juicy meat that can be served quite rare.

Checking for doneness
T e red color of meat comes not from blood, but from myoglobin, a red-pigmented 
protein. Most of the red “juices” in steak are myoglobin dissolved in water. Around 140°F, 
myoglobin starts to denature and releases oxygen, and the meat color changes from red to 
grayish brown. T e interior color, however, is not a reliable indicator of doneness. When 
you cut into a steak to check its doneness, the myoglobin is exposed to air and again binds 
with oxygen, which turns myoglobin back to a deeper shade of red. For the most accurate 
doneness test, use an instant-read thermometer instead of checking the color.

Simple, Summery  
Tomato-Bread Salads
Juicy, vine-ripened beauties shine in satisfying no-cook dishes.
By Susie Middleton

--- Page 57 ---
Basic (But  
Delicious!) Tomato  
and Bread Salad,  
p. 58
There’s no question that tomatoes and crusty bread are 
made for each other. The alchemy that occurs as the bright, 
sweet juice of the tomatoes soaks into the bread is why people love  
bruschetta and BLTs. And it’s what makes a tomato and bread 
salad—ripe tomatoes cut into chunks and tossed with bite-size 
pieces of bread, a tangy vinaigrette, and fresh herbs—so satisfy-
ing as a main course for lunch, a light supper, or a sensational side. 
Come summer, when my tomato plants are weighed down by juicy 
beefsteaks and adorable Sungolds, I make bread salads often, includ-
ing riffs on the classic Italian panzanella, made with a hearty Italian 
bread, and Middle Eastern fattoush, made with pita. But I don’t stop 
there. Thanks to a range of bread styles, so many beautiful and tasty 
tomato varieties (see p. 60 for some of my favorites), plus add-ins, 
such as olives and fennel, this pretty dish is endlessly adaptable. 
Read on for more details about the building blocks for a great tomato 
and bread salad. I’ve provided a few recipes, but to be honest, I almost 
never make my salads exactly the same every time. It’s too much fun 
tweaking the formula based on what I’m in the mood to eat and what 
looks good in my garden. And after making so many delicious permu-
tations of this pretty dish, I’ve learned that as long as I start with good 
bread and tomatoes, it’s almost impossible to go wrong.

One-Dish Vegetarian Meals.

THE BREAD
Although bread salad was created as a way to use up old bread, 
it can be awkward to plan to let your bread go stale. That’s why 
I prefer to grill or broil it. I also love the flavor the bread picks 
up from a bit of char and the way the outside gets crisp but the 
inside stays slightly chewy, ready to sop up a tasty vinaigrette and 
delicious tomato juices. I especially like how ciabatta, with all of 
its airy pockets, grills. Italian bread, sourdough, and other rustic 
loaves work great, too. Whole-grain and rye breads are fun to 
experiment with, as are naan and pita.
 
Toast first, cut later. For the best flavor and texture, broil or  
grill thick slices of bread (or pita or naan) until golden brown or  
until grill marks have developed. Let cool briefly before cutting into 
bite-size pieces.
THE TOMATOES
You’ll want to use a good amount of beefsteaks, those big, classic 
beauties, because they tend to be the juiciest with a nice meaty 
texture. Add other varieties for a mix of flavors, textures, and 
colors (see p. 60). I like to add both plum and cherry tomatoes, 
including my favorite tomatoes of all, Sungolds, which are so 
sweet that I eat them like candy. At farmers’ markets, you can 
find quirky heirlooms—varieties passed down unaltered through 
the generations—that may look funky (they come in all sizes and 
shapes) but that boast superior flavor. For drama as well as bold 
flavor, look for increasingly popular “black” tomatoes. 
Cut to similar size and salt briefly. I prefer that my tomatoes be 
cut into bite-size (¾ to 1 inch) pieces. Depending on the size of the  
tomato, these can be halves, wedges, or slices, and a mix of differ-
ent cuts is always appealing. I like some of the juices to be drawn 
out by salting so that they can easily mingle with the dressing, but 
I also want each bite of tomato to be juicy. For that reason, I salt 
them, combine the tomatoes with the vinaigrette, and let them sit 
for 5 minutes. In that short time, some, but not all, of their juice will 
flow out. 
THE DRESSING
An oil-and-vinegar dressing is classic and adds welcome acid as 
well as moisture. I always add garlic to the dressing because I like 
that pop of flavor. However, you can change the kind of vinegar and 
add other flavor boosters, such as shallots, onions, and anchovies. 
HERBS AND OTHER ADDITIONS
Given the season, it makes sense to take advantage of the bold 
flavors of fresh herbs. I chop them and add them by the hand-
ful. (In fact, I rarely put them in a measuring cup, eyeballing the 
amount instead; if you do, don’t crush them.) I tend to stick with 
tender herbs such as basil, parsley, mint, tarragon, and cilantro. 
Bonus ingredients like olives, roasted red peppers, capers, thinly 
sliced fennel, cucumber, salad greens, beans, and cheeses like 
feta and mozzarella are also welcome additions. I personally 
don’t include meat or fish, but cooked bacon, pancetta, shrimp, or 
chicken added to a bread salad can make it feel more hearty.

Marinating
Always marinate in a nonreactive  
vessel. Aluminum and copper react with 
acidic ingredients, which can change the 
color of the food and give it a metallic  
flavor, so choose stainless steel, glass,  
or plastic instead. 
« Make sure the food is coated with  
the marinade. If the food is not covered  
completely by the marinade, turn it  
occasionally to make sure all sides are 
coated while marinating.
Cover and refrigerate for long soaks.  
Since most marinades penetrate only the 
outer ¼ inch of the food, a short marinat-
ing time is often sufficient, though longer 
can make food more flavorful.
 If soaking for more than 1 hour, cover 
and refrigerate the food in the marinade. 
If the food starts to turn whitish gray, it 
means acids or enzymes in the marinade 
are “cooking” the food; if you see this 
happening, remove the food from the 
marinade.
« If you plan to baste as you grill, 
reserve some marinade before soaking 
raw meat, poultry, or seafood. Use the 
reserved marinade for basting; that way, 
you will avoid bacterial contamination. 
Never use a marinade as a sauce if it 
has come into contact with raw meat

When I eat out, part of the fun comes from 
ordering foods I don’t normally cook at home. 
Charred octopus is one such item. While it has always been

At Oak + Rowan in Boston, chef Justin Shoults features

Good to Know
Octopus comes in a range of 
sizes. At his restaurant, chef 
Shoults tends to prepare baby 
ones that are just a couple 
of inches long once cooked. 
(Octopus shrinks mightily when 
cooked.) Slightly larger and 
thicker tentacles—about 1 inch 
across at the thickest end before 
cooking—can be easier to grill and 
a better introduction for cooking.
Look for octopus in the frozen 
food aisle. Almost all octopus  
is frozen before shipping. 
Fortunately, the quality of 
octopus does not suffer much 
from freezing; plus, freezing 
begins the tenderizing process. 
If octopus has been blanched—
it sometimes says so on the 
packaging—that’s fine and won’t 
affect the recipe here.
Look for cleaned octopus. 
Cleaning octopus is not for the 
squeamish as it involves remov-
ing the innards from the head 
sack, but most octopus is sold 
already cleaned. If you happen 
upon a fresh, uncleaned octo-
pus, ask the fishmonger to clean 
it for you. If the octopus still has 
the head and beak attached, 
cut off the head (if the octopus 
is large) and remove the beak, 
situated amid the legs, by pop-
ping it out the other side. 
To tenderize octopus, cook it 
until ... tender. Cooking time will 
vary depending on size. You can 
test with a knife—the thickest 
part will yield to the blade—but 
Shoults recommends taking a 
bite to be sure it’s tender.

I ate my first crêpe while sitting at 
the base of Mont Saint-Michel in 
France. It was an impossibly romantic moment 
on my honeymoon. The crêpe, a thin, tender pan-
cake with ever so slightly crisp edges, was wrapped 
around ham and melty cheese. My husband and I 
each took bites between sips from a bottle of Cal-
vados and thought we’d never be that lucky again. 
Little did we know we’d be eating crêpes the very 
next day in Paris and that they’d become a favorite 
regular meal of our trip. They fit into our modest 
budget and were so satisfying that with the endless 
varieties of fillings, we could eat them for breakfast, 
lunch, and dinner. 
Here in the U.S., crêpes have a reputation for  
being fussy, but in France, they’re an everyday 
food that even street vendors sell to be eaten on 
the go. Such versatility is part of the beauty of 
a crêpe. This super-thin pancake can be served 
sweet or savory, fancy or ordinary, for any meal at 
any time of the day.
Crêpes didn’t become a standard in my cook- 
ing repertoire back at home, however, until I 
had kids. I found that I could get my boys to eat 
all kinds of foods if presented in a crêpe. There’s 
something fun about eating food that comes in its 
own little package.
Crêpe fillings can be as complex as you want, 
but the batter is easier to put together than the 
simplest pancake mix. Crêpes also freeze well, 
so you can always have a stash on hand. Reheat-
ing them takes just a moment in a dry skillet. All 
this means that conjuring up Paris—or getting a 
finicky kid to eat—is only a quick crêpe away.

I love frozen yogurt and make it all the time in the summer. 
To keep it easy, I use good-quality purchased yogurt, though you can certainly 
use homemade if you’re so inclined. I don’t make frozen yogurt to save on calo-
ries, though it tends to have fewer than ice cream. And I don’t make it to save 
money, either. The reason I make it is simple. It’s just good: rich, creamy, and—
best of all—flavored just the way I like it. 
When making fro-yo, I use whole-milk Greek yogurt, which delivers an  
indulgently creamy mouth-feel. Sweeteners such as sugar, honey, and maple syrup 
not only temper yogurt’s telltale tang, but also inhibit ice crystals from forming  
during freezing. Other ingredients, such as extracts and fruit vinegar, boost flavor 
and refine texture. 
As with homemade ice cream, the possibilities for flavoring frozen yogurt are 
endless. I love the contrast that a strawberry swirl adds, as in the recipe on p. 89, 
or how loads of lemon make a fro-yo that—given it’s a dairy product—is unbe-
lievably refreshing. 
If you’ve ever made ice cream at home, you’ll enjoy how easy frozen yogurt 
is to make. That’s because there’s no need to get out a saucepan to make an egg 
custard, the starting point for most ice creams. (In fact, there’s no need for eggs at 
all.) Simply mix purchased yogurt with a few ingredients, and then churn them in 
your ice cream maker.  
That brings up an important point: You do need an ice cream maker to  
make frozen yogurt. A decent one will cost about $50, but once you try a spoon-
ful of one of these delicious frozen treats, you’ll know that it was definitely worth 
the investment.

black olive caramel
Makes 1¼   cups
combine 1½   cups granulated sugar and 2 tsp. water in a 1-quart 
heavy-duty saucepan. cook without stirring over low heat until 
the sugar begins to brown, about 8 minutes. Swirl the pan to even 
the color, and continue to cook until the caramel turns amber. 
Slowly and carefully add ¾   cup olive brine (from a jar of olives) 
and 25 pitted Kalamata olives. The sauce will harden at first; 
stir it with a wooden spoon until loose. Let cool until warm, about 
10 minutes. Transfer to a blender and blend until smooth. The 
caramel will keep, covered and refrigerated, for about 1 month.

asparagus, pasta, and even ice cream.                  —Joanne Smart

Lime leaves
Not all lime leaves are created equal. 
Leaves from the makrut lime tree 
(also sometimes known as kaﬃ  r lime 
leaves) are particularly fragrant and 
fl avorful. You might be most famil-
iar with them from their use in Th ai 
cuisine, where they infuse a citrusy 
fl avor in broths and soups, such as the 
classic tom yum, and also in curries 
and dressings.
Th e leaves of this particular lime 
plant contain more essential oils than 
do its fruit, making the leaves prized 
for cooking. Th ey have a pronounced 
fl oral-lime aroma but are very fi brous, 
so they’re commonly used whole 
to infuse fl avor to broths, soups, or 
sauces during cooking. Th ese whole 
leaves are not eaten and are most typi-
cally discarded before serving, much 
as you would do when cooking with 
bay leaves. However, if you remove 
the most fi brous veins from the back 
of fresh leaves, the remaining portion 
can be minced or cut into very thin 
slices and tossed directly into a dish. 
Used as a garnish, their deep green 
color and high-gloss sheen make a 
striking visual addition.
Lime leaves are available fresh and 
frozen (see Sources, p. 100).     —D.A.

It’s a snap
Sugar snap peas, tender and sweet, are one of the first delicacies 
of spring and are in season through the summer. To eat them 
raw or cooked, the fibrous flosslike string that runs the length 
of both sides of the pod has to be removed. Using a small par-
ing knife, snap off the tough stem end of the pod. At this cut 
end, you’ll find the strings. Grab hold using your thumb and 
the edge of your knife, and pull to remove.              —Ronne Day

Maryland blue crabs
Blue crabs have strikingly blue colored claws. Their 
Latin name, Callinectes sapidus, means “beauti-
ful swimmer that is savory” owing to their graceful 
beauty in the water and uncommonly sweet meat. 
In the United States, they can be found 
throughout the Atlantic and Gulf coasts. They 
are harvested as hard-shell and soft-shell crabs 
(immediately after molting). Many people boil the 
crabs, but most Marylanders prefer steaming be-
cause it imparts less moisture to the meat during 
cooking. Maryland crab season runs April through 
December with soft-shell crabs available mid-May 
through September. To get the biggest and fattest 
hard-shell crabs at the best prices, look for them 
in September and October.                                        —L.L.
