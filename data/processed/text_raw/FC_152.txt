Memorable Meals
I fi rst tasted burrata about eight years ago at an Italian 
restaurant in New York City. Nothing prepared me for 
its buttery, milky fl avor and oozing texture—a purse of 
mozzarella fi lled with shreds of mozzarella in cream 
(stracciatella). Paired with fresh heirloom tomatoes and 
fruity extra-virgin olive oil, the dish was the highlight 
of my meal; I was immediately captivated. Fortunately, 
I wasn’t alone in my adoration, as this once-obscure 
cheese with limited distribution outside of Italy has now 
become as ubiquitous in restaurants across the country as 
tattooed chefs.
I’ve learned that the key to enjoying truly great bur-
rata is eating it ultrafresh, and you can’t get it any fresher 
than when you make it yourself. So we were thrilled when 
Christine Clark, an educator at Murray’s Cheese Shop in 
New York City, off ered to show us (and you) how to make 
it at home (p. 50). It’s a fun and satisfying process, and the 
end result is not just delicious; it will also leave you feeling 
like a proud cheesemaker.
Another cooking teacher—renowned food writer Patri-
cia Wells—shows us a brilliant method for making french 
fries that, as she says, is foolproof (p. 40). Th e trick is to 
put the potatoes into cold oil and slowly bring the oil up 
to frying temperature. Fries made this way have a creamy 
interior and golden, crunchy exterior. Th is technique is 
shockingly successful and so easy that we agree: It’s fool-
proof. Just beware—the fries are also addictive!
On a sweet note, cookbook author and baking expert 
Abby Dodge takes us down memory lane with three classic 
versions of the American cream pie—banana, coconut, 
and chocolate—and then brings each into the present with 
a modern makeover (p. 76). Chocolate espresso, bananas 
Foster, or coconut caramel, anyone? You’ll be hard-pressed 
to bake just one.
In addition, our amazing editorial staff  shares their 
favorite go-to pasta dishes (p. 66), each packed with lots 
of fresh fl avor. And just in time for Easter or any spring 
celebration, food writer Tracey Seaman cooks up a menu

Between Harlem and Heaven
Afro-Asian-American Cooking for Big 
Nights, Weeknights, and Every Day
By JJ Johnson and Alexander Smalls with 
Veronica Chambers (Flatiron Books; $37.50)
On a quiet corner in Harlem, just a few blocks 
from my home, is a cozy restaurant and jazz 
club called Minton’s, where, until recently, the 
African diaspora met the Silk Road. The nightly 
menu, prepared by rising-star chef Joseph “JJ” 
Johnson, included such signature dishes as 
Udon Noodles with Edamame and West African 
Peanut Sauce, BBQ Brisket Egg Roll, and Collard 
Green Salad with Coconut Dressing. The neigh-
borhood mourned last summer when Johnson 
left the restaurant owned by his mentor, chef 
Alexander Smalls (a Grammy- and Tony-award 
winning opera singer). But now, home cooks 
across the nation can rejoice, because the Afro-
Asian-American comfort food that earned Min-
ton’s near-constant praise and glowing reviews 
is once again on offer in this one-of-a-kind book. 
All you’ve got to do, writes Johnson, is “dive in 
with an open heart and a hungry belly.” And if 
you’re anything like me, that hungry belly will 
interrupt your reading of the authors’ compel-
ling narratives and propel you into the kitchen to 
whip up something new.
Not to be missed: Collard Green Salad with 
Coconut Dressing. Hearty and nourishing, 
this dish is the essence of simplicity. Sweet-
ness and spice balance the greens’ natural 
bitterness. 
My Lisbon
A Cookbook from Portugal’s City of Light
By Nuno Mendes (Ten Speed Press; $35)
Hands down, this lavishly produced hardcover 
is the most beautiful book of the season. In this 
love letter to his hometown, Lisbon native and 
acclaimed chef Nuno Mendes takes readers on 
an insider’s tour of one of Europe’s last best-kept 
culinary secrets. In the oldest city in Western 
Europe, the rhythm of the day revolves around 
mealtimes. Hence, the chapters of this book 
follow the culinary clock. Pastries and coffee in 
the early morning. Salgados (savory snacks) a few 
hours later. At midday, there’s lunch, which even 
in this time-pressed era is a three-course affair 
of soup, fish or meat with rice and potatoes, and 
dessert; and in late afternoon, friends meet up to 
share petiscos, little plates of this and that (e.g., 
Tomato and Strawberry Salad, Garlic and Turnip 
Green Rice with Piri Piri Oil, Marinated Mushrooms 
with Bacon). Dinner never begins until eight o’clock 
and always includes dessert. And if you have the 
stamina, you might also nab a late-night sande, 
or sandwich. Although it’s hard to imagine being 
hungry after all that, the pages of this book—where 
evocative stories about café culture, beach life, 
and myriad other exciting discoveries are inter-
spersed among tempting recipes and luminous 
photographs—are likely to leave you hungry for 
one more thing: a taste of the real Lisbon. Soon. 
Humble but lovely: Potatoes with Caramel-
ized Onions and Melting Cheese. Turn to 
p. 242 to discover why these savory smashed 
potatoes are a perennial Portuguese favorite.

Tasting Paris
100 Recipes to Eat Like a Local
By Clotilde Dusoulier (Clarkson Potter; $30)
Montmarte denizen Clotilde Dusoulier knows 
Parisian food. She is the author of such titles 
as The French Market Cookbook and Clotilde’s 
Edible Adventures and the creator of the award-
winning blog Chocolate and Zucchini, in which 
she shares recipes from her own kitchen. She 
is also a remarkably graceful writer, effortlessly 
transporting readers into the heart of Paris. So 
it’s no surprise that reading her new book makes 
you feel like you’re spending the tastiest day 
ever in this enchanting city, exploring its vibrant 
open-air markets and discovering its best street 
foods with a dear friend. The recipes she offers 
range from iconic bistro and brasserie fare such 
as classic Croque-Madame, Quiche Lorraine, 
and Mousse au Chocolat to unexpected dishes,

Cream of Fiddlehead Soup
Sauté fiddleheads in butter and olive oil until crisp-tender. Add 
minced garlic, and cook for 30 seconds. Season to taste with salt 
and pepper, then transfer to a blender, add chicken broth, and 
purée until smooth. Transfer to a saucepan, and bring to a sim-
mer. Serve with crème fraîche and crisp prosciutto.  —Ryan Skelton
Morels with Asparagus 
and Quinoa
Cook quinoa with slivered toasted 
almonds and raisins according 
to package directions. Cut fresh 
morels and asparagus into bite-
size chunks, sauté in olive oil until 
tender and browned, and finish 
with sea salt and balsamic vinegar. 
Serve over the quinoa. Sprinkle 
with chopped fresh rosemary 
to taste.                            —Tanya Steel
Linguine with Sorrel,
Ricotta, and Smoked 
Mozzarella
Sauté sorrel in olive oil until just 
wilted, then add chopped garlic 
and a pinch of ground coriander. 
Season to taste with kosher salt 
and freshly ground black pepper. 
Toss with freshly cooked linguine, 
herbed ricotta, and shredded 
smoked mozzarella. Top with 
finely chopped fresh parsley.
—Ronne Day
Bok Choy
Green Smoothie
In a blender, combine 1 large 
banana, 1 chopped green apple, 
2 chopped stalks bok choy, a dash 
of finely grated fresh ginger, a few 
raw cashews, and a handful of ice 
cubes. Purée until smooth. Thin 
with orange juice as needed.
—Lisa Lahey 
Strawberry, Ginger & 
Lemon Compote
Combine hulled, chopped straw-
berries, finely grated fresh gin-
ger, lemon zest, and lemon juice 
in a small saucepan. Cook until 
thickened. Serve over biscuits or 
ice cream.               —Will Stewart
Artichoke Frittata 
Slice 2 large fresh artichoke bot-
toms paper thin. Toss with eggs, 
Parmigiano-Reggiano, diced 
onion, chopped fresh parsley, 
granulated garlic, salt, pepper, 
and a pinch of crushed red pep-
per flakes. Transfer to an oiled 
shallow baking dish, top with 
seasoned breadcrumbs, and 
bake until cooked through and 
golden brown.    —Diana Andrews
Mango-Black 
Bean Salad 
In a small skillet over low heat, 
warm olive oil, then add cumin, 
mild chile powder, and thinly 
sliced scallion whites. Heat until 
the spices smell fragrant and the 
scallions soften, then set aside. 
In a medium bowl, combine one 
15-oz. drained, rinsed can black 
beans, diced mango, thawed fro-
zen corn, diced sweet red pepper, 
and thinly sliced scallion greens. 
Toss with the spiced oil and fresh 
lime juice; serve at room 
temperature.            —Chris Hoelck

curried yogurt potato salad
Serves 4
Cut 1¼   lb. baby Yukon Gold potatoes to the 
same size (in halves or quarters), and boil in 
well-salted water until just tender, about 
10 minutes. Drain and rinse under cold 
water. Meanwhile, cut 2 medium carrots
into matchsticks, slice 2 Persian cucum-
bers into thin half-moons, and finely dice 
one-half of a sweet onion. Heat 1 Tbs. olive oil
in a small skillet over medium heat. Add 1 Tbs. 
grated fresh ginger and ¾ tsp. curry powder and cook, stir-
ring, until fragrant, about 1 minute; transfer to a large bowl. To the bowl, 
whisk in ¾ cup plain whole-milk Greek yogurt, ½   tsp. finely grated lime 
zest, 1 tsp. fresh lime juice, and 1½ tsp. kosher salt. Add the carrots, 
cucumbers, and onion, and toss well, then add the potatoes and toss 
gently. Season to taste with salt and freshly ground black pepper, and 
garnish with additional lime zest, if you like.                       —William Stewart
greens with oranges and sesame-ginger dressing

roasted asparagus with almond-thyme crumbs
Serves 4
Heat the oven to 475°F. Finely chop 2 Tbs.

Pancakes
Why do some rise to the occasion 
while others fall flat? Read on for the 
secrets to perfect pancakes.
BY DAVI D JOACH IM 
AND ANDREW SCHLOSS
FOOD SCIENCE
What are pancakes?
Th ese fl at, round quick breads or cakes are made from a 
batter of fl our, liquid (oft en milk), eggs, and fat (oft en melted 
butter) and are cooked in a pan on a stovetop. Th ough diff er-
ent types of pancakes are enjoyed the world over, here we’ll 
focus on the American breakfast staple. Made from a leav-
ened batter (see  p. 32), this pancake is fl uff y, not fl at; tender, 
not chewy; and lightly sweet, with a touch of richness.
Most American pancakes are 
leavened with baking soda or 
baking powder, although some 
get a rise from yeast or whipped 
egg whites.
Baking soda is sodium bicar-
bonate, a natural alkali. When dis-
solved in a liquid and combined 
with an acid (such as that found 
in buttermilk or yogurt), the alkali 
undergoes a chemical reaction 
that produces carbon dioxide, 
a gas that becomes trapped as 
bubbles in the batter. Yeast and 
whipped egg whites can also be 
used to introduce bubbles into 
pancake batter. Either way, when 
heated, the gas trapped in these 
bubbles expands and lift s the 
batter as it heats during cooking, 
creating a fl uff y pancake. 
Baking powder is essen-
tially baking soda that includes a 
powdered acid, usually sodium 
aluminum sulfate. It doesn’t 
require additional acid to 
react, so you can use plain milk 
instead of acidic buttermilk in 
pancake batter. Most baking 
powder is double-acting, which 
means carbon dioxide is released 
twice—once when mixed with 
liquid and then again when the 
batter is heated in the pan.
Acid + sodium 
= leavening 
gases (carbon 
dioxide)
Get a lif 
Sweet + salt
Sugar adds sweetness to pancakes. It also melts when heated 
and attracts water, helping to make pancakes moist. As it melts, 
it enhances browning, breaking down into small molecules 
that react to each other to create toasty, buttery, caramelized 
fl avors. Sugar can also help tenderize pancakes when it makes 
up at least 10% of the overall weight of the batter. In that case, 
the sugar competes 
with water for the 
fl our’s protein bonds, 
weakening the gluten 
structure.
Salt balances the 
sweetness of pan-
cakes. It also increases 
the crosslinking of 
gluten proteins in the 
batter, creating pan-
cakes with a fi rmer 
structure.

Liquid assets
Although the most common liquid used in pancake 
batter is milk, it can be almost anything: water, butter-
milk, or even beer. The important aspect is the ratio 
of liquid to dry ingredients. By weight, pancake batter 
contains roughly twice as much liquid as flour. The liquid 
disperses the flour’s gluten protein strands so widely 
that they can form only loose connections. Since strong 
gluten connections make cooked batters chewy and 
elastic, the high ratio of liquid helps to ensure tender, 
moist pancakes. 
Replacing milk with buttermilk adds acid to the batter, 
which further weakens the gluten structure, making but-
termilk pancakes finer-textured and even more tender. 
Pro tip: If you replace milk with buttermilk, replace 
one-third of the baking powder in the recipe with baking 
soda to increase the leavening alkaline content and fully 
neutralize the increased acid in the batter. 
Mixing flour and water 
(or milk) forms a matrix 
of interwoven strands of 
gluten proteins that traps 
leavening gases and 
allows the batter to rise.
Twice as much liquid as flour disperses the gluten strands, 
loosening the gluten matrix and yielding soft pancakes.
Flour adds structure
American pancakes are typically made with 
all-purpose fl our, which has a protein content of 
about 10 percent. When mixed with liquids, these 
proteins begin to unravel and connect, creating a 
viscous network. Gluten gives pancake batter its 
strength and elasticity as it puff s during cooking.
Heat your cooking surface over medium-
high heat (cast iron, which holds constant 
heat well, is best). When ready, a splash of 
water should bubble up and dance on the 
hot surface. If your cooking surface is too 
cool, the pancakes will spread more, making 
them thin, and the pancakes won’t brown or 
rise well, since the air bubbles will expand 
and pop before the batter sets. Conversely, 
an overheated pan will burn the surface of 
the pancakes before the batter sets in the 
interior, leaving a gooey or even raw center.
Th e heated cooking surface should be 
greased with butter, oil, or shortening. Butter 
will brown best because it contains protein 
as well as fat. You’ll need only a thin glaze of 
liquefi ed fat—just enough to act as a vehicle 
for heat transference and to fi ll the micro-
scopic openings in the metal that can trap 
batter and cause it to stick. Too much fat will 
fry the surface of the pancakes and make 
them extra crisp. 
When pancake batter hits a hot, greased 
cooking surface, moisture steams off  the batter, 
and proteins coagulate, setting the bottom of 
the pancake. As heat is conducted through the 
batter, the leavening gases expand and rise. 
When the top is full of unpopped bubbles, the 
pancake has set about halfway through and is 
ready to be fl ipped. Aft er it’s turned, cook for 
about half the time you cooked the pancake on 
its fi rst side. At that point, the starch granules 
in the fl our will have soft ened, absorbed the 
liquid, swelled, and thickened, transforming 
the fl uid batter to a solid pancake. Serve im-
mediately, since the loose gluten structure of 
pancakes is not strong enough to keep them 
fl uff y for more than a few minutes. As they 
cool, they lose air and become dense.

Better with butter
The fat in butter adds 
richness and helps to 
keep pancakes tender. 
Melted fat inhibits the 
ability of flour to absorb 
liquid, thereby limiting 
the flour’s proteins from 
bonding, which could 
make the pancakes 
chewy. Other fats such 
as oil have a similar 
effect in pancakes, but 
butter provides a more 
complex flavor.

MIX IT UP
Mixing liquid into dry ingre-
dients (rather than the other 
way around) discourages 
lumps. When you mix the liq-
uid into the dry ingredients, 
the liquid is dispersed more 
evenly, and by controlling 
the speed at which you add 
the liquid (slower is better), 
you can keep the batter thick, 
breaking up lumps as you go. 
Don’t obsess about getting 
rid of all the lumps. Excessive 
mixing encourages gluten 
development, which will 
make for tougher pancakes. It 
can also interfere with leaven-
ing. A lightly mixed batter 
will create just enough gluten 
elasticity to give structure to 
the carbon dioxide bubbles 
in the batter, allowing them 
to expand during cooking; 
an overmixed batter will 
interfere with rising by creat-
ing too many small pockets of 
leavening gases. Rather than 
attacking the lumps with vig-
orous mixing, use a whisk and 
gentle strokes, concentrating 
on the lumps as you add the 
liquid. If a few lumps remain, 
don’t worry—they’ll soft en as 
the batter rests. 
Letting the batter rest for 
15 minutes before cooking not 
only allows the fl our to fully 
hydrate, minimizing lumps 
and creating a smoother, more 
uniform texture, but also gives 
any gluten developed during 
mixing time to relax, making 
the pancakes less chewy. 
PRO TIP: When resting pan-
cake batter for more than two 
hours, withhold the leavener 
(baking powder, baking soda, 
and/or egg whites); whisk it 
in within 30 minutes of cook-
ing the pancakes to maximize 
its leavening power.
The number of air 
bubbles directly 
affects the quality of 
the finished pancake. 
New air bubbles do 
not form when cook-
ing the batter. Exist-
ing bubbles simply 
expand when the 
batter is heated.

One of France’s greatest chefs, Guy Savoy was 
an early advocate of nouvelle cuisine, which

beautifully presented. He opened his eponymous

Spring Fling
Spring is a joyful brihge from 
winter to summer, when bright 
green chives spring from the ground 
and we anticipate delicious produce to 
come. Tiny fl owers pop on strawberry 
runners, and pink radishes and baby 
salad greens can be plucked from 
the earth. Daydreaming about mild, 
warm April days inspired me to create 
a menu that takes full advantage of 
spring’s post-winter bounty.
My celebration menu begins with 
a pink and green salad of lettuce, 
strawberries, radishes, and chives 
with a slightly sweet clementine 
dressing, enriched with honey and 
a splash of balsamic vinegar. Fresh 
strawberries enhance the fl avor.
Th e focal point is a classic spiral 
ham, but I’ve taken it up a notch by 
using an uncured cut and coat-
ing it with a simple glaze of grainy 
mustard, herbes de Provence, and 
apricot preserves. 
To accompany the ham, small 
golden potatoes are cooked just until 
tender, peeled, quartered, and then 
tossed with luscious young onions 
in a velvety cream sauce. Also on the 
side is rainbow chard tossed with 
tender shallots, then baked under 
Parmigiano-coated baguette slices. 
To wrap up the meal in true 
spring fashion, I turn to rhubarb. 
Th is tangy, blushing beauty is 
showcased in an updated version 
of a blitz torte: layers of tender 
golden cake topped with billowy 
meringue and crunchy almonds. 
My cake originated from the 1940s 
paperback Th e Victory Binding of 
the American Woman’s Cook Book.
Instead of sandwiching with the 
traditional blitz torte fi lling of cus-
tard or cream and fresh berries, I 
go with a jamlike strawberry-
rhubarb fi lling.
Th is menu signals the freshness 
and beauty of spring and of all the 
delicious meals to come. 
Cookbook author Tracey Seaman has 
worked in test kitchens from Food & 
Wine to Martha Stewart Living; there’s 
never been a recipe she couldn’t improve 
upon or create.
The season’s best ingredients—from asparagus 
to strawberries—star in this delicious menu.

On any given hay at Murray’s Cheese Shop in 
New York City’s Greenwich Village, you’ll finh 
some  hifferent cheeses, reahy for sampling. 
As someone who has introduced countless customers to 
myriad mind-blowing varieties, I can say with confidence 
that one in particular steals everyone’s heart: burrata. And 
for good reason—silky, milky, and wonderful, burrata is like 
the best fresh mozzarella you’ve ever had, only better. 
Although burrata is not mozzarella, it’s made from mozza-
rella. More precisely, burrata is a supple pouch of tender moz-
zarella stuffed with stracciatella, a luscious blend of fresh cream 
and soft mozzarella shreds.
Invented in southern Italy, burrata began as a frugal way for 
cheesemakers to use mozzarella scraps. But the concoction was 
so wonderful, it promptly became a delicacy in its own right. 
The only trouble is that the cheese’s exquisiteness is ephemeral. 
Burrata is meant to be eaten within hours, if not immediately, 
and it’s certainly past its prime after 48. This is the best argu-
ment I can put forth for learning to make it. Your own burrata 
will be fresher, more delicious, and—ultimately—less expensive 
than any you can buy in a store.
For all its allure, burrata is among the simplest of cheeses. 
If there’s one thing I want you to hold in mind, it’s this: Yes, 
making it involves lots of little steps, but this is not a monu-
mental undertaking by any means. The entire process will 
take less than one hour. (And a mere 15 minutes if you don’t 
make your own curds!)

Did you know?
Not so long ago, burrata was  
available only in the southern Italian  
region of Puglia. Although hardly common- 
place today, the popular cheese is increasingly 
easy to find on restaurant menus and in stores.  
Traditionally, burrata was wrapped in leaves  
of asphodel, a leek relative, moistened  
with whey. While the leaves were green, the  
cheese was fresh; once they’d begun to dry,  
it was no good. Nowadays, it’s common  
for the cheese to come in a  
plastic bag or container.

Whether snow, snap, or English, this vegetable 
adds verdant color, sweet flavor, and loads of

When fresh peas start showing up at my local 
market, it means I can finally stow my heavy wool sweat-
ers and get ready for spring. These harbingers of warmer 
weather reflect the coming season in their color as well as in 
their sweetness.
English, snap, and snow. Classified as “shelling peas” 
because they’re shucked from their fibrous pods before they’re 
eaten, tender English (aka garden) peas may be the first kind 
of pea that comes to mind. Bright-green sugar snap peas and 
lighter colored Chinese snow peas are known as “pod peas” 
because they’re housed in edible casings; the French call them 
mangetout, for “eat everything.” Though the aforementioned 
types of peas can sometimes be used interchangeably, there are 
a few uses that each is best suited for. 
English peas are perfect for soups and are classic addi-
tions to pastas as well as pilafs, paellas, risottos, and other rice 
dishes. Sugar snaps, which are also fabulous raw, make a great 
side dish when cooked for a few minutes in well-salted boil-
ing water or gently sautéed in butter or olive oil with shallots 
or garlic and a splash of water. Good on their own, they’re 
even better tossed with a pinch or two of ground or crushed 
toasted spices (think coriander, cumin, red pepper flakes, and 
more), a dash of citrus zest, and/or chopped toasted almonds 
or other nuts. Snow peas are best used raw or cooked briefly, 
which is why they work so well in salads, stir-fries, and sautés.
With these different kinds of peas and so many ways to 
enjoy them, a pea devotee can easily cook her way through the 
season with nary a repeated dish: Toss peas in a pan with some 
smoky bacon to make a great side dish (p. 62) for skillet-cooked 
salmon or crisp-skinned chicken, or purée them into a silky 
soup that’s not only lightning fast to prepare but also delicious 
warm or chilled (p. 60). Peas go well with all kinds of tender 
fresh herbs, especially tarragon and chives, and they won’t over-
whelm subtly flavored cheeses, such as the ricotta and burrata 
featured in the recipes that follow.

Choose evenly colored, shiny, 
firm pods. Snow peas are at their 
peak when blemish-free, sturdy, and 
filled with barely visible peas. The shells 
of sugar snaps and English varieties 
should be filled with small to medium-
size peas. Avoid overly large or bulging 
pods, which indicate tough, starchy 
peas as well as noticeably lightweight 
pods, which yield immature peas with 
little flavor. To try a fun test, gently 
press on podded English peas so that 
the peas inside rub against one another; 
if you hear a squeak, the peas are fresh. 
All peas are best eaten as close as 
possible to the time they’re picked. If 
you don’t plan to eat them on the day 
you buy them, refrigerate them in a 
tightly sealed plastic bag.
Easy-peasy prep tips. Before cook-
ing sugar snap peas, pull back the stem 
sides to unzip the strings, which will 
be present along one or both of the 
peas’ lengths; discard the strings. Use 
sugar snaps whole or sliced. To remove 
English peas from their pods, remove 
the strings, and then use your thumb 
to push the fresh peas from their pods. 
(Empty English-pea pods can be saved 
for making vegetable stocks; they keep 
well frozen for several months and  
don’t need to be thawed before using.) 
Snow peas simply require their ends 
trimmed, and then are good to go, 
whole, chopped, or sliced. 
 Although nothing beats the sweetness 
and snap of freshly picked peas, frozen 
peas are great for perking up post-season 
dishes. They’re especially great in soups, 
mashes, sautés, pestos, and dips. That 
said, I almost never choose frozen over 
fresh once spring has sprung, the grass  
is green, and my wool sweaters are but  
a memory.

 I use both sprouts and

This cut is juicier and tastier 
than breasts—and it’s easier  
on the wallet, too.
BY JOANNE SMART
THE FIRST TIME I SWOONED 
OVER A CHICKEN THIGH, I was 
at The Purple Pig in Chicago. The res-
taurant’s name makes it obvious that it 
specializes in pork dishes, but someone 
at our communal table recommended 
the chicken-thigh skewers served with 
tzatziki and fried smashed potatoes. As 
promised, they were a standout—pieces 
of boneless, skin-on chicken fried to 
crisp perfection. What made them so 
good was the choice of the thigh.
As someone who used to mindlessly 
reach for skinless, boneless breasts when 
it came to cooking chicken for dinner, I 
became a fan of the thigh, and now I  
use it for most of my chicken dishes. 
Thinner than the breast, the more 
deeply flavored dark cut provides a 
perfect ratio of skin to meat, and it stays 
tender and moist even when cooked 
over high heat. Aside from having more 
flavor than breast meat, thighs are also 
cheaper—a bonus. 
Here, I’ve collected a few of my favor-
ite chicken thigh recipes and included 
why the thigh beats the breast. To try 
to replicate those Purple Pig skewers, I 
painstakingly boned some chicken thighs 
but left the skin on. While the result was 
tasty, removing the bones from chicken 
thighs was a thankless task. I decided  
instead to roast bone-in thighs in very 
high heat  
until the skin  
is crackly, and the 
meat, rubbed with lots of 
garlic and some paprika, falls off 
the bone anyway. Yum. 
Another great way to use chicken 
thighs is in soup. The recipe on p. 72 
is loosely based on one by Boston chef 
Barbara Lynch. She uses a whole bird 
for hers, but thighs are easier to maneu-
ver in the pot. On their own, they add 
plenty of body and rich flavor. After 
long cooking, thighs, unlike breasts, 
retain flavor, so the meat is delicious 
shredded and used in the soup. 
I’ve seen recipes that say to use 
chicken breast because it’s so bland 
that it acts as a canvas for other flavors. 
With thighs, though, you can always 
taste true chicken flavor, even in highly 
spiced dishes like the saag on p. 73 or 
the spicy chicken on p. 74. So the next 
time you’re thinking of chicken for din-
ner, reach past those pricey breasts and 
grab the more humble thighs; your dish 
may just be better for it. 
Joanne Smart is a contributing editor  
to Fine Cooking.
How to choose
Though I’ll cheer the day when supermar-
kets sell boneless, skin-on chicken thighs, 
they now come basically two ways: with skin 
and bone intact or without.
Boneless thighs cook up quickly and can 
be used in just about any recipe calling for 
chicken breasts. They usually need very 
little in the way of trimming. If searing bone-
less thighs, they will look best if you start 
them smooth side down. As with breasts, 
they can range in size from brand to brand, 
so know that the number of thighs in pack-
ages of the same weight may vary. 
Bone-in thighs take more time to cook. 
Some people like to trim away excess skin 
before roasting, but not me; those bits that 
stick to the pan are a treat. 
As noted elsewhere, you can also bone 
thighs yourself; for the best method to do 
that, see p. 92.

B Y A B I G A I L J O H N S O N D O D G E
Who can resist the old-fashioned lusciousness of a silky 
cream pie? I’ve been eyeballing the cream pies displayed in the glass enclo-
sures at diners for as long as I can remember (and that’s a lot of years and a ton 
of diners). Th ere they sit, tempting me with whipped cream piled a mile high 
on top of thick layers of pudding. Th ey look larger than life and amazing, but all 
too oft en with one bite, the reality falls well short of expectation. Th ese disap-
pointments have shaped my baking maxim: Every recipe I create should taste 
as delicious as it looks.
What follows are my versions 
of three diner-style cream pies: 
banana, chocolate, and coconut. 
Each one features a deeply fl avored, 
creamy pudding spooned into an 
easy-to-make, tender pie crust and 
topped with lightly sweetened, bil-
lowy whipped cream.
From there, I gave each a modern 
update. My goal wasn’t to change 
the fl avors but to intensify and 
elevate them. Th e Southern dessert 
bananas Foster inspired the banana 
pie update that features a layer 
of rum-spiked sautéed bananas. 
Chocolate cream becomes even 
more decadent with bittersweet 
ganache and an espresso-fl avored 
mascarpone topping. For the 
coconut, I reduced coconut milk 
to a thick, more intensely fl avored 
cream and paired it with a layer of 
salted caramel. 
Between the fl aky crust, rich fi ll-
ings, and creamy whipped toppings, 
each of these pies will make you 
swoon at fi rst glance, and their rich 
fl avors and velvety textures will elicit 
delight with every bite. Even better, 
there’s no need for an excursion to 
the diner to enjoy them.

Notes for success
  When preparing the fillings, make sure the

  While cooking the filling, whisk the entire

MAKE THE INFUSED MILK
Cut the peeled bananas into ½- to ¾-inch 
pieces and put them in a medium saucepan 
along with the milk. Bring to a simmer over 
medium heat, stirring frequently.
Slide the pan off the heat, cover, and let the 
mixture steep for at least 20 minutes or up 
to 2 hours. (The more bananas used and the 
longer the steeping, the more pronounced 
the banana flavor will be.)
Arrange a fine-mesh sieve over a 4-cup 
measure and pour the banana-milk mixture into 
the sieve. Using a silicone spatula, gently press 
on the banana pieces to squeeze out the milk. 
Scrape the underside of the sieve, add to the 
milk, and discard the banana pieces. You should 
have 2 23 cups. If you have less, add more milk
and if you have more, pour off some of the 
milk (save it for your morning cereal). Wipe the 
saucepan dry—no need to wash.
MAKE THE BANANA LAYER
Cut the peeled bananas lengthwise in half, and 
then cut crosswise into ½- to ¾-inch pieces. Put 
the butter in a medium skillet, and cook over 
medium heat until bubbling. Add the brown sugar 
and stir until dissolved. Add the banana pieces, 
and sprinkle with the rum and salt. Increase the 
heat to medium high and cook, using a silicone 
spatula to turn and gently stir, until the bananas 
are soft and the juices are reduced and thick, 5 to 
6 minutes. Slide the pan from the heat and set 
aside, stirring occasionally, until completely cool. 
(For faster cooling, scrape into a bowl and set the 
bowl over a larger bowl filled with ice, stirring and 
scraping the sides frequently until chilled.) 
MAKE THE FILLING 
Scrape the cooled banana mixture into the 
baked and cooled piecrust and spread evenly.
Put the sugar, cornstarch, and salt in the 
saucepan and whisk until well blended. Add 
23 cup of the infused milk and whisk until the mix-
ture is smooth. Whisk in the egg yolks (or whole 
eggs, if using), and then the remaining milk.
Set the pan over medium-high heat and cook, 
stirring with a whisk, until the mixture comes to a 
full boil. Reduce the heat to medium and, whisk-
ing constantly, boil for 1 minute, then slide the pan 
off the heat. Add the butter and vanilla, and stir 
until the butter melts and the pudding is smooth. 
Scrape the filling into the piecrust on top 
of the banana layer, spread evenly, and press 
a piece of plastic wrap directly on the surface 
to prevent a skin from forming, if you like.  
Refrigerate until chilled and firm, about 
5 hours. 
WHIP THE TOPPING 
Put the heavy cream, mascarpone, brown 
sugar, rum, and salt in the bowl of a stand  
mixer fitted with the whisk attachment. Beat 
on low speed until smooth. Increase the 
speed to medium high, and beat until the 
cream is thick and holds medium-firm peaks. 
Peel off the plastic from the filling. Scrape 
the topping onto the center of the pie and, 
using a small offset or silicone spatula, spread 
it to cover the filling, leaving the center slightly 
domed along with lots of swirls and peaks. 
Arrange banana slices on top of the whipped 
cream, if you like. Serve immediately or cover 
loosely and refrigerate for up to 8 hours.

The reduced coconut milk and caramel 
toppings can be made ahead, cooled, 
and refrigerated (two days for the 
coconut milk and up to a week for the 
caramel).  Serves 10 to 12
 
1   fully baked and cooled All-Butter 
Piecrust (see recipe p. 78)
FOR THE CONDENSED COCONUT MILK
 
2  cans (13½   oz. each) coconut milk 
 
1  cup whole milk
FOR THE CARAMEL
 
½    cup heavy cream
 
5   Tbs. (2½   oz.) unsalted butter, cut into 
10 small pieces, at room temperature
 1½    cups (10½   oz.) granulated sugar
 
½    cup water
 
¼    tsp. table salt
FOR THE FILLING 
 
½    cup (3½   oz.) granulated sugar 
 
¼    cup (1 18 oz.) cornstarch, sifted
 
¼    tsp. table salt
 
3   cups condensed coconut milk 
(see above)
 
4  large egg yolks (or 2 large eggs)
 
3   Tbs. (1½   oz.) unsalted butter, cut into 
8 small pieces, at room temperature 
 1½    tsp. pure vanilla extract
 
½     cup (1¼   oz.) sweetened shredded 
coconut, toasted (see p. 88)
FOR THE TOPPING
 
1  cup heavy cream, chilled 
 
23  cup (5 38 oz.) mascarpone 
 
13   cup (1 38 oz.) confectioners’ 
sugar, sifted; more to taste
 1½   tsp. pure vanilla extract
 
 Pinch table salt 
 
  Shredded or flaked coconut, 
toasted, for garnish
 
 Reserved caramel, for garnish 
MAKE THE CONDENSED COCONUT MILK
Vigorously shake the coconut milk cans before 
opening, then pour into a large saucepan, and 
bring to a boil over medium heat. Boil, stirring 
the side and bottom of the pan frequently, 
especially toward the end of cooking, until 
reduced to 2 cups, 12 to 18 minutes. Stir in the 
whole milk to bring the mixture to 3 cups. Set 
aside until warm or cool completely, cover, and 
refrigerate up to 2 days before proceeding with 
the recipe.
MAKE THE CARAMEL
Put the heavy cream in a small saucepan and 
heat until very warm. Keep the warm cream 
nearby along with the butter. 
Put the sugar and water in a large, heavy 
saucepan. Cook, stirring gently, over low heat 
until the sugar dissolves, about 5 minutes. 
Stop stirring, increase the heat to high, and 
bring to a boil. Cook, without stirring, until the 
sugar begins to color around the edges of the 
pan, about 5 minutes. Swirl the pan over the 
heat until the caramel is an even deep amber 
color, another 1 to 2 minutes. (Test it by put-
ting a drop on a white plate. If the caramel 
is too light, the sauce will be too sweet. 
See p. 90 for more on caramel.) 
Slide the pan off the heat, 
and slowly add the warm 
heavy cream, butter, and 
salt. (Be careful—it will 
sputter, and the steam 
is hot.) Whisk until the 
caramel is smooth. If 
necessary, return the 
pan to the heat and 
continue whisking 
until the caramel 
dissolves, but do not 
boil. Set aside until 
slightly cooled, 15 to 
20 minutes. (For faster 
cooling, set the bowl over 
a larger bowl filled with ice and a little water, 
stirring and scraping the sides frequently.)
Measure out ¾   cup of the caramel, and 
pour into the bottom of the baked and cooled 
piecrust. Spread evenly. Freeze the caramel-
filled pie plate while making the filling. Cover 
and refrigerate the remaining caramel until 
ready to serve.
MAKE THE FILLING 
Put the sugar, cornstarch, and salt in a 
medium saucepan and whisk until well 
blended. Add 23 cup of the condensed 
coconut milk, and whisk until the mixture is 
smooth. Whisk in the egg yolks (or whole eggs, 
if using), and then the remaining condensed 
coconut milk.
Set the pan over medium-high heat, and 
cook, stirring with a whisk, until the mixture 
comes to a full boil. Reduce the heat to 
medium and, whisking constantly, boil for 
1 minute, then slide the pan off the heat. Add 
the butter and vanilla, and stir until the butter 
is melted and the pudding is smooth. Stir 
in the toasted coconut. Pour into a bowl or 
4-cup measure, and press a piece of plastic 
wrap directly on the surface to prevent a 
skin from forming. Refrigerate until no longer 
hot (the pudding will still feel warm), 20 to 
30 minutes.
Pour the mixture into the piecrust over the 
caramel, spread evenly, and press a piece of 
plastic wrap directly on the surface to prevent 
a skin from forming, if you like. Refrigerate 
until chilled and firm, about 5 hours. 
WHIP THE TOPPING
Put the heavy cream, mascarpone, confec-
tioners’ sugar, vanilla, and salt in the bowl of a 
stand mixer fitted with the whisk attachment, 
and beat on low speed until smooth. Add con-
fectioners’ sugar to taste. Increase the speed 
to medium high, and beat until the cream is 
thick and holds medium-firm peaks. 
Peel off the plastic from the filling. Scrape 
the topping onto the center of the pie and, 
using a small offset or silicone spatula, spread 
it to cover the filling, leaving the center slightly 
domed along with lots of swirls and peaks. 
Drizzle with some of the reserved caramel 
(slightly reheated) and scatter with toasted 
coconut. Serve immediately with the remain-
ing caramel.

Cured vs. 
uncured ham
Spiral-cut hams are skinned and 
cooked, usually coated with a sweet 
glaze, and vacuum-packed in plastic 
wrap. The spiral-cut ham for the 
spring menu on p. 42 came out 
delicious when we tested with both 
cured and uncured hams, but the 
uncured ham that author Tracey 
Seaman favors was a bit more 
succulent and flavorful. 
UNCURED HAMS are also 
called fresh hams. They’re the 
same cut as cured, but haven’t 
been soaked in a curing solution or 
smoked. They’re usually light pink or 
even grayish, as you would expect in 
uncooked meat.
CURED HAMS are typically 
brighter pink and either soaked in or 
injected with a brine.                              
—Lisa Lahey

Maple syrup always

Th e old grading system for maple syrup used an A, 
B, and C classifi cation system. Th e new system, 
designed to highlight the qualities of diff erent maple 
syrups, does away with the B and C grades, which 
gave the impression that syrups receiving those 
grades were of a lesser quality.
Now, to show that the diff erences are not of quality 
but of color and taste, there are four classifi cations all 
under the Grade A umbrella.                 —L.L.
INGREDIENT
As the editorial director of Fine Cooking, I’m frequently asked for cooking 
advice. I’m happy to engage in these conversations because sometimes 
I learn a few things, too (who knew you could cook bacon in a waﬄ  e 
iron?). Many of the questions I hear revolve around cooking basics, and 
my responses are mostly the same. With this in mind, I’ve devised these 
fi ve rules I think every cook, regardless of skill level, should practice. If 
you start here, I believe your success rate in the kitchen could improve at 
best, and at the very least, you’ll enjoy cooking more.
1. Start with the best ingredi-
ents possible. Will this make 
your food taste better? Defi -
nitely. Great cooks produce 
fabulous meals because they 
use quality ingredients. Sure, a 
good recipe can produce good 
results, but your dish will taste 
only as good as the ingredients 
that go into it. So get rid of 
old, expired spices; frequent 
your local farmers’ market; 
fi nd a good butcher and fi sh-
monger; buy good olive oil. 
You’ll taste a diff erence. 
2. Read the entire recipe 
before you begin. Th is 
may sound obvious, but even 
seasoned cooks will jump into 
a recipe without reading it fi rst 
(yep, I’ve been guilty, too), 
only to discover midway that 
they’re missing a key ingredi-
ent or that something needs 
to marinate for several hours. 
Th ese types of surprises can 
quickly derail your cooking 
plan but are easily avoided by 
reading the recipe ahead. 
3. Prep ingredients ahead of 
time. Mise en place is a French 
term that means “everything in 
its place.” Th is is how profes-
sional kitchens run, and for 
good reason. Getting all your 
         ingredients prepped and 
         gathered in one place before 
you start cooking cuts down on 
mistakes, creates effi  ciency, and 
reduces stress. As one culinary 
instructor explained it to me, 
“Prep the dish until you can’t 
prep it anymore.”
4. Invest in a good chef’s 
knife. A chef’s knife is the 
most indispensable tool in the 
home kitchen. It’s the knife 
you’ll use most to chop, dice, 
slice, mince, smash, carve, and 
more, so it’s worth it to buy a 
good one. Keeping it sharp is 
also important, not only for 
ease of use, but also because a 
sharp blade is safer than a dull 
one. Compensating for a dull 
blade by adding extra force can 
be dangerous. 
5. Keep on cooking. You can’t 
become a good cook without 
some fl ops along the way. If 
your dish fails, don’t despair; 
consider it a learning experi-
ence on the path to being a 
better cook. Failures happen to 
everyone, even accomplished 
cooks, so don’t give up—and 
remember, takeout is just a 
phone call away. 
—Kathy Kingsley

RECIPE
fava bean dip
The season for fava beans is short. Even then, they can 
be difficult to find, but their flavor is worth seeking out. 
If you can’t find them, frozen edamame make a fair 
substitution in this recipe. Makes about 4 cups
 
1 cup plus 1 Tbs. extra-virgin olive oil
 
1 small yellow onion, finely chopped
 
2 large cloves garlic, minced
 
1 cup vegetable stock
 
1  lb. shelled fresh fava beans or edamame 
 
¼   cup finely chopped fresh flat-leaf parsley
 
1 Tbs. finely chopped fresh rosemary
 
  Kosher salt and freshly ground black pepper
 
 Fresh lemon juice (optional)
In a medium skillet, heat 1 Tbs. of the oil over medium heat. Add 
the onion and garlic, and cook, stirring frequently, until tender and 
translucent, about 4 minutes. 
Add the stock and beans, and simmer until the beans are 
tender but still brightly colored, about 6 minutes. 
Drain the beans, reserving the cooking liquid. 
Put the beans, parsley, rosemary, and remaining 1 cup of oil 
into the bowl of a food processor. Purée until smooth. Thin with 
the reserved liquid, and season to taste with salt, pepper, and 
lemon juice, if you like. Let cool before serving.                   —L.L.

As much as possible in our recipes, we 
offer both sensory cues and time markers to 
help judge doneness. While time markers are 
great, they can serve only as an approxima-
tion since home appliances, environmental 
conditions, and quality of ingredients can vary. 
Visual cues are more dependable since they 
will happen at some point during the cooking 
process (i.e., until golden, until crisp-tender, un-
til al dente). But using the two together will give 
you the best results. If it’s taking much longer 
to reach, say, golden brown than is indicated by 
the time in the recipe, increase the heat; don’t 
just wait for what you’re cooking to eventually 
brown, which will most likely result in excessive 
moisture loss.

Manini’s gluten-free pasta: I go

Blitz torte: 
German or American?
It may be the best-tasting cake you’ve never heard of. 
Or maybe you have a German-American background 
and grew up eating your oma’s version? While there are 
as many versions of the blitz torte as there are theories of 
its origin, this elegant, multitextured cake is an innovative 
confection that stars golden cake layers topped and baked 
with crunchy meringue. 
Likely the cake hails from Germany and was brought over 
by immigrants, many of whom settled in the Midwest, where 
it remains a beloved dessert. Th e recipe was fi rst published 
here in the mid-1940s in Th e Victory Binding of the American 
Woman’s Cook Book, but became much more popular when it 
appeared in Th e Betty Crocker Cookbook. No matter where it’s 
from, one thing is for sure: Our version, sweetened with a silky 
strawberry-rhubarb jam fi lling, will be high on your list of 
celebration cakes for years to come.                   —Tanya Steel
