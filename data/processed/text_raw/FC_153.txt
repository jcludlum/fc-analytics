NOTE FROM THE EDITOR

Not Your Standard Fare

IF YOU HAVEN’T NOTICED, IT’S CLEARLY A GREAT
TIME TO BE A BURGER LOVER. From the ridiculously
expensive burgers crafted with dry-aged Wagyu beef imported
from Japan to those made with Black Angus beef from just
down the road, to others featuring charred vegetables or fresh
seafood—and all with a vast array of toppings—it seems like
every restaurant is getting in on the burger action. Consider-
ing the countless ways to enjoy them, why not?

In this issue, we're featuring four standouts from the pros.
For example, chef Chris Cosentino puts the “ham” in ham-
burger with succulent brined pork, and a teriyaki-sweet potato
burger with chimichurri sauce is the brainchild of chef Jose
Martinez. Turn to p. 54 for more deliciously creative takes on
this classic—you won't be disappointed.

Another pro, chef Tom Douglas, gives us a lesson in grill-
ing fish—something near and dear to his Seattle-based heart.
Douglas not only shares with us four delectable dishes (how
does Grilled Salmon with Red-Miso Glaze and Nectarine-Thai
Basil Relish sound?), but also his foolproof tips for achieving
perfect results on the grill (p. 70).

Just as burgers and grilling reign supreme this time of year,
so does the simplicity of a salad. Frequent contributor Ivy
Manning offers up five delicious variations on grain salads
(p. 64), and author Erin Coopey provides you with an arsenal
of homemade dressings (p. 80) to perk up everything from
mixed greens to chicken and beyond.

And what would summer be without a fresh fruit pie? For a
new take on this dessert, try one of the outstanding sheet pan
versions on p. 88 from food stylist and baker Erin McDowell.
Call in the troops because one of these pies constitutes a party!

For all of their differences, there is one quality that all of
the food in this issue has in common: inventiveness. At first
glance, a lineup of burgers, grilled fish, salads, and fruit pies
may seem like standard fare, but the recipes on these pages are
far from standard. Enjoy!

—Kathy Kingsley,

CUBAN RICE,

BEANS & RAISINS

INGREDIENTS

Black beans:

3 slices thick-sliced bacon

/4 cup seeded, chopped, colored sweet peppers
tablespoon finely chopped garlic

2 teaspoons seeded, finely chopped jalapefio pepper

2 teaspoons seeded, finely chopped serrano pepper

2 teaspoons seeded, finely chopped, yellow hot pepper
teaspoon ground cumin

2 (15-ounce) cans black beans, rinsed, drained

/2 cup chicken broth

/2 teaspoon kosher salt
/4 teaspoon freshly ground pepper

Rice:
/2 cup Sun-Maid Raisins
cup jasmine rice
cup canned coconut milk, well-stirred
3/4 cup chicken broth or stock
tablespoon freshly squeezed lime juice
2 tablespoons butter
2 tablespoons chopped red onion

/2 teaspoon freshly grated lime zest
/2 teaspoon kosher salt

Plantains:
2 tablespoons butter

2 tablespoons coconut oil

llarge, ripe plantain, peeled, cut into 1/4-inch slices
2 tablespoons coconut sugar (or white sugar)

1/2 teaspoon kosher salt

DIRECTIONS

Black beans:

Ina medium-sized skillet, over medium heat, cook bacon,
turning halfway, until golden brown and crisp. Remove
bacon; cool, crumble, set aside. In the same skillet, use up
to 2 tablespoons of bacon fat, and cook all peppers and
garlic over medium heat until slightly tender. Add cumin;
stir for 1 minute. Add black beans, chicken broth, salt and
pepper. Bring to a boil; reduce heat to medium and cook
until heated through. Keep warm.

Rice:
Ina 2-quart saucepan, add all rice ingredients. Bring toa
boil; cover and reduce the heat to a simmer. Cook until rice
is tender. Fluff with a fork and keep warm.

Plantains:

Ina medium skillet, over medium heat, melt butter and
coconut oil. In a large bowl, toss together sliced plantains,
sugar and salt. Place plantains in the skillet, and cook until

golden brown; turn over and continue cooking until the
second side is golden brown. Remove from the skillet and
place on a parchment-lined cookie sheet. Cool slightly, then
mash with the bottom of a cup or your palm. Repeat with
remaining plantain slices.

To serve: Place a large scoop
of rice and beans ona plate.
Surround with plantains.

If desired, sprinkle with
chopped cilantro and
crumbled white cheese.

Makes 4-6 servings

Minty Watermelon Salad

cubes and putin a large bowl with

Blueberry-Lemon-

Thyme Sauce

blueberries, sugar to taste, and fresh
lemon juice. Cook over medium-

Summer Squash “Pasta”
Using a spiralizer or vegetable
peeler, slice two summer squash
or zucchini into noodles or ribbons.
Squeeze and pat dry. Cook the
squash in olive oil, stirring often, until
softened and light golden brown.
Add halved cherry tomatoes and
pesto, and stir until combined and
the tomatoes soften. Season with
salt and pepper, and garnish with
chopped fresh basil.

—Tanya Steel

Garlic Scape Hummus
Combine % cup extra-virgin olive
oil, 4 cup chopped garlic scapes,
and 2tsp. ground cumin ina small
saucepan. Cook over medium-low
heat, stirring often, for 3 minutes.
Ina food processor, purée the garlic
scape mixture, 2 cans drained and
rinsed chickpeas, /2 cup tahini, 3 Tbs.
fresh lemon juice, 1 Tbs. soy sauce,
and %tsp. kosher salt, adding water
to thin, if you like. —Sarah Opdahl

Sautéed Broccoli Rabe

with Garlic
Blanch chopped broccoli rabe in
well-salted water until the stems
are just tender, then transfer to an
ice bath. Drain and blot dry with
paper towels. Warm olive oil inalarge
skillet. Add the broccoli rabe, thinly
sliced garlic, a splash of vegetable
broth, anda pinch of red pepper
flakes. Cover and cook until crisp-
tender. Serve with lemon wedges.
—Diana Andrews

Whole Trout with Pickled Cipollini Onions

Sprinkle the inside of a whole trout with Worcestershire sauce,
then stuff with pickled cipollini onions, fresh thyme sprigs, a
drizzle of honey, salt, and pepper. Close the fish with toothpicks,

and then roast or broil. Great with baby potatoes.
—Brett Goldman

GOOD SPIRITS

Elderflower Power

This gently floral liqueur is the go-along, get-along spirit
that makes a wide range of drinks and desserts blossom.
TEXT BY NANETTE MAXIM; RECIPES BY RONNE DAY

FOR JUST FOUR TO SIX WEEKS IN LATE SPRING
TO EARLY SUMMER, DELICATE WHITE FLOW-
ERS SHAPED LIKE STARS APPEAR ON THE ELDER
TREE. For generations, especially in Europe, elder-
flowers have been gathered and made into juices
and cordials, as well as teas as a home remedy for
colds and flu. (The elder leaves, flowers, bark, and
berries all have unique medicinal properties.)

Europeans have enjoyed elderflower liqueur for
many years, yet the lightly sweet, slightly citrus,
and gently floral drink hadn't made a splash across
the pond. But in 2007, in cocktail bars across the
United States, the beverage was transformed from
wallflower to life of the party when young distiller
Robert J. Cooper introduced his version of the
liqueur, which he called St-Germain, and packaged
it in a striking Art Deco-inspired bottle.

Cooper came from a family of distillers, but they
werent sold on his idea. So he struck out on his
own, launching Cooper Spirits and setting up shop
in the French Alps, where he hired a team of farm-
ers to grow and gather the tender elderflowers that

are then macerated in a special process with eau de
vie (a fruit-based brandy)—hence the hints of pear,
lychee, or melon.

Thanks in no small part to Cooper’s enthu-
siastic promotion of the subtle honeyed, flowery
spirit, mixologists began to experiment, pairing
St-Germain with everything from gin to bourbon.
Soon, dashes of elderflower liqueur were being
added to margaritas and martinis, gimlets, and gin
and tonics, and its popularity in cocktails and as a
digestif surged.

The liqueur’s floral essence and slightly sweet
flavor make it a handy ingredient, especially for
desserts. It's also a natural for sauces, like a creamy
custard paired with fruit (see p. 26). It also adds
citrus and floral notes to baked goods, such as cakes,
cupcakes, and cookies. You can fold it into whipped
cream or créme fraiche, too. On the savory side, a
bit of the liqueur added to a tart chutney or relish
can add just the right hint of heady sweetness. The
elderflower itself may be ephemeral, but the liqueur
lets the flavor of spring live on and on.

staple with some stellar recipes from these renowned chefs. While

their ultimate recipes plus insights into their techniques. Your family

REMEMBER WHEN WHOLE GRAINS WEREN T
MAINSTREAM? When they were available only in bulk

at natural-food markets and eaten more for health reasons
than for flavor? Times sure have changed. Thanks to newly
“discovered” ancient grains like the now-ubiquitous qui-
noa, but also up-and-comers like freekeh and kamut, whole
grains have never been more popular or accessible. While
whole grains are still full of fiber, protein, and antioxidants,
what gets more attention now is their nutty-sweet flavor
and interesting textures.

I fell hard for whole grains while teaching at Bob’s Red
Mill, the pioneering grain company based just outside my
hometown of Portland, Oregon. Whole grains contain
three parts of the kernel: the bran, germ, and endosperm,
and though there’s no official definition for “ancient”
grains, the name implies the grain has not been tinkered
with and so has remained the same over time. While de-
veloping recipes for my classes, I found that salads—think
whole grains combined with spinach, shrimp, and piquant
piquillo peppers or roasted eggplant, fresh mint, and the
strained yogurt known as labneh—are a wonderful way to
showcase the wide array of grains.

Boost the flavor of whole grains. Cooked on their own
without any seasoning, whole grains can be a bit boring. That’s
why I always infuse their cooking liquid, using broth instead
of water, for example, or adding spices or herbs to the pot. Salt
is also a must; without it, any grain tastes flat. If [ve cooked
the grain ahead and refrigerated it, I let it come to room tem-
perature before serving. Cold starches tend to stiffen and dull
in flavor when very cold. Once cooked, whole grains can also
benefit from a toss with a vinaigrette or dressing.

Like rice, these grains may be cooked in different ways:
boiled like pasta in ample water, with the absorption method,
or like a pilaf, in which the grains are toasted with aromat-
ics before liquid is added. The rapid-boil method tends to be
easiest since you simply cook the grains until they’re a texture
you like. If you go with the absorption method, you can
always drain off excess water, or even add hot water if your
grain isn't tender enough after the liquid has been absorbed.

Balance whole grains with acidity, sweetness, and fat. A
generous amount of vinegar or citrus boosts flavor so that
salads pop. To counter the acid, add something sweet—sugar,
dates, or honey. Fat is also a must; use good-quality extra-
virgin olive oil, Asian sesame oil, and tangy cheeses to tie
everything together. Texture is also important: Roasted nuts
and crisp-tender blanched vegetables add welcome crunch.

With all those elements in one bowl, these new spins on
grain salads are incredibly satisfying, whether theyre starring in
your lunch box or youre serving them at your next cookout.

Fish on the Grill

Go beyond burgers and steaks this summer, and try your hand at
grilling fish. With a kiss of smoke and little know-how, perfect results
are easier to attain than you might think. BY TOM DOUGLAS

WORKING THE EDGE. THAT’S THE SIMPLE CON-
CEPT YOU HAVE TO MASTER TO BECOME A WORLD-
CLASS FISH GRILLER. After 40 years of manning the grill,
opening 18 restaurants, and cooking meals for presidents,
kings, and fishermen alike, I think it’s safe to say that my
technique stands with the best of them.

I was 18 when I moved to Seattle. I didn’t know much
about anything when I got here, but I knew enough to realize
that the pristine wild seafood was an unbelievable resource,
ready to be rocked on the grill. There’s no hesitation in this
town when it comes to firing up fruit-wood charcoal, mari-
nating some salmon or halibut fillets, and grilling dinner. The
smoke of the grill goes beautifully with the fat content of wild
salmon, for example—and I’ve grilled tens of thousands of
pieces of salmon over the years—but almost any firm-fleshed
fish will be enhanced by a lick of fire and the char of the grill.

Most likely, you know how to grill steak or burgers or
chicken, but if you haven't tried grilling fish, it’s time to put
that at the top of your summer to-do list.

Whether you light up a gas or charcoal grill, you're
going to work the edges of your heat, so move the coals to
the center of your kettle or light only the middle burners on
your gas grill. Unlike steaks or burgers, fish can’t handle the
red-hot center of the grill; however, you still want to be close
enough to the fire to achieve a bit of sear; unlike steaks or
burgers, however, fish can't handle the red-hot center of the
fire. Hence, working the edge. Here’s how:

Place your fillets right on the edge of the heat line, and
be sure not to disturb the fillet for a minute or two so that a
“crust” develops.

Use a fish spatula to gently lift the edge of the fillet to
loosen it from the grate, and then turn it over. The goal is to
have a clean release, leaving nice grill marks on the fish.

One-two-five—it’s the magic number. Continue cook-
ing the fish on the edge of your fire until your digital meat
thermometer reads 125°F when inserted into the thickest part
of the fish.

I'm a big fan of simplicity when it comes to cooking fish. If
you begin by investing your dollars in the highest-quality sea-
food, you'll be delighted to discover how easy it is to let your
grilling talent—and the deliciousness of the fish—shine.

SUMMERTIME COOKING IS ABOUT
EASE AND SIMPLICITY, and nothing
fits that criteria more than a big, healthy
salad. All you need are ultra-fresh greens
and produce, maybe a pantry staple or
two (canned beans or tuna, for example),
and last—but arguably most important—
a flavorful homemade dressing.

If you have everything on hand, dress-

they stay delicious in the fridge for at least

Keep it fresh

Because salad dressings often
contain small amounts of fresh
herbs, it’s tempting to substi-
tute dried. If you’re looking for
vibrant flavor, though, fresh
herbs are the way to go.

Although dried herbs have
concentrated flavor, they
quickly lose pungency as their
essential oils and compounds
evaporate. Dried herbs are
best for longer-cooking recipes
because the cooking process
reconstitutes them, drawing out
their flavor. A freshly prepared
salad dressing doesn’t allow
enough time for the same
extraction of flavor.

artisanal vinegars

Conversations about quality oils abound, particularly olive
oil, but don’t overlook quality vinegar as well.

Traditionally, vinegar is made in a slow, time-consuming

commercial vinegars are produced at lightning speed with

Parbake for perfection

The road to a perfect pie
crust can be paved with
pitfalls. But parbaking is
one step you can take to
ensure a crisp bottom crust
for pies with fillings that are
baked in the crust.
Parbaking (also some-
times called blind baking,
although that process usu-
ally refers to acrust that is
fully rather than partially
baked) allows the bottom
crust of a pie to partially
bake and dry so that it will

absorb less moisture when
the filling is added and not
become soggy.

To ensure success, dock
the dough by pricking it all
over with the tines of a fork.
This will prevent bubbles
from forming and create a
flat, even layer at the base.
Next, line the dough with
parchment or foil, and fill

another pan on top). This

TECHNIQUE

Where’s the fire?

Before grilling, it’s important to know the difference
between direct and indirect grilling.

Direct-heat grilling is placing food on the grill right
over the flame. This method is best suited to small,
tender, quick-cooking foods like steaks, burgers, chops,
fish, high-moisture vegetables, and skewered items.
The hot side of the grill can also be used for searing
foods to develop color, grill marks, and a crust before
moving them over indirect heat to finish cooking.

Indirect-heat grilling is placing food on the grill
so that it is next to, near, or between the fire. Use this
method when grilling large, whole, or fattier cuts of meat
such as pork loin, pork shoulder, or whole chickens that
require longer, slower cooking.

The indirect-heat side of the grill also serves as a
flame-free zone. In case of flare-ups, just move your
food there until the flames die down.

A grain of truth

You hear it all the time: Eat whole grains for a healthy
diet (unless you're gluten intolerant). But did you
ever wonder what exactly a grain is? Grains are types
of cultivated grass harvested for their seed, aka grain,
and are comprised of three components: the endo-
sperm, which provides the plant’s food source; the
germ, which is the nucleus of the seed and grows the
plant; and the bran, which forms the outer skin of the
seed. A whole grain has all three of these, whereas a
refined grain (that found in white flour) has the bran
and germ removed, and thus lacks the healthy iron,
fiber, and some vitamins found in the whole grain.

So go with the (whole) grain. —Tanya Steel

homemade labneh

Stirred into soups and sauces, this extrathick yogurt
adds tangy creaminess, and it’s rich enough to use
in place of sour cream as a topping, as a dip for pita

Linea strainer with cheesecloth and set over a medium bowl.

Cilantro

You Say cilantro; | say coriander

An annual herb with various names, coriander (aka
Chinese parsley, aka cilantro, aka tasty) is used by many
but understood by few. Two parts of this one plant are
edible: The fruit or seed is known as the coriander seed,
while the plant’s feathery leaves are known as cilantro.
(Fun fact No. 1: Cilantro is Spanish for coriander.)

The seeds are commonly sold whole or ground, and
their warm, nutty flavor, heightened when roasted, is
used in curries and chilis, and often paired with cinna-
mon and cumin. Cilantro, with its sprightly, citrusy flavor,
is chopped up and used in many Latin American and
Southern Asian dishes. (Fun fact No. 2: A small percent-
age of people have a gene that makes cilantro taste soapy
to them.) Use it in our Cilantro-Lime Guacamole or Spicy
Cilantro Chicken Wings (found on FineCooking.com).
