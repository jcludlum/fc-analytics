I RECENTLY HAD THE PLEASURE OF
VISITING CHEF CHRISTOPHER
KosTOW’S CULINARY GARDEN

for an episode of our PBS show,
Moveable Feast with Fine Cook-
ing. Teeming with fresh vegetables
and herbs (plus bees and goats), the
garden inspires the menus at the
chef’s two Napa Valley restaurants,
The Restaurant at Meadowood and
Charter Oak. I was taken with the
lush bounty and immediately started
dreaming of ways to cook with

the sun-warmed tomatoes, earthy
carrots, and spicy radishes. It's the
same kind of inspiration that can
come from local farmers’ markets,
especially at this time of year.

Just ask cookbook author and
Fine Cooking’s editor at large, Susie
Middleton. Her menu on p. 48 is all
about making the most of overflow-
ing vegetable gardens and farm-
stands by hosting a relaxed party.

This fresh and colorful vegetarian
menu also features a do-it-yourself
artisan toast bar where guests

can mingle and get creative with
toppings—a surefire, delicious way
to get your party started.

Another Fine Cooking alum,
Shelley Wiseman, shows us how to
throw an authentic taco party (p. 66).
The secret? Homemade tortillas and
tamarind margaritas—and we won't
argue that. A partner in The Farm
Cooking School (see right) and a taco
guru, Shelley provides step-by-step
instructions that will make you a tor-
tilla expert in no time, and her taco
fillings and salsas sing with flavor.

No celebration is complete without
a sweet treat to top it off. When the
heat rises outside, keep dessert cool
with one of cookbook author Jessie
Sheehan's no-bake treats (p. 82).
Deep-Dish Mascarpone Fig Pie with
Ginger-Hazelnut Crust, No-Bake
Almond Cheesecake with Cherry-
Kirsch Compote, Tahini Chocolate
Icebox Cake—these outstanding
flavor combinations are definitely not
the icebox cakes you grew up with.

There's plenty more here, too—
fresh corn salads, chefs’ favorite
lobster dishes, and fruit smoothies,
to name a few. Now’s the time for
cooks to take advantage of nature's
bounty, and this special issue
is here to help you do just that.
Enjoy!

—Kathy Kingsley,

By Tanya Steel

Food Fight! is a colorful, fact-packed romp through

the history of food from the basic (That “I’m hungry!”
feeling we all experience is certainly what motivated
early humans to stand up and walk) to the complex (It
was the price of bread that sparked the French Revolu-
tion). Written by FC contributing editor Tanya Steel, the
chapters range from The Prehistoric Era to The Future
World, and each offers an easy recipe or two. While
there’s surely a place for this fun, informative book on
any bookshelf, Food Fight! makes an endlessly engaging
gift if you're looking to share your love of food with your
favorite kid.

Sautéed Brussels Sprouts with Bacon and Pine Nuts

Sauté bacon until the fat has rendered. Remove the bacon, crumble,
and set aside. Add trimmed and halved Brussels sprouts, and sauté until
lightly browned and tender. Add pine nuts, and continue to sauté until

the sprouts are deep golden brown and the pine nuts are toasted. Toss
with the bacon, season to taste with salt and pepper, drizzle with pure

maple syrup, and serve. —Pamela Rainone Taylor, Montreal, Canada

Blending a
Superfood Meal

Smoothies are the perfect solution for
fast, delicious, nutritious mini meals.
Here’s my formula.

BY ERIN ALDERSON

SMOOTHIES TEND TO BE MY ANSWER WHEN I'M
LOOKING FOR A QUICK, ON-THE-GO BREAKFAST OR
THE PERFECT TODDLER-APPROVED SNACK. Having
a few smoothie recipes in my cooking arsenal ensures
that I always have an easy win. Best of all, I can control
exactly what goes into each concoction: no synthetic
powders or sugar-laden yogurt here. Just delicious,
nutrient-dense fruits, vegetables, nuts, and seeds.

I have a few hard-and-fast rules when it comes
to making smoothies. The first is to invest in a solid
blender. Powerful high-speed machines like those
from Vitamix, Blendtec, and even Magic Bullet make
quick work of smoothies, pulverizing even the tough-
est seeds.

Next, the ingredients are of the utmost importance.
Good quality is critical, and overripe produce is fine;
this is where all those odds and ends can really shine.
Leftover coffee? Stick it in the refrigerator for a coffee
smoothie later. Bananas turning brown? Toss them in
a freezer-safe container and stash away for smoothies.
Indeed, frozen fruit provides a thick, creamy texture,
negating the need for ice cubes. To that end, I like to
freeze batches of berries and stone fruit from summer
so that I can make smoothies year-round. (Frozen fruit
from the store also works well.)

Beyond the fruit and vegetables, I like to add some-
thing creamy, such as avocado or yogurt, to enhance the
texture. I also play fairly loose with the liquid amounts.
I recommend starting with less liquid and adding more
as needed. Finally, I like the flavors of the fruits and
vegetables to really shine, which means I keep sweeten-
ers to a minimum. As with the liquid, start with less
and add more to suit your taste. A few of my favorite
sweeteners are pure maple syrup, honey, and dates.

If you follow my tips, smoothies can come together
in no time with whatever is hanging around in your
refrigerator and freezer. Try some of these recipes
to get your creative juices flowing (and find more at
FineCooking.com/smoothies).

Erin Alderson is the founder of Naturally Ella
(naturallyella.com).

Why starch matters

Starches consist of chains of glucose sugar molecules, and the arrange-

Amylose and amylopectin are the two types of starch found in rice. A
typical chain of amylose starch consists of about 1,000 glucose sugars
arranged in a few long branches. This simple structure causes amylose
molecules to settle into tight, compact structures that are difficult to
soften, helping them keep their shape even after cooking.

Adding onion

Onions are added to even the most basic pilaf. Add-
ing onion when sautéing the rice boosts a pilaf’s overall
flavor. The heat breaks down the onion fiber, allowing it

vehicle to transport the onion’s aromatic chemicals to
the simmering rice.

BY DIANA ANDREWS

FRESH, SWEET LOBSTER REMINDS ME OF SUMMER VACA-
TIONS WITH MY FAMILY IN MAINE. The memories are still so
vivid that if I close my eyes, I can almost smell the briny ocean as

we drove to the lobster shacks along the rocky coastline, my mouth
watering and my anticipation building with each moment. We

made a pact to try a different shack every day, and although I can’t
remember my favorite place, I remember the red picnic tables, the
bright blue sky, and the pure unfettered delight of slurping that sweet
lobster meat out of its shell.

These days, especially in summer, I prefer to devour a butter-
drenched steamed lobster in the privacy of my home, where I’m not
embarrassed to surgically dig out every last morsel of the exquisite
snowy white meat. While steamed lobster in the summer always hits
the spot, I started thinking that it needs to be enjoyed more than
once a year. That’s why I asked some of my favorite chefs for their
best lobster recipes.

Like me, they believe that something extraordinary doesn't really
need a zillion ingredients or complicated steps to make it sublime.
The Truffled Lobster Macaroni and Cheese from Steve Corry is a
perfect example. A luscious, silky-smooth cheese sauce lightly coats
the lobster without overwhelming its delicate flavor. Finished with
a modest drizzle of truffle oil and a few truffle peelings, it’s luxuri-
ous without going overboard. For a refreshing take on lobster rolls,
try Ginger Pierce and Preston Madson’s version with just the right
ratio of lobster to chopped salad—the brioche bun adds a touch of
sweetness and absorbs the tasty mayonnaise dressing without getting
mushy. Emily Peterson offers up a unique and utterly delectable
dish of Lobster Poached in Gewiirztraminer and Pear Nectar. The
surprising combination of ingredients delivers sophisticated flavor
and is easy to make. And last but not least, a showstopping Lobster
Benedict from Matt Jennings will make any weekend breakfast or
brunch truly memorable.

You can simplify all of these recipes by purchasing cooked lobster
meat, but it’s pretty easy to prepare for yourself (see the sidebar on
p. 61). Besides, when you cook the lobster yourself, you can steal a
few bites in the kitchen—plate and napkins optional.

BUYING AND STORING

Look for lobsters that are
lively when removed from
their tanks.

Store live lobsters in the
coldest spot in the refrigera-
tor (usually the lower shelf),
lightly covered with damp
newspapers. Never store live
lobsters on ice.

To grow, lobsters must molt,
or shed their old hard shells.
Lobsters whose shells are soft
and have not yet hardened
are called new-shell lobsters.
Some consider new-shell
lobster meat to be sweeter
and softer than hard shell.
Although lobsters are avail-
able year-round, new-shell
lobsters are available only
from midsummer through
midfall, and usually need to
be specially requested.

e If you’ve cooked frozen
lobster tails and found
them to be mushy, it doesn’t
mean that the lobster is
bad. Instead, you most likely
have a rock lobster tail. Rock
lobsters don’t have claws
and generally are sold in tail
form only. They come from
the warmer waters of places
such as Florida, California, the
Caribbean, or Latin America.
Although the flavor will still
be tasty, the texture will
definitely disappoint. The
more desirable and expensive
cold-water lobster tails, with
their firmer flesh, are har-
vested from Maine, Canada,
Australia, South Africa, or
New Zealand. How to distin-
guish between the two? Rock
lobsters usually have white
spots on their shells.

ANATOMY OF ALOBSTER

e The soft green mass found
in the lobster’s body cavity
after cooking is called the
tomalley; it functions as the
liver and pancreas. Some
consider this a delicacy.
Enjoy in moderation, though,
because as with all livers, it
can contain toxins.

e Thered mass found along
the tail of a cooked lobster is
the coral, or lobster roe. It’s

edible, and many consider it
atreat.

The thick white congealed
liquid remaining in the
steaming pot and partially

in the lobster after it’s cooked
is called the hemolymph and
is equivalent to blood. This
liquid is clear when the lob-
ster is alive. The hemolymph
is flavorless and harmless to
consume.

HOW TO STEAM ALOBSTER

Fill a pot large enough to fit the
lobsters with 1 inch of well-
salted water. Cover and bring

the water to a rolling boil. Mean-

while, dispatch the lobsters, if
desired (see our video at Fine
Cooking.com/article/video-
how-to-kill-a-lobster). When
the pot fills with steam, add the
lobsters, holding them by the
body and arranging them on
top of each other, facing in dif-

ferent directions so the steam
can easily circulate. Cover the
pot, and steam the lobsters
until the shells are bright red
and the antennae can be easily
pulled off, 10 to 12 minutes for
1-lb. lobsters, 2 minutes longer
for each additional % Ib. As

an easy alternative, ask your
fishmonger to steam whole
lobsters for you.

and fat, and high in pro-

Whether it’s co-writing a book about them

fridge for cool, no-bake desserts. The assembly pro-

up a luscious mascarpone pie or to soften chocolate

cooler than heating the oven in the middle

cream or a crisp cookie crust filled with
something decadent and chocolatey, a

casual dinners on the back porch and
