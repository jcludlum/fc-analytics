I cucumber, thinly sliced

Mix the marinade items into a paste; rub it into the lamb.
Marinate for eight hours. Heat 2 Tbsp. oil ina pan and sear
the lamb. Add the onions and sauté. Blend together the
veggie broth and chipotles. Add this and the bay leaves
to the lamb pan. Bring to a boil, then cover. Cook on low
for 60 minutes. Uncover and cook until the liquid reduces
to a sauce and the lamb is tender (about 30 minutes).
Then, shred the lamb with a fork. While the lamb is
cooking, put the raisins and balsamic in a small saucepan.
Simmer until the raisins are plump and the vinegar reduces
halfway. Remove the raisins, setting them aside with the
other toppings. Heat the pitas in a pan with a little oil.
Add the meat and toppings.

Beer-Braised Turkey

Serves 10 to 12 with leftovers

1 packed cup dark brown sugar
Kosher salt

2 Tbs. black peppercorns

6 bay leaves

6 (12-ounce) bottles stout beer

1/2 cup dark molasses

112- to 14-Ib. turkey

1 large yellow onion, cut into thick wedges

1 large red onion, cut into thick wedges

8 shallots, halved if large

5 Tbs. all-purpose flour

1 cup lower-salt turkey or chicken broth;
more as needed

1 Tbs. unsalted butter

1tsp. minced fresh thyme leaves
Pomegranate seeds, for garnish

In a large pot, combine 4 cups water with
the sugar, 1/2 cup salt, the peppercorns,
and bay leaves, and simmer over medium
heat until the sugar and salt dissolve,
about 7 minutes. Remove from the heat;
let cool completely.

Add the stout, molasses, and 8 cups of
cold water to the pot. Add the turkey to
the brine, breast side down, and top with
a heavy lid to keep it submerged. Cover
and refrigerate for 24 hours.

Position a rack in the bottom of the oven
and heat to 325°F. Remove the turkey from
the brine; pat dry. Tie the legs together,
and tuck the wing tips under the neck.

Put the turkey, breast side up, in an If You
Care Extra Large Parchment Roasting Bag,
with seam side up on a large rimmed bak-
ing sheet. Don’t push the turkey all the way
to the back of the bag. Add the onions and
shallots. Seal the open end of the bag by
folding tightly as many times as possible
toward the top until it snugly fits the turkey.

Roast the turkey for 1-1/2 hours. Remove
the turkey from the oven, and raise heat
to 375°F. Cut the top of the bag (the
steam will be hot) to expose the turkey.
Roast uncovered until an instant-read

NOTE FROM THE EDITOR

New Traditions

‘ NG
- Si,

Editorial director Kathy Kingsley (left) with cook-
book author and Fine Cooking contributor Jessie
Sheehan (right) during one of our Facebook Live
events. See right for a review of Jessie’s latest book.

“We've always done it this way” is a common
refrain around Thanksgiving. And indeed,
for many of us, Thanksgiving dinner var-

ies little from year to year. The recipes are
heirlooms, passed down from generation

to generation, reminders of who we are and
where we came from.

But as I see it, one of the best things about
tradition is that you can create your own. This
issue is filled with dozens of recipes that could
be traditions in the making. Leading the way
are three talented chefs and their favorite turkey
recipes (p. 76). David Guas of Bayou Bakery
in Arlington, Virginia, soaks his turkey in a
cider brine before deep-frying. Emily Brekke
of North End Grill in New York City has been
perfecting her technique since she was a teen,
using a Champagne-stock mixture to great ef-
fect. Luke Verhulst of Reserve Wine & Food in
Grand Rapids, Michigan, ups the creative factor
by making a decadently moist yet crisp turkey
confit. If you're serving a crowd, consider cook-

ing two birds, one your tried-and-true way and
then one of these—you wont be disappointed.

Mashed potatoes are perfect for cradling pools
of velvety gravy, but you can bring them to a
new level by using Peruvian purple potatoes and
adding corn and crispy scallions (p. 74). The Has-
selback Russet Gratin (p. 73) offers lots of cheesy
flavor and a touch of elegance with its stunning
presentation. And if youre looking for a show-
stopper side (or main), the Curried Cauliflower
Salad (p. 63) from chef Ris Lacoste is layered with
flavors and textures, and perfect for vegetarian
guests at your holiday table.

Probably no word evokes Thanksgiving
dessert more than “pie,” and few people are
as passionate about making a great crust than
Ken Haedrich. He’s the author of Pie Zero to
Pie Hero: How to Ditch Your Fears, Master the
Crust, and Bake the Perfect Pie and also the
dean of ThePieAcademy.com. Turn to p. 32 for
his piecrust tips and techniques. Then sit back
and listen to the oohs and aahs your Thanks-
giving (or any other) pies receive.

Beyond Thanksgiving, I would be remiss in
not mentioning that the Homemade Spiced
Cider Doughnuts (p. 58) are the best I’ve ever
had, and so worth it, as are the other dishes in
our feature on cooking with cider (p. 56) from
Fine Cooking alum Tony Rosenfeld. And just in
time for autumn, we have four recipes using our
new favorite appliance, the Instant Pot (p. 64).
What's not to love about a flavorful minestrone
that’s ready in just 30 minutes?

This issue is packed with outstanding seasonal
recipes, all of which could someday become new
traditions in your home. The cider doughnuts
will definitely become one of mine!

—Kathy Kingsley, editorial director

Old baking brochures are filled
with plenty of recipes many of
us might not want to revisit, but
among their age-tinged pages,
Jessie Sheehan has found gold.
Inspired by her collection of
vintage pamphlets, Sheehan has
upgraded classic recipes with
contemporary ingredient tweaks.
With an inventive twist, Shee-
han’s “Everything” Buttermilk
Biscuits updates a classic with
the popular bagel-topping mix-
ture, with delicious results. She
also brings back forgotten ideas,
like incorporating cookie crumbs
into the batter of “S’More” Gra-
ham Cracker Cake. (Who knew
graham cracker cakes were even
a thing?) The Vintage Bakerisa
fun, inspirational look at where
baking has been and where it is
today. —Chris Hoelck

here is no island culture more distinct—and more

Just a 90-minute flight from New York City, or 650 miles
off North Carolina’s coast, this 21-square-mile island
possesses an unparalleled beauty of pink-sand beaches,
turquoise-blue waters, and pastel-colored buildings.

It’s little wonder Bermuda has been a haven for the
adventurous, nature-seeking, and spirited since 1505
when it was discovered by the Spanish sea captain Juan de
Bermudez. Its beauty and locale captivated many, including
the British, who made it an overseas territory in 1707.

This history has resulted in a proud patchwork of
British, Caribbean, West African, Portuguese, and Native
American cultures and influences, and this is clearly evi-

dent in its polyglot cuisine, which makes great use
: of its fresh fish, fruits, and, yes, rum.

Unlike other islands in the Atlantic Ocean, seafood and

Other favored fresh fish include yellowfin tuna, rockfish,

Mastering Pizza

The Art and Practice of Handmade

(Ten Speed Press; $29.99)

The best books about pizza are, in actual-
ity, books about obsession, and that’s a
good thing. If someone's going to teach
us to make pizza—real pizza—then that
someone had better be stark raving mad
about it. And Marc Vetri is. Trust him
when he promises in the early pages of his
new book to “help you make better pizza
in whatever oven you have with whatever
dough you want.” (By “whatever dough,”
he means, of course, any one of the

20 obsessively perfected dough recipes
from his book.)

Great dough is the key to great crust.
And great crust, the key to great ’za. “It’s
all about nailing the crust,’ Vetri writes.
And nail it, you will. Not the first time,
perhaps, and maybe not even the second
or third. Immediate gratification is not
the point here. The point is to pursue a
passion. Becoming a pizzaiolo takes time
and practice.

Between the covers of Mastering Pizza,
you'll find all the information you need—
plus step-by-step photos that demystify
the process—to begin practicing and

‘The opening chapters are a deep dive,

super-thick rectangular or square pizza al
taglio, which is cut with scissors. Vetri has
some fun with the dough along the way,
offering dozens of variations on his mas-
ter recipes, plus loads of toppings, from
classic pepperoni or margherita (tomato,
mozzarella, basil) to innovative carbonara
(eggs, pecorino, guanciale) and granchio
(fresh crab and roasted peppers). Which-
ever starting point you choose, whether
you nail the crust or not, the results are
sure to be delicious.

THE BAKER’S CORNER

Rose’s Baking Basics

100 Essential Recipes, with More

Than 600 Step-by-Step Photos

By Rose Levy Beranbaum (Houghton

Mifflin Harcourt; $35)

Like most bakers, I value precision. So I
appreciate the detail that Rose Levy Beran-
baum incorporates into her recipes. If you're
a Beranbaum fan, I suspect you've come

to expect it, too. Nearly every ingredient in
every recipe of her new book, Roses Baking
Basics, includes measurements in both
volume and weight (in grams—again, preci-
sion). To top it off, where germane, recipes
also include gram weights for the quantity of
batter or dough, which is super handy if you
want to portion things equally to create cake
layers, muffins, or cookies that are the same
size. (Why don't more baking books do this?)
This attention to detail extends to the book’s

more than 600 step-by-step photographs,
too, depicting how to make biscuit dough,
say, or to shape a loaf of bread.

The recipes at first glance look like a
collection of tried-and-true treats and
techniques, but look closer, and you'll
discover many fun twists. For instance, it
doesn’t get more classic than chocolate-
chip cookies; yet Rose’s Chocolate Chip
Cookies has an ingredient tweak—golden
syrup—that produces beautifully brown,
wonderfully chewy results. Likewise, her
Fudgy Pudgy Brownies contain—sur-
prise—melted white chocolate, which
gives the bars a divinely creamy texture.

My favorite discovery in the book isn’t
even a recipe. It’s a technique for lining a
tart pan with dough. I love tarts, but I al-
ways manage to mangle the buttery dough
a bit trying to get it into the fluted, sharp-
edged pan. Then I tried Beranbaum’s tech-
nique. What a game changer! I liked her
method so much the first time I attempted
it that I immediately made a second batch
of dough and lined another pan to confirm
that her technique was indeed that clever.
It was. Fortunately, Beranbaum’s new book
has four tart recipes. I’m looking forward
to trying them all. —Chris Hoelck

KITCHEN BASICS

THIS MIGHT RUFFLE SOME FEATHERS, but I have a bone
to pick with whoever coined the expression “as easy as pie.”
As a pie instructor, the author of four pie cookbooks, and
the dean of The Pie Academy, an online community of pas-
sionate pie makers, I can assure you that a majority of home
cooks believe there’s almost nothing easy about pie, espe-
cially the crust; confusion and disappointment can reign.
Boiling water is easy. Making a grilled-cheese sandwich is
easy. Making a reliably good piecrust, not so much.

Accurate measurements can solve a lot of the problems
that plague a piecrust, but I think the big culprit is how we
interpret the language of pie-dough preparation. One cook’s
pea-size pie dough mixture might look like lentils, another's
the size of giant butter beans. If tell you to pulse the food
processor to incorporate the fat and flour, will your pulses
be % second or 3 seconds long? How firm is your cold but-
ter? Almost rock hard, or coolish and yielding to medium
thumb pressure? The sum of these seemingly small details
can have a real impact on your crust.

My aim here is to nudge us toward a common pie lan-
guage and understanding so that we shrink the margin of
error and achieve a more predictable outcome.

I mentioned the food processor because it’s the tool I use
almost exclusively in my day-to-day pie-dough making. ’'m
an old-school pie maker—I learned how to do everything
by hand—so I resisted the processor at first. Over time,

Think of the food processor as a high-speed pastry
blender. What typically takes five or more minutes to
do by hand takes a matter of seconds in the food proces-

required. My rule of thumb is to use only short 1-second
pulses when I’m preparing dough in the processor. Pulsing
is key because the pulsing action keeps tossing particles up
from the bottom of the bowl, which promotes even mixing.
When you run the machine nonstop, the blade tends to
compact the particles in the bowl, resulting in an unevenly
mixed dough.

I’ve experimented with different combinations of fats
and have decided that a blend of mostly unsalted but-
ter with a small amount of vegetable shortening, such as
Crisco, makes the best possible crust. The viscous shorten-
ing quickly coats the flour and helps prevent the formation
of gluten, which can make a crust too chewy. The shorten-
ing also acts like a muscle relaxer, so the dough is easier
to roll and less prone to shrinkage. The butter, for its part,
contributes to the crust’s flakiness and yields a flavor that
everyone adores.

Incidentally, size matters when it comes to the food
processor and pie dough. If your machine has a capacity

out of the refrigerator for about 10 minutes before rolling.

all nuance perfected over a lifetime. You'll find very few

The flaky vs. mealy question

Bakers tend to talk about flaky pie crust like it’s the
holy grail, but you’re not alone if you’ve ever won-
dered precisely what that means.

One reason it’s hard to pinpoint the definition of
flaky is because it’s not simply one thing. Rather, it’s
a textural spectrum that includes everything from
very small flakes—sometimes called a mealy crust—to
much bigger flakes created by larger pieces of butter
in the dough. The more the fat is worked into the flour,
the smaller—or shorter—the flakes become.

Everyone seems to have a preference. But the size
of the flakes, in my opinion, has little to do with the
overall goodness of the pie. Rather, the outcome
depends, among other things, on the quality of the
fruit, the balance of flavors, the doneness and tex-
ture of the filling, and the browning of the crust.

Autumn’s sweet apple drink
lends depth and complexity
to rich roasts and braises, and
heavenly doughnuts.

BY TONY ROSENFELD

CHEFS HAVE LONG EXTOLLED WINE’S
VERSATILITY IN THE KITCHEN. No doubt
you know to add a splash of red or white to
braises, sautés, and even marinades to impart
depth of flavor. Consider apple cider, then, as
wine’s close culinary kin. Pour this sweet nec-
tar—traditionally, an unfiltered blend of multiple
apple varietals—into a range of hardy fall prepa-
rations, and you'll be rewarded with nuanced,
even elegant, dishes that highlight the mood and
harvest of the season.

Of course, an old standby like cider doughnuts
is surely a headliner for the beverage’s utility. Just
mix a cider reduction with spices into a batter,
and fry. Once you taste them, you'll discover
that the crisp, toothsome rings have nothing in
common with the lifeless rounds from national
doughnut chains. And these doughnuts are so
easy to make that they'll help you to conquer
your fear of frying.

Cider is equally adept in the savory side of the
kitchen. Add it to a brine for roast pork loin, and
the exceedingly lean cut becomes succulent with
robust layers of flavors. Cider is also great for
quick-braising shellfish: Sweat onions or shallots,
and then steam clams or mussels in an inch or
so of cider until they just open so that the briny
and sweet flavors meld. And capturing the spirit
of autumn in a saucepan can be as simple as
reducing cider to a glazy consistency and tossing
in roasted root vegetables so that the browned
pieces become sweet, just like cider itself.

Tony Rosenfeld keeps busy as the chef of b.good, a

Boston-based restaurant group that he co-founded.

FIVE TIPS FOR INSTANT

DELICIOUSNESS

1. Best Is Best
The quality of the ingredients you cook with is,
as always, paramount. Use the best seasonal
ingredients you can find.

2. Brown First
When cooking meat, sear first, either in the
multicooker or a skillet, to add extra flavor.

3. Don’t Pile On
Never load the pot more than the maximum fill
line, or about two-thirds full.

4. Liquid Math
You don’t need a lot of liquid for a multicooker
or slow cooker; the covered, hot, moist environ-
ment makes it unnecessary. A 2-to-1 ratio of
ingredients to liquid is a good rule of thumb.

5. Tough to Tender
If not using the pressure-cooking function, start
with the toughest, longest-to-cook items on the
bottom when adding ingredients, and put more
delicate foods on top.

BRINE THE TURKEY

When it’s time to fry, tie the legs and tuck the wing tips

temperature reaches 165°F, turn off the fryer, and

In a large pot, warm the oil over medium heat until

Ruling the roast

When cooking a turkey, the pan you use is almost as important as the
quality of bird you buy. What’s a confused cook to do? First, choose a
deep pan with a heavy bottom and handles. Make sure it’s big enough for
the bird to comfortably fit in the pan without touching the sides, but not
so large that there is a lot of space around the bird, which would result in
burned vegetables and evaporated juices. Also, be sure the pan isn’t too
heavy; who wants to hoist 30 Ib. of turkey and pan out of the oven?
While there isn't one standard size for pans, a good rule of thumb is
to use a 10x14 pan for a smaller bird (10 to 14 Ib.); a 14- to 20-Ib. bird
fits nicely into an 11x17 pan whereas a larger one (in the 20- to 25-lb.
range) needs a 14x19 roasting pan. Pro tip: A wire rack at the bottom
keeps your bird crisp all over. —TS.

TIP

The stock (and broth) market

Using store-bought chicken stock or
broth is a simple way to fortify the gravies
for the turkey recipes on pp. 76-83.

We prefer boxed lower-salt or unsalted
broths and stocks. Although store-
bought broths and stocks are used inter-
changeably, there's a difference between
the two that’s not always apparent on the
ingredient list.

STOCKS have a richer flavor and

are generally made from simmering
bones (sometimes roasted to add fla-
vor), any small amount of meat that may
be on the bones, and aromatics. They are

cooked for a long period of time (four to
five hours) to break down connective tis-
sue and release collagen and gelatin into
the cooking liquid, resulting in substan-
tial body and flavor.

BROTH, on the other hand, is made
by simmering mostly meat (occasion-
ally some bones) and aromatics and is
a much quicker process, taking only
about 45 minutes to one hour. Stock
may be an ingredient in broth to add
some flavor and body, but the overall
character of broth is much thinner.

—D.A.

content to be a perfect fruit, it also offers
us glorious juice that’s both a magical
cooking ingredient, as the recipes on

pp. 56-61 demonstrate, and also a tasty
drink. Well, actually, many drinks—
which vary as wildly in complexity and
nuance as different types of apples do, as
we explain in this overview.

SWEET CIDER: Often called “apple
cider” or “cider,” this freshly pressed
apple juice is unfiltered, unsweetened,
unfermented, sometimes unpasteurized,
and nonalcoholic. Cider makers craft

it by grinding a blend of apple varietals
into a mash that is then squeezed in a
press to extract the juice. Ideally, the re-
sulting cider displays a distinctive flavor
and an appealing balance between tart
and sweet. Cider is perishable, so keep it
in the refrigerator for up to two weeks or
in the freezer for up to one year. (If kept
too long, sweet cider begins to ferment.)

HARD CIDER: This is what forms when
sweet cider ferments. Although Ameri-
cans refer to this fizzy, alcoholic brew as
“hard cider,” outside the United States,
it’s simply called “cider” When sweet
cider ferments, natural sugars in the
cider produce alcohol and gas.

APPLE JUICE: It’s filtered to remove
solids, so the liquid is clear. And it’s
pasteurized so that the beverage keeps
longer. —Kimberly Y. Masibay
