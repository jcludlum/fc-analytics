NOTE FROM THE EDITOR
Cookies and more 
It’s my good fortune to 
have a German mother-in-
law who carries on her mother’s 
tradition of baking pounds and 
pounds of Christmas cookies every 
holiday. With help from loved ones, 
she spends days mixing, rolling, 
cutting, decorating, and packing up 
an extravaganza of goodies that she 
shares with family and friends: vanilla 
strips, springerle (recipe at left ), Pfef-
fernüsse, maultaschen, thumbprints, 
and buttery splits, to name a handful. 
A huge box fi lled with cookies arrives 
at my house the fi rst week in Decem-
ber just in time to celebrate the feast 
of St. Nicholas on December 6. My 
daughters, who now live in New York 
City and Boston, each receive a box 
as well, as do all her children, grand-
children, and many others. 
Christmas cookies are a beloved 
tradition—the activity, the smells, 
the warmth of the oven, the tables 
covered with various shapes and 
colors. Plus, it’s a wonderful way to 
make the shortest, coldest days of 
the year cozy and delicious. Th is is 
what inspired our cookie feature on 
p. 80—a collection of staff  favorites. 
From Diana’s classic Greek koura-
biedes and Sarah’s pecan tassies to 
my chocolate-dipped biscotti and 
Chris’s spiced cranberry-pecan 
chocolate chip cookies, there are 
10 sweet reasons here to start bak-
ing—and tasting.
Th e brightening of winter could 
be a subtheme of this issue, as 
contributor Tony Rosenfeld brings 
us into the glow of his family’s 
Hanukkah celebration with a deli-
cious menu that pays homage to 
tradition and nods to the modern 
(p. 48). Morning light comes 
into the picture with our special 
Make It Tonight breakfast section 
(p. 33), a collection of quick-and-
easy guestworthy recipes. Who 
wouldn’t love sitting down to an 
asparagus frittata with prosciutto 
and caramelized onions or over-
night French toast mufg  ns?
Easy yet impressive is also the 
idea behind our article on how to 
create the perfect cheese platter 
(p. 58). Armed with these tips and 
inspiration, you’ll be creating spec-
tacular spreads that will leave your 
guests oohing and aahing.
As always, there’s a lot more in 
this issue: four simple and delicious 
30-minute dinners from revered 
chef Yotam Ottolenghi (p. 72), new 
twists on green-bean sides (p. 66), a 
collection of gift s for those on your 
list who love to cook (p. 22), and a 
roundup of some of our favorite new 
cookbooks (p. 28).
Getting back to my mother-in-
law, Jo Ann Kingsley, I’d like to add 
that she has a lot of other admirable 
qualities besides her generosity and 
baking skills. Also worth mention-
ing: No other family member has 
ever spotlighted her cookies in a 
magazine. Could that be enough to 
earn us a few extra this year? ;-)
Happy holidays from all of us at 
Fine Cooking!
—Kathy Kingsley, editorial director

Makes about 2½   dozen cookies
 
3  eggs 
 1 58  cups granulated sugar
 
¼    tsp. baking powder
 
¼    tsp. almond extract
 3¼    cups flour
 
 Aniseed
Using the dough hook on your mixer, beat the eggs, 
sugar, baking powder, and almond extract for 
15 minutes. Beat in the flour to make a soft dough. 
On a floured board, roll the dough to ½   inch thick. 
Press a springerle rolling pin firmly over the dough. 
Cut and arrange the springerle on heavily greased 
baking sheets that have been lightly sprinkled with 
aniseed. Let stand, uncovered, overnight.
Heat the oven to 350°F. Bake the spingerle for 
15 to 20 minutes, or until just lightly golden on the 
bottom. Remove from the oven, and transfer the cook-
ies to a wire cooling rack. The longer the springerle sit, 
the harder they become.
Keep cookies in an airtight container for 2 or 3 weeks.

FOR THE CRUST
 8   oz. (1 cup) unsalted butter, softened, 
cut into 6 pieces
 
2/3  cup granulated sugar
 
1  Tbs. ﬁ nely grated orange zest
 ¼  tsp. kosher salt
 11¼   oz. (2½ cups) unbleached 
all-purpose ﬂ our
FOR THE CARAMEL TOPPING
 2   cups pecan halves, toasted and 
coarsely chopped
 
1  cup packed light brown sugar
 ¾  cup heavy cream
 4   oz. (½ cup) unsalted butter, 
cut into chunks
 ½  cup light corn syrup
 ¼  tsp. table salt
FOR THE GANACHE
 2   oz. good-quality bittersweet 
chocolate, ﬁ nely chopped 
(about ½ cup)
 6  Tbs. heavy cream
MAKE THE CRUST
Position a rack in the center of the 
oven and heat the oven to 350°F. Line 
a straight-sided 13×9-inch metal bak-
ing pan with a large piece of parch-
ment, leaving a 2-inch overhang on 
two sides.
In a stand mixer ﬁ tted with the paddle 
attachment or in a large bowl with 
a hand mixer, beat the butter, sugar, 
orange zest, and salt on medium-
high speed until light and ﬂ uﬀ y, 1 to 
3 minutes. Scrape the bowl. On low 
speed, mix in the ﬂ our until the dough 
is uniformly sandy, 1 to 2 minutes.
Transfer the dough to the prepared 
pan and press evenly into the bottom. 
Bake until lightly golden, about 
20 minutes. Let the crust cool 
completely on a wire rack.
MAKE THE TOPPING
Sprinkle the pecans evenly over 
the crust.
In a heavy medium saucepan, bring 
the brown sugar, cream, butter, corn 
syrup, and salt to a boil over 
medium-high heat, stirring until 
smooth. Let the mixture continue to 
boil, without stirring, until a candy 
thermometer registers 240°F, about 
6 more minutes. Turn oﬀ  the heat and 
immediately (but carefully) pour the 
caramel evenly over the prepared 
crust. Let the bars cool completely, 
about 2 hours, before garnishing with 
the ganache.
MAKE THE GANACHE
Put the chocolate in a small heat-
proof bowl. In a small saucepan, 
bring the cream to a boil. Remove 
from the heat and pour over the 
chocolate. Let sit for 3 minutes. Stir 
gently with a silicone spatula until 
combined and smooth.
Shape parchment paper into a cone, 
ﬁ ll it with the ganache, and drizzle 
the chocolate decoratively over the 
caramel bars. Let the ganache set 
for 30 minutes to an hour. Carefully 
lift the bars from the pan using the 
parchment sides, and transfer them to 
a cutting board. Slide the parchment 
out from under the bars. Cut the bars 
into 1½-inch squares. Store at room 
temperature in a covered container 
for several days and up to a week.
Turtle Bars Yields about 4 dozen 1½-inch-square bars

 11/8   oz. (about ¼   cup) all-purpose

2   Tbs. finely grated orange

 ¼  tsp. table salt
 
 Butter, for the pan
 
  Mixed berries (optional)
Position a rack in the center of the 
oven and preheat the oven to 325°F.  
Wrap the bottom and sides of a

set aside.
Combine the following ingredients in 
this order: sugar, egg yolks, ricotta, 
mascarpone, ﬂ our, baking powder, 
orange zest, Cointreau, and salt in a 
food processor or blender, adding one 
at a time, pulsing brieﬂ y, and scraping 
down the bowl after each addition.

Put the egg whites in a separate

Serve the cake at room temperature,

ONE READER’S SEASONAL SPECIALTY
What we’re cooking now
Fine Cooking editors and a reader share 
some delicious ideas for in-season ingredients.
AT THE MARKET
Mashed Turnips and Carrots
Boil turnips and carrots with fresh ginger and cinnamon, then 
mash with butter and a little yellow mustard. Great with corned beef 
and cabbage or a smoked shoulder.
 
 
 
 
 
—Leasa Walker, Austin, Texas
Candied Meyer
Lemon Peel
Pare the zest from Meyer lemons in 
lengthwise strips, leaving behind as 
much of the white pith as possible, 
then slice the zest into thin strips. 
Blanch and drain the strips twice. 
Bring water and granulated sugar 
to a boil, stirring until the sugar 
dissolves. Add the peel to the syrup, 
and cook until the syrup thickens 
and coats the peel, about 3 minutes. 
Drain the peel, and toss in granu-
lated sugar. Transfer to a baking 
rack to dry. Excellent in cocktails 
and for baking.     —Diana Andrews
Maple-Roasted Endive
Peel off any wilted outer leaves 
and trim the bottom of the endive, 
leaving the root end intact. Cut in 
half lengthwise, toss with oil, and 
roast cut side down in a hot oven 
until just tender. Flip the endive, 
drizzle with pure maple syrup, 
sprinkle with goat cheese and 
chopped cashews, and return to 
oven until the cheese is melted and 
the nuts are lightly toasted. 
 
           —William Stewart
Hot Pickled 
Jerusalem Artichokes
Bring white-wine vinegar, pepper 
flakes, toasted fennel seeds, and 
salt to a boil. Season to taste with 
sugar, then add peeled and thickly 
sliced Jerusalem artichokes. Allow 
to cool. Transfer to canning jars, 
and refrigerate for up to two weeks. 
Great with grilled fish.   —Ronne Day
Baked Kohlrabi Fries
Peel and cut kohlrabi into thick 
matchsticks. In a large bowl, toss 
the kohlrabi with olive oil, a dash of 
white-wine vinegar, minced garlic, 
grated Parmigiano-Reggiano, a 
sprinkle of kosher salt, and a good 
grind of black pepper. Arrange in a 
single layer on a lightly oiled rimmed 
baking sheet, and bake in a hot oven 
until browned.                      —Lisa Lahey
Roasted Radicchio with 
Shiitake Mushrooms in 
Honey Glaze
In a large bowl, combine thin rib-
bons of radicchio and coarsely 
chopped shiitake mushrooms. 
Drizzle with olive oil, reduced-
sodium soy sauce, and honey. Stir 
to combine. Transfer to an oven-
proof dish, and roast in a hot oven 
until the radicchio is wilted and 
the shiitake are browned. Season 
to taste and serve.  
—Tanya Steel
Pineapple-Lime Infused 
Vodka Spritzer 
In a quart-size mason jar, add 
chopped pineapple and lime 
slices. Fill the jar with vodka. Cover 
tightly with a lid, and refrigerate 
for at least 3 days and up to 1 week. 
Strain the vodka through a fine-
mesh strainer into a bowl, then 
return the vodka to the jar. Com-
bine sugar and lime zest, and coat 
the rim of a glass. Serve the vodka 
over ice, topped with seltzer.
—Mary Jo Romano

Holiday braised and glazed lamb shank

Serves: 4-8 | Cook Time: 2 hr 30 min

4 Aussie lamb shanks 3/4 vanilla bean, split down the center
2 tbsp + 1 tbsp salt 1 cup scotch

2 tbsp vegetable oil 1 cup cabernet

1 onion, peeled and cut in quarters 1 orange

2 tsp juniper berries 1/2 cup of fresh or frozen cranberries

1. Season the four shanks with the 2 tbsp of
salt, cover and allow to rest overnight in
refrigerator.

2. Preheat the oven to 300°F.

3. On a stove top, preheat a large Dutch oven
or braising pan on high. Add the 2 tbsp of
vegetable oil. Sear the shanks until golden
brown on all sides. Remove from pan and
sear onions on all flat sides until golden.
Remove onions and add dry spices to toast
for a few seconds until aromatic. Remove
from heat and carefully add scotch, and
cabernet wine.

4. Return to heat and bring to boil. Juice and
peel one orange into the pan.

—

Add cranberries. Return the shanks and
onions to the pan and gently reduce the
liquid in the pan until almost dry. Add beef
stock. Cover and place the pan in the oven
for about 2 hours. Remove shanks from pan
when fork tender.

5. Preheat oven to broil. Reduce all braising
liquid to nappe. (Nappe consistency is
achieved when the sauce reaches a
thickness that allows it to coat the food
evenly. It should not be too thick or too thin.)
Mount all butter, one dice at a time.

6. Glaze shanks and reheat on broil until bubbly
and sticky. Re-glaze and serve.

One of the last meals my paternal 
grandmother ever made for me was her ethereal chicken 
soup. Unexpectedly, the broth that aft ernoon contained 
slices of sautéed shiitake mushrooms and fl ecks of sun-
dried tomato. “I saw them at the market,” she shrugged 
upon setting the steaming bowl in front of me. 
I shouldn’t have been surprised. By that point, Grandma, 
at 91 years of age, had served up a lifetime of staggeringly 
good meals. And though she wouldn’t know umami from 
Miami, she knew intuitively how to make good things taste 
even better. She watched me eat—while, of course, insisting 
that I eat more—and that meal, like so many of hers, 
reminded me that it’s OK to disrupt traditions, to gently 
push them forward and in new directions. 
So I’m sure Grandma would have loved this Hanukkah 
menu, with its respectful twists on traditional off erings. 
Th e challah toasts that lead off  the meal, for example, 
are reminiscent of the classic Sunday bagel, albeit one 
that’s gussied up for a holiday soirée. Likewise, here, the 
latkes—and what Hanukkah spread would be complete 
without lacy, crispy, salty latkes?—are served along with

Buying basics
It’s important to shop somewhere you trust. Find a 
place staffed by friendly, knowledgeable cheesemon-
gers who are generous with advice and tastes. Ask 
them for stories or fun facts to pass along to those 
sharing your platter.
Aim for three to five cheeses with a range of tex-
tures and milk types. Three is plenty if the board will 
accompany other dishes in a multicourse meal. Opt 
for five if cheese will be the star of the show. (If you 
include too many, your guests won’t know where to 
start and certainly won’t remember the names of all 
those wonderful cheeses.)
Buy about 1 oz. per person per cheese, maybe a bit 
more if cheese is all that’s being served. 
Include at least one entry-level cheese like a 
creamy Brie or an aged Cheddar, along with some-
thing most people in the room won’t be familiar 
with, like Challerhocker, a creamier cousin of Gru-
yère, or Caveman Blue, a balanced, toasty blue from 
Rogue Creamery in Oregon. 
Arranging the platter
Make sure to take your cheese out of the fridge a couple 
of hours before serving; it should be at room temperature 
when you serve it.
Be creative, and have fun with your board choice. 
The serving vehicle can often be as much a source of 
conversation as the cheese itself. Serve your assort-
ment on a wooden cutting board, a favorite serving 
plate, or a cheese board or slate. 
When plating, leave oozier cheeses in one piece for 
your guests to serve themselves, but cut a small starter 
piece, including the rind, to signal that rind should be 
included in the bite. If it’s a creamy round packaged in a 
box or wrapped in spruce bark, you can remove the top 
rind and allow guests to dip into the cheese. 
For hard cheeses, it’s nice to portion for your guests 
so that it’s easy to grab a piece. When portioning, 
equal rind distribution is key. Don’t stress over it, 
though. If a cheese seems to want to crumble, use a 
paring knife to create chunks, rather than pieces.
Choosing accompaniments
Classic companions include honey, jam, cornichons, 
olives, cured meat, figs, pears, or dried fruits and 
nuts, particularly Marcona almonds. But don’t be 
afraid to try something new. The most memorable 
cheese boards are playful and unstuffy. Try bacon 
with a washed-rind cheese like Époisses, chocolate 
with an aged Gouda, or gingersnaps as the “crackers” 
for blue cheese. 
As far as actual crackers and bread go, aim for 
variety. Offer a neutral option, such as a baguette or 
water cracker, and a more complex, texture-driven 
one, such as crostini, seeded crackers, or miche.

Cheese tastes best when it’s 
between 66°F and 71°F. Much 
colder, and the flavors will be 
muted; much warmer, and the 
texture suffers. Fortunately, it’s 
easy to bring cheese to the ideal 
temperature: Just take hard 
cheeses out of the fridge an 
hour before serving and softer 
cheeses a half-hour before.
There are no definite rules when 
it comes to pairing wines with 
your cheese platter, but here are 
a few general guidelines: Harder, 
nutty cheeses with more intense 
flavors will stand up well to the 
tannins in full-bodied red wines; 
fruit-forward wines like Merlot 
are good with smoked cheeses; 
full-bodied, buttery white wines, 
such as Chardonnay, comple-
ment sharp, semihard cheeses 
like Asiago; crisp, acidic white 
wines like Sauvignon Blanc bal-
ance out buttery, rich cheeses; 
and sweet wines, like Riesling, 
make a good counterpoint to 
the saltiness of blue cheeses. 
Blue cheeses tend to be salty, so 
if you decide to include a blue on 
your board, consider pairing it 
with something sweet, like honey, 
caramel, chocolate, sweet-tart 
jams, dried fruit, candied nuts, 
or slices of artisan bread that 
are studded with fruit and/or 
nuts. In fact, sweet things go very 
well with other salty cheeses, 
too, including hard ones like 
Parmigiano-Reggiano and funky 
washed-rind varieties like Taleg-
gio, Limburger, and Époisses.
When you’ve gone to the trouble 
of hunting down and splurging 
on amazing cheeses, you want 
to be sure the flavors of the 
cheeses really shine. So it’s wise 
to skip bread and crackers with 
bold seasonings. Plain crusty 
bread and neutral crackers are 
the best bets.

A world of beans
green bean is a generic term for the skinny, long beans that are almost universally available in grocery 
stores.  But the world of beans is much wider and more colorful than this standby. Here are a few varieties with 
subtly diff erent textures, hues, and fl avors; all can be used interchangeably. 
Haricots vert
Literally “green bean” in French, 
this thinner, slightly longer 
version of the classic Ameri-
can variety is picked young, 
resulting in a more flavorful and 
tender veggie with a mild seed. 
A favorite of chefs, they are a 
little more expensive than the 
standard bean. 
 
Speckled (dragon tongue) 
beans
A beautiful mottled purple-
burgundy bean pod filled with 
pale green beans, these string-
less 6-inch-long wide wax beans 
are from the cranberry bean 
family. When young, their crisp 
exterior and nutty seed can 
be eaten whole. When mature, 
their bean pods can be shelled.
Chinese long beans
Known by many names (aspara-
gus bean, snake bean, pea bean, 
and yard-long bean), this variety 
can grow up to 3 feet long. Young, 
sweet, tender, and crisp, they 
are often paired with Southeast 
Asian ingredients. Choose them 
if you’re looking for an intense 
bean flavor.
Romano beans
About 6 inches long, wide, and 
flat, this variety, also known 
as the Italian flat bean, has a 
meaty, mild, somewhat grassy-
tasting seed. It can also be yel-
low or purple, and is stringless, 
making for easy access to its 
tender seeds. Best when lightly 
cooked, braised, or stir-fried.
Rattlesnake beans
An 8-inch heirloom pole bean, 
it’s popular for its purple-
speckled green appearance, 
its sweet  and mild flavor, and 
its ivory-hued pods that taste 
nutty and meaty when mature. 
When cooked, the beans turn a 
uniform green.
Yellow wax beans
Soft yellow in appearance, these 
beans taste similar to their 
green cousin, with a mild nutty 
seed, a crisp shell, and a string-
less pod. Available year-round, 
they make an attractive mix-in 
with the classic green variety.
Green beans
Also known as string or snap 
beans, this unripe common bean 
differs from others because it is 
harvested young and consumed 
whole, before the seeds have 
matured. Grown from the earth 
or on a pole, this is the most 
commonly consumed variety 
of bean.
Purple (violetta) beans
Few beans are as striking as this 
stringless variety, which has a 
dark purple shell and light green 
seeds. With the same flavor and 
texture as the green variety, 
they are essentially inter-
changeable. When cooked, the 
bean turns from purple to green.

YO: Simple means diff erent things to diff erent people. For 
some, simple cooking is all about being able to make a meal 
from beginning to end in 20 or 30 minutes. For others, though, 
it’s more about being able to make something well in advance of 
serving, if you have friends coming over or want to eat as soon as 
you get home. 
Th e title of my new book, Simple, refers to both the feel of the 
recipes in general—these are not complicated to make, I promise—
but also the letters in the word itself, which break down into six cat-
egories: the six ways in which I think cooking can be made simple. 
Th e letters in Simple each stand for one of these diff erent ways:
     is “short-on-time” recipes
        is all about the number of ingredients: “10 or less”
        is for “make-ahead” recipes
         stands for “pantry-led,” meaning you can look in your

or in the oven and forget about
         recipes are “easier than you think,” things like confit

but actually is so effortless
Yotam. Ottolenghi.

Ottolenghi’s cuisine delights those who

and merry man. A Mediterranean infl u-

but these days, he’s as likely to reach for

and preserved lemons. Like all adventur-

At his restaurants and in his own home,

FC: As you point out, every cook has their own idea of what’s 
simple and what’s not. What does simple cooking mean to you?
YO: It depends on the occasion and the day of the week, but for 
everyday cooking at home, I tend to be an S (“short-on-time”) 
cook. So I like to stop at my grocer’s on the way home from work, 
pick up some vegetables, and start from there. I don’t get thrown 
off by kids or friends being in the kitchen when I’m cooking. I just 
want to be able to prepare something in the evening in the amount 
of time it takes to have a glass of wine. My husband, Karl, tends to 
be a bit more organized than I, so when we have friends over, he’s 
the one who thinks ahead and does loads of prep before everyone 
arrives, when he has time to spare and has the kitchen to himself.
But it’s important to be flexible. Keeping your cool and keep-
ing things simple totally depends on all sorts of things: the  
occasion, the season, even the weather outside. If it’s pouring rain 
and no one is going anywhere, then it’s time to cook from the 
pantry, to bake, to stew, to pull from or to stock up the freezer.
FC: Any words of wisdom on simplicity for the Fine Cooking tribe?
YO: At the end of the day, I have found that the two best ways to 
keep things simple in the kitchen are to cook something that you 
would love to eat and to be yourself. 
Don’t try to be someone else in the kitchen. If you are super 
organized and like to be in control, then don’t think you can 
turn into a “spontaneous-cook-whilst-you-chat” kind of host just 
because you’ve seen someone else cooking like that and you like 
the vibe. And vice versa, if you’re happy chopping and stirring 
and tasting whilst chatting with friends, then don’t think this is 
any less special or “proper” than having it all ready and waiting 
when everyone comes. Be who you are. Enjoy your own food. 
And trust that if you think something tastes lovely and you’d love 
to eat it, then chances are everyone else will, too. 
Also, perhaps the key to it all: Shop well. It makes all the dif-
ference. If you start with good ingredients, you really are so far 
on your way to a delicious meal, especially when preparing great, 
simple food.
FC: Why are you excited about the book Simple? What unique 
goals did you hope to achieve here that you perhaps didn’t 
consider in your other titles?
YO: My aim with Simple was to produce an Ottolenghi cookbook 
for everyday use. Ottolenghi food has a reputation for being asso-
ciated with special occasions or weekend cooking. Although I am 
super happy with this association—I love that my food is synony-
mous with celebration—I wanted to create a book that people were 
going to reach for on a Monday night when home alone. 
I am so excited by the extent to which I think I’ve pulled this 
off. Every recipe in the book is simple in one or more ways without 
any compromise on the delivery of flavor, surprise, and delight. 
FC: What surprised you as you created the book? 
YO: I thought imposing these various “simple” limitations on my 
recipes—the number of ingredients or the time spent cooking, 
for example—was going to feel restrictive, but actually, it was 
really liberating. That was the biggest surprise. Less, it turns out, 
really can be more.
FC: How can a cook get ahead on a meal without compromis-
ing deliciousness?
YO: Sauces, stews, soups. These can be made two or three days 
ahead without any compromise to flavor. In fact, sauces and 
stews very often taste even better a day or two after making. 
Always hold back on the fresh herbs, though; add these at the last 
minute to preserve their freshness. Squeezes of lemon and lemon 
zest also should be held back until just before serving. And never 
underestimate the benefit of a final drizzle of olive oil. 
If a dish is uncooked—a chopped or leafy salad, for example—
I always do as much chopping and slicing as I can beforehand 
and just hold off on assembly until ready to serve. 
FC: Can a cook limit the number of ingredients without invit-
ing blandness?
YO: Absolutely! If the aim is to avoid blandness, there are some 
real flavor bombs that can inject a huge amount into all sorts of 
dishes: The finely chopped skin of a preserved lemon, for example, 
is an ingredient that can be added to all sorts of things, from 
yogurt sauce to green salad to pasta, and the result will be the very 
opposite of bland. Rose harissa (used in the recipe on p. 79) is 
another ingredient like this. There are many more: miso, tamarind, 
black olives, tahini. Any one of these really packs a punch.
FC: Brieﬂy remind us of the importance of a well-stocked 
pantry. And also of the value of popping in at the market on the 
way home to buy just what you need or crave.
 
YO: This is the yin and yang of simple cooking. It’s really  
useful to make sure your cupboards are all stocked up with olive 
oil, pasta, cans of tomatoes, tins of anchovies and tuna, chick-
peas, tahini, spices, and so forth. Then, armed with some fresh 
fish or a couple of eggplants that you grabbed on the way home 
from work, you’re never far from an easy, comforting, and deli-
cious meal.
Yotam Ottolenghi’s other titles include Ottolenghi, Plenty,  
Jerusalem, Plenty More, NOPI, and Sweet. He also writes a 
regular column for the Guardian.

Baking is an exacting science, so it should 
come as no surprise that everything from 
the choice of fl our to the pan liner you 
use will aff ect the result. And while many 
recipes may lead you to believe that parch-
ment paper and silicone baking mats are 
interchangeable, the truth is that there are 
quite a few important diff erences between 
the two.
Silicone baking mats
Made of fl exible fi berglass coated in 
silicone, these are nonstick by nature. 
Candies and pralines are best made on 
these liners because hardened sugar is 
much more likely to stick to parchment 
than silicone. Th ese mats are thicker than 
parchment, providing more insulation 
on the bottom of what you’re baking, so 
cookies come out less browned on the 
bottom (but not necessarily less crisp 
since silicone also encourages spreading). 
Th is insulation creates a safer surface for 
baking delicate confections like fl ourless 
cakes and cookies. One last diff erence: 
Because they’re reusable, they’re a great 
eco-friendly option. 
Parchment paper
If you need to cut out a shape to fi t a pan, 
parchment is the product for you. It’s avail-
able in a variety of forms—rolls, precut rect-
angular sheets, and rounds for standard-size 
cake pans. Parchment is also the surface to 
bake browned-on-the-bottom cookies and 
ones that don’t spread, resulting in a soft er, 
cakier texture.                        —Nancy Duran

supply of olive oil. Miraculously, 
the oil lasted for eight nights. 
Th us, the tradition of using oil 
in the holiday’s foods is a potent 
symbol of the Jewish miracle. 
Latkes, doughnuts, and fritters 
are all typical Hanukkah dishes. 
Challah, while not exclusively 
tied to Hanukkah, is a common 
Jewish holiday and Shabbat 
bread. It’s baked most oft en with 
three dough braids, symbolizing 
truth, peace, and justice, and 
seeds such as poppy or sesame to 
symbolize manna from heaven. 
Th e result? One delicious and 
meaningful holiday table.  —T.S.
