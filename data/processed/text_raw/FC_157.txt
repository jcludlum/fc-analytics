NOTE FROM THE EDITOR
25th Birthday
In , when Fine Cooking published its 
fi rst issue, we ate diff erently—simpler, maybe, but not 
badly. Supermarkets started carrying ingredients like 
wonton wrappers, lemongrass, and goat cheese. Exotic 
“new” fl avors—Caribbean jerk spices, wasabi, and 
oil-cured olives—had arrived. Risottos abounded, and 
discussions of seasonality began to emerge. 
Fast forward 25 years, and we still enjoy the same foods, 
along with exponentially more. Supermarkets outdo them-
selves carrying the most diverse selection of produce and 
ethnic foods possible—plus you can order it all online. Eat-
ing locally, seasonally, and organically are the norm. Farm-
ers’ markets and food trucks have exploded in number, and 
words like sustainable, gluten-free, vegan, and grass-fed fi ll 
the marketplace. Th ings haven’t so much changed over the 
past 25 years as they’ve evolved. 
As I paged through past issues of Fine Cooking to put 
together this anniversary issue, the word evolve kept 
coming to mind. I discovered that topics covered in our 
fi rst issues might still be some of the same we’d cover 
today—but with a diff erent spin. For example, we might 
swap Hungarian goulash for Peruvian cazuela; French 
macarons for coconut macaroons; and feature a selec-
tion of small plates instead of a four-course Italian meal. 
I look forward to seeing how things like health, technol-
ogy, and personalization will impact the food world over 
the next 25 years. In the meantime, though, one thing will 
remain constant. We will always be grateful to our loyal 
readers and fans for giving us a chance to cook with you—
we consider it an honor and a joy, and that will never 
grow obsolete. 
                                 —Kathy Kingsley, editorial director

is earned through act-
ions, not talk. However

ONE READER’S SEASONAL SPECIALTY
What we’re cooking now
Fine Cooking editors and a reader share 
some delicious ideas for in-season ingredients.
AT THE MARKET
Cannellini Bean Paste with Fresh Rosemary 
Sauté shallots and garlic in olive oil over medium heat until softened. Add a 
drained and rinsed can of cannellini beans, fresh rosemary leaves, salt, pepper, and 
cayenne. With the back of a wooden spoon, smash the beans a bit while they simmer. 
Finish with a generous splash of lemon juice and a pat of butter. Thin with vegetable 
broth, if you like. Serve spread on crusty bread.
 
 
 
                                      —Susan McIlnay, York, Pennsylvania
Cara Cara 
Orange-Glazed Bacon
Put 10 bacon slices on a rack over a foil-
lined rimmed baking sheet, and bake 
at 375°F for 10 minutes. Meanwhile, 
combine orange zest and juice from 
2 medium oranges with brown sugar, 
and boil until reduced to a glaze. Drain 
the fat from the baking sheet, and put 
the bacon directly on the foil. Brush both 
sides with glaze, and bake another 6 to 
8 minutes, flipping once. Serve sprinkled 
with more zest.                —Nancy Duran
Fennel & Sausage 
Sheet-Pan Dinner
Toss 1-inch-thick fennel-bulb slices 
and halved baby potatoes with olive oil, 
salt, and pepper. Arrange on a baking 
sheet with sweet Italian sausage links, 
skins pricked. Roast at 425°F until the 
fennel and potatoes are tender and the 
sausages are cooked through. Add a 
handful of grape tomatoes to the pan 
during the last 10 minutes of cooking.
 
           —Sarah Breckenridge
Brussels Sprout Hash
In a large skillet, cook bacon until crisp. 
Keeping the fat in the pan, transfer the 
bacon to a plate lined with paper towels. 
Into the same pan, add chopped shallot, 
minced garlic, and chopped trimmed 
Brussels sprouts, and sauté over 
medium-high heat until tender. Add 
a splash of apple-cider vinegar and 
stir. Add chopped cooked red potatoes 
and the bacon, crumbled. Sprinkle with 
sea salt, and serve with a drizzle of bal-
samic vinegar.                    —Tanya Steel
Grated Carrot Salad
Grate carrots on the large holes of a 
box grater. Roughly chop a handful of 
flat-leaf parsley. In a medium bowl, make 
a vinaigrette with extra-virgin olive oil, 
fresh lemon juice, Dijon mustard, honey, 
kosher or sea salt, and a few grinds of 
black pepper. Toss the carrots and pars-
ley in the dressing and serve.
 
             —Kimberly Y. Masibay
Sweet Potato Toast
Bake a sweet potato, and then scoop out 
and mash the flesh with unsalted butter, 
and salt and pepper to taste. Spread the 
warm mashed sweet potato on a slice 
of toasted rustic boule, sprinkle with 
chopped dried cranberries and toasted 
pecans, and drizzle with pure maple 
syrup. 
 
     —Chris Hoelck
Celery Citrus Salad
Combine very thinly sliced celery with 
shaved fennel, fresh cilantro leaves, 
and Mandarin orange segments. Toss 
with extra-virgin olive oil, a splash of 
balsamic vinegar, and salt and pepper to 
taste. Top with thinly shaved Asiago and 
chopped glazed pecans.
 
 
—Diana Andrews
Mashed Rutabaga 
and Apple
Cut 1 peeled medium rutabaga and 
1 peeled and cored Granny Smith apple 
into small pieces. Add both to a large 
pot, cover with salted water, and boil 
until the rutabaga is tender, about 
40 minutes. Drain well and mash with 
unsalted butter, cream, and salt and 
pepper to taste.           —Kathy Kingsley

It’s Fine Cooking’s 25th anniversary! We’re 
joining the party with a twist on this classic 
dessert. Make tender, buttery biscuits on 
easy-to-bake and clean If You Care 
Non-Stick Parchment Paper, layer them 
with fresh seasonal fruit and a sweet

If You Care Non-Stick Parchment Paper

SHORT CAKES
FOR THE SHORTCAKES
2 cups unbleached all-purpose ﬂ our; more 
for rolling
1/3 cup plus 1 Tbs. granulated sugar
2½ tsp. baking powder
¼ tsp. baking soda
½ tsp. kosher salt
4 oz. (½ cup) cold unsalted butter, 
cut into  ½-inch pieces
1 large egg
¼ cup heavy cream; more for brushing
¼ cup buttermilk
FOR THE FILLING AND TOPPING
3 cups diced fresh pineapple, roasted if desired
3 cups orange segments
½ cup pomegranate seeds
2 Tbs. granulated sugar
3 cups whipped heavy cream
FOR THE CHOCOLATE SAUCE
½ cup heavy cream
4 oz. semisweet chocolate, ﬁ nely chopped
Make the biscuits-Heat the oven to 425°F. 
Line a large baking sheet with If You Care 
Parchment Paper.
Combine the ﬂ our, 1/3 cup of the sugar, the 
baking powder, and baking soda into a large 
bowl. Stir in the salt. Using a pastry blender, 
a fork, or your ﬁ ngertips, work the butter into 
the dry ingredients until the mixture resembles 
coarse cornmeal.
In a small bowl, beat the egg and heavy cream 
with a fork. Mix in the buttermilk. Make a well 
in the center of the ﬂ our mixture and pour in 
the cream mixture. Mix with the fork until the 
dough is evenly moistened and just comes 
together; it will still look a little shaggy. Gather 
the dough and gently knead it three or four 
times. If the dough seems dry and doesn’t 
form a cohesive mass, work in more cream, 
1 tsp. at a time. 
Transfer the dough to a lightly ﬂ oured surface 
and roll it into a 3/4-inch-thick disk. Using a 
2½-inch biscuit cutter, cut the dough into 
rounds and transfer to the prepared baking 
sheet. Gather the dough scraps, gently knead 
them together, re-roll, and cut out more 
biscuits until you have a total of 6.
Lightly brush the biscuit tops with cream and 
sprinkle with the remaining 1 Tbs. sugar. Bake 
until the biscuit tops are lightly browned, 
10 to 15 minutes. 
Make the ﬁ lling-In a medium bowl, combine 
the pineapple, oranges, pomegranates and 
sugar. Cover and chill until ready to serve.
Make the sauce-Heat  the cream in a small 
saucepan until just below the boiling point. Put 
the chocolate into a medium bowl and pour 
over the hot cream. Let stand 3 minutes, then 
stir with a rubber spatula until smooth.
Assemble the shortcakes-Using a serrated 
knife, split the warm biscuits in half horizon-
tally and transfer the bottoms to 6 dessert 
plates. Spoon the fruit mixture with juices 
over the biscuit bottoms. Top with a dollop of 
whipped cream and cover each with a biscuit 
top. Spoon the chocolate sauce into a parch-
ment cone, and pipe it over the shortcakes.
Serves 6

shortcakes through a parchment

To really impress your guests, pres-
ent this fish whole, nestled on a bed of

¼  cup olive oil

(about 10)
 
  Kosher salt and freshly ground  
white pepper
 1½ oz. (3 Tbs.) unsalted butter
 
1   large sweet onion, thinly sliced  
lengthwise (about 2½ cups)
 
1   small fennel bulb, cored and thinly  
sliced lengthwise (about 2¾ cups),  
1 tsp. finely chopped fronds reserved

parts only, cut in half lengthwise,

(about 1½ cups)
 1¼  cups dry white wine

1   tsp. coarsely chopped

 Mojo de Ajo (recipe follows)
 
 Flaky sea salt, for serving
 
 Lemon wedges, for serving
Position a rack in the center of the oven, and 
heat the oven to 425°F.
Line a large rimmed baking sheet with foil. 
Brush the foil with 1½ Tbs. oil. Line the sheet with 
the tomatoes in a single layer, season gener-
ously with salt and pepper, and set aside. 
Add 1 Tbs. oil and the butter to a large skillet 
over medium-high heat. When the butter is 
melted, add the onion, and stir occasionally 
until it begins to soften, about 3 minutes. Add 
the sliced fennel and leek, stirring occasionally 
until the vegetables are soft and translucent 
but not browning, about 6 minutes more (if 
the mixture begins to brown, lower the heat to 
medium and stir in 1 to 2 Tbs. water). 
Add the wine and cook, stirring frequently, 
until most of the wine evaporates, 4 to 5 minutes. 
Lower the heat to medium, add the mascarpone 
and heavy cream, and stir until well combined. 
Remove from the heat and stir in the zest, dill, tar-
ragon, fennel fronds, 1 tsp. salt, and ½ tsp. pepper. 
Let cool to room temperature. 
Pat the fish dry inside and out with paper 
towels. Generously salt and pepper the inside 
of the cavity. Spoon the leek-fennel mixture 
into the cavity, and tie the fish lightly with 
kitchen twine. Transfer to the bed of toma-
toes, and brush the remaining 1½ Tbs. oil 
over the fish. Roast until the fish turns golden 
brown in places and the eyes turn opaque, 
45 to 50 minutes. Remove from the oven, and 
gently baste with some of the juices from the 
pan. Let rest on the pan for 10 minutes, then 
remove the string, and transfer to a large serv-
ing platter. Serve with the Mojo de Ajo, flaky sea 
salt, and lemon wedges on the side.

with friends and family. Each dish is rich in symbolism. Th e reci-

Stock up
Chef Ming Tsai’s Red-Roast Chicken or Duck with 
Sweet Potatoes on p. 65 features a master stock, a classic 
component of Cantonese and Southern Chinese cook-
ing that helped defi ne this great cuisine for centuries. 
Th e stock is generally made with chicken or other fowl 
poached in a broth featuring soy sauce, rock sugar, 
wine, and aromatics such as cinnamon, ginger, and star 
anise. Th e resulting liquid has a deeply meaty, fragrant 
fl avor and a gorgeous reddish-brown hue. 
Notably, it need not be discarded aft er each use. 
On the contrary, it is meant to be used repeatedly, 
with its fl avor becoming more complex and robust 
with each use, thus its ambitious nickname “thousand-
year sauce.” Aft er each poaching, the stock must be 
brought back to a boil, skimmed, strained of solids, 
and cooled quickly to avoid contamination. It can then 
be refrigerated for up to three days or frozen for up to 
one month. Each time it’s brought back up to the boil, 
its life cycle is extended. In this way, a family master 
stock can literally be passed down from generation to 
generation, connecting descendants to long-departed 
ancestors through the magic of food. —Nancy Duran

Buffalo chicken wings take flight
While there are as many claimants to the Buffalo chicken wing as 
there are versions of the recipe, it’s generally agreed that the 
first version was made in 1964 at the Anchor Bar in Buffalo, New

bar snack for late-night patrons. In a panic, she threw some 
wings into a deep fryer, then jazzed them up by coating them in 
a cayenne hot sauce. The recipe has been flying off the menu 
(ha, ha) ever since.

Does sifting matter?
Like so many cake recipes, the White Chocolate Layer Cake on p. 84
calls for sift ed fl our. But most of us ignore that step, not wanting

get out the sift er for baking perfection.

25. Roasting pan: These are critical at Thanksgiving; we like heavy-gauge stainless steel with handles for its perfect conductivity.

INGREDIENT
You’ve heard chefs mention 
it but likely not made it yourself. Simply 
put, clarifi ed butter is butter minus its 
water content and milk solids. Its intensely 
pure fl avor makes it the perfect choice for 
fi nishing a dish, where its rich, creamy taste 
can really shine through. Th at’s chef Chris 
Montero’s motivation for using it in his 
sensational Crystal Hot Wings on p. 80.  
Luckily, it’s super easy to make: Start 
with unsalted butter in a saucepan over low 
heat. Cook gently to gradually evaporate 
the water in the butter. As the butter gently 
simmers, the milk solids start to foam and 
rise to the top. Th ey appear as a white layer 
over the golden liquid butterfat. Skim these 
off  the top of the liquid and reserve for an-
other use (such as hollandaise), or discard. 
Some milk solids will also fall to the bottom 
of the pan. When you pour off  the butterfat, 
they will be left  sitting on the bottom. Th e 
liquid butterfat can be strained through a 
cheesecloth-lined strainer at this point to 
remove any remaining traces of milk fat. 
Store in the refrigerator for a month or 
in the freezer for many months. Because 
the lower-burning-point milk solids and 
water are fi ltered out, clarifi ed butter can 
be used in high-temperature cooking, such 
as frying.
Clarifi ed butter’s close cousin, ghee, is a 
popular ingredient in Indian cooking. It’s 
essentially clarifi ed butter that has been 
cooked further, caramelizing it, deepening 
its color, and imparting a pleasing nutty fl a-
vor. Ghee is best used in high-temperature 
cooking.                                              —N.D.

chicken dishes; apples and applesauce 
can be found throughout Northern 
French cuisine.
On the breakfast table  
Soaking oats in applesauce overnight

big flavor, leaving you with a grab-and-
go breakfast in the morning. It’s an 
easy, tasty way to start the day.  —N.D.
