NOTE FROM THE EDITOR
Americans are known for their 
fieeting iove affairs with cook-
ing gadgets—spiralizers, air fryers, 
fondue sets. Who remembers the salad 
shooter? But the one piece of cookware 
that we’ve had an enduring relationship 
with is the cast-iron skillet.
It’s the pan our grandmothers and 
their mothers cooked with, many proudly 
passed down through generations 
along with storied cooking traditions. 
My treasured cast-iron skillets are not 
family heirlooms, but were found in the 
basement of a house we purchased many 
years ago. I like to imagine, however, that 
they have their own stories of Grandma’s 
fried chicken, Sunday-morning bacon, 
and birthday upside-down cakes—it’s 
probably not a stretch. 
For me, nothing compares to the ebony 
patina of a well-seasoned cast-iron skillet. 
It’s what makes the surface nonstick, and it 
comes only from repeated use and careful 
upkeep. Th e pan also maintains high heat, 
is nearly indestructible, and can go from 
stovetop to oven without a thought. Th e 
only disadvantages of the cast-iron pan are 
its heft  and its need for regular mainte-
nance to keep it seasoned and looking 
nice, but that’s surprisingly easy to do and 
certainly well worth the eff ort.
Th is issue pays tribute to America’s 
favorite cookware with a host of recipes 
and a special section on seasoning and 
caring for cast iron (p. 84). Chef Geoff rey 
Zakarian shows us how to roast a chicken 
in a cast-iron skillet with juicy, perfect 
results and a pastrami-style rub, as well 
as two other fabulous methods for roast-
ing chicken (p. 46). Cookbook author 
and baking master Erin Jeanne McDow-
ell creates the puff y oven pancakes of 
your dreams, called Dutch babies—either 
sweet or savory, there’s one here for you 
(p. 68). And the one-skillet meal gets a 
thoroughly modern makeover from food 
writer and cookbook author Rukmini 
Iyer. She amps up textures and fl avors 
with her new spins, such as Lime-
Roasted Salmon with Avocado, Pome-
granate, and Bulgur (p. 67) and Crispy 
Peanut-Chile Chicken with Sweet Pota-
toes (p. 66). 
Th ere’s a lot more in this issue beyond 
our shout-out to cast-iron cooking. Turn 
to p. 22 for all the tips and techniques 
you need to deliciously enjoy artichoke 
season; learn how to make brown butter 
(p. 72); slurp some comforting spicy 
soups from around the world (p. 56); and 
lighten up your carb intake without com-
promising fl avor by harnessing the power 
of caulifl ower to make everything from 
pizza crust to “tater” tots (p. 52). 
Certainly, some of the dishes above 
can be made in a cast-iron skillet as well, 
as there’s not much you can’t cook in a 
well-seasoned pan. Th e one thing that can 
destroy a good skillet is neglect. It only 
wants to be used regularly and cared for. In 
exchange, it will persevere for generations 
doing what it’s meant to do: Make meals 
and memories worth sharing. If you aren’t 
the current owner of a cast-iron skillet, 
then go out and buy one and start your 
own legacy—or at least make some darn 
good cornbread (p. 85).
—Kathy Kingsley, editorial director

ONE READER’S SEASONAL SPECIALTY
What we’re cooking now
Fine Cooking editors and a reader share 
some delicious ideas for in-season ingredients.
AT THE MARKET
Beet Smoothie
In a blender, combine 1 medium or 2 small roasted beets, Greek yogurt, 
fresh or frozen berries, 1 banana, and a handful or two of greens (such as 
spinach, chard, collards, or kale), and blend until smooth. Add a little milk 
or water to thin, if you like. To sweeten, add a drizzle of honey.
—Laura Spicer, Baltimore, Maryland
Scallion-Quinoa Salad
Toss together cooked and cooled mul-
ticolor quinoa with thinly sliced scallion 
greens, corn kernels, and a handful 
of quartered cherry tomatoes. Dress 
with peanut oil, rice vinegar, a dash of 
sesame oil, and fish sauce, and season 
to taste with salt and pepper. Marinate 
at room temperature for 30 minutes 
before serving.        —Diana Andrews
Cape Gooseberry and 
Orzo Salad
In a medium bowl, combine halved 
gooseberries with cooked orzo, 
chopped cucumber, and a handful 
of chopped fresh mint and basil. In a 
resealable jar, shake to combine lemon 
juice, a little Dijon mustard, extra-virgin 
olive oil, and a small minced shallot. Toss 
the salad with the dressing, and top with 
crumbled feta cheese and chopped 
roasted pistachios. Season to taste with 
salt and pepper.              —Nancy Duran
Carrot-Dill Purée
Sauté chopped onion, celery, and dill 
seed in canola oil until tender but 
not brown. Add sliced carrots, and 
cook for about 3 minutes. Cover with 
vegetable broth, bring to a simmer, 
and cook until the carrots are tender. 
Drain the carrots, then transfer to 
the bowl of a food processor. Pulse 
until almost smooth. Add a generous 
handful of coarsely chopped fresh 
dill, and process until smooth. Season 
to taste with kosher salt and freshly 
ground black pepper, and serve with 
broiled salmon or roast chicken. 
                               —Chris Hoelck
Miso Soup with Baby 
Turnips and Greens
Sauté quartered baby turnips until 
lightly browned on the cut sides. Add 
dashi or vegetable stock, and simmer 
until the turnips are tender. In a small 
bowl, whisk a small amount of the hot 
broth into some white miso paste, then 
whisk the mixture back into the soup. Stir 
in coarsely chopped turnip greens, and 
let sit until just wilted. Garnish with thinly 
sliced scallions.     —Sarah Breckenridge
Steamed White 
Asparagus with
Chive-Mustard Aïoli
Using a mortar and pestle, smash 
1 clove of garlic with kosher salt. 
Transfer to a small bowl, and mix in 
mayonnaise, Dijon mustard, snipped 
fresh chives, and fresh lemon juice. 
Trim a pound of white asparagus 
spears, then peel the stems with a 
potato peeler. Steam until tender, 
12 to 15 minutes. Serve with the aïoli.
                                
  —Kathy Kingsley
Baby Lettuce and 
Berry Salad
In a large bowl, whisk together maple 
syrup, raspberry vinegar, extra-virgin 
olive oil, sea salt, and freshly ground 
black pepper. Add sliced strawberries or 
a mix of your favorite berries, and mac-
erate for 10 minutes. Add mixed baby 
lettuces and watercress or baby arugula. 
Toss gently and season to taste with 
salt and pepper. Sprinkle the salad with 
toasted sliced almonds and crumbled 
goat cheese.           —Kimberly Y. Masibay

I grew up near the coast of North-
ern Caiifornia, where artichokes 
thrive in the cooi ocean ciimate.
They were (and still are) easy to find at  
roadside farmstands and on restaurant 
menus. As a kid, I dipped the leaves one by 
one into melted butter at family dinners  
and enjoyed them in creamy artichoke 
soup with hunks of sourdough bread at 
Duarte’s Tavern on Highway 1. I heard tales 
of how my Italian great-grandparents grew 
artichokes in their backyards and how my 
grandfather was sent to “summer camp” 
outside of San Francisco, but it wasn’t camp 
at all; it was a stint at his uncle’s farm in Half 
Moon Bay, where he was put to work pick-
ing artichokes. 
Clearly, artichokes were a big part of 
my childhood. Yet the process of turning 
a thistle into a delicious treat remained a 
mystery to me until I took on professional 
cooking jobs that required me to learn how 
to quickly break down artichokes and pre-
pare them in myriad ways.
Now, as a chef and cookbook author,  
I demonstrate vegetable butchery and  
produce-inspired cooking around the coun-
try. And I’ve noticed that artichokes, more 
than any other vegetable, intimidate both 
seasoned cooks and amateurs. No matter 
the city, season, or theme of the course I’m 
teaching, I am invariably asked this question: 
“How do you break down an artichoke?”
You’re not alone if you, too, deem the 
artichoke unapproachable and confounding. 
They certainly are impenetrable and inedible 
at first, and they give us no clues on how to 
approach them. It takes work to trim away 
all those thorn-tipped leaves and to reveal 
and remove that fuzzy choke. The reward of 
meaty leaves and the tender, nutty heart hid-
ing within, though, is worth the fuss. 
I promise that once you learn how to 
select and store artichokes and understand 
how to easily prepare them, cooking arti-
chokes will become routine and even fun. 
You’ll find countless ways to enjoy them and 
make new food memories of your own.

PREPARE THE ARTICHOKES
Remove small and tough leaves from the base and stem of the artichoke.
Prepare the artichokes for steaming as directed on p. 26, squeez-
ing lemon juice onto the cuts to prevent browning.
Set a collapsible steamer basket in a large pot, and add enough 
water to skim the bottom of the basket. Bring the water to a boil over 
high heat. Stand the artichokes stem end up in the steamer; fit them 
snugly, if necessary. Sprinkle the artichokes with salt. Reduce the 
heat to medium, allowing the water to maintain a low boil. Steam, 
covered, until the outer leaves release easily when pulled, and the 
heart and stem are completely tender and can be pierced by a 
paring knife without resistance, 20 to 30 minutes for medium-size 
artichokes, and 30 to 40 minutes for large artichokes. Add more 
water to the pot while cooking, if needed.
MAKE THE DIP
In a small skillet, heat 2 Tbs. of the oil over medium-high heat. Add the 
garlic and pepper flakes, and cook, stirring the garlic and tilting the pan 
to allow the garlic to simmer in a pool of oil at the edge of the pan, until 
tender and just beginning to turn golden (do not let it brown or burn), 
1½ to 2 minutes. Immediately remove from the heat.
In a food processor, blend the yogurt and hot oil. Scrape down 
the bowl, and add the remaining 1 Tbs. of oil, the tarragon, orange 
zest and juice, shallot, and ¼ tsp. fine sea salt; blend until smooth.

the dip to a small bowl, sprinkle the artichokes with flaky sea salt, 
and serve.

SELECTION & 
STORAGE
Artichokes are the edible immature flower 
of a large thistle. In the U.S., they’re grown 
along the northern California coast, where 
the crop thrives in the cool coastal cli-
mate. Spring is peak season for artichokes, 
with a secondary harvest in the fall. 
At the market, look for artichokes that 
are vibrant in color, heavy for their size, 
and have tightly closed leaves. To deter-
mine freshness, rub the leaves with your 
fingers: They will squeak if they’re fresh, 
and the small outer leaves around the base 
will snap. Avoid artichokes with browned, 
black, dry, or split leaves. Artichokes lose 
moisture soon after harvest. Buy them as 
fresh as possible, and use them right away, 
ideally no longer than a few days after pur-
chase. Store  in a sealed plastic bag in the 
coldest part of your refrigerator.
GREEN GLOBE
This is the most common 
variety found in the U.S., 
with medium to large 
heads and meaty leaves. 
It’s grown commercially 
on the California coast.
ITALIAN AND 
FRENCH PURPLE
Varietals such as Siena and 
Fiesole are often wine-
colored and tender-hearted 
because they’re harvested 
young. Some types are 
elongated, others rotund.
BABY
Young artichokes, 
also found on the 
California coast, have 
small heads, tender 
inner leaves, and an 
undeveloped choke.
VIOLETTA
This heirloom purple 
artichoke originated in 
Italy and is prized for 
its mild flavor and 
tenderness.

7 EASY WAYS 
with ARTICHOKES
STEAM: Cover and steam whole until the 
leaves and heart are tender, 20 to 30 min-
utes for medium artichokes, and 30 to 
40 minutes for large artichokes; dip into aïoli 
or vinaigrette (see p. 23).
BOIL: Drop whole trimmed artichokes into 
a large pot of boiling salted water with one to 
two lemon halves. Top with a heavy heatproof 
plate to keep the artichokes submerged. Lower 
the heat to a strong simmer until the leaves are 
tender and the heart can be pierced easily with a 
paring knife, 10 to 30 minutes, depending on size.
GRILL: Quarter and steam artichokes until 
just tender. Marinate in olive oil, lemon or orange 
juice, balsamic vinegar, garlic, and herbs. Grill 
about 5 minutes on each side. Serve with more 
of the marinade, and top with flaky sea salt.
RAW: Trim and peel raw baby artichokes 
down to the pale green-yellow leaves. Thinly 
slice or shave them; toss with your best 
olive oil, lemon zest and juice, Parmigiano-
Reggiano, spring herbs, and pine nuts or 
hazelnuts. Enjoy as a salad, or layer over 
ricotta-smeared toast. 
FRY: Dredge baby artichokes in semolina 
flour and breadcrumbs, and fry until crisp and 
tender. Finish with a generous sprinkle of flaky 
sea salt, and dip into a creamy sauce or pesto. 
ROAST: Toss halved or quartered baby 
artichokes with olive oil, salt, and pepper; 
roast cut side down at 400°F until golden and 
crisp, tossing once halfway through cooking, 
about 25 minutes (see recipe at right).
BRAISE: Simmer halved or quartered, cov-
ered, in equal parts olive oil and white wine 
with sautéed mirepoix, thyme, and bay leaf 
until tender, 15 to 18 minutes. Remove the 
artichokes, and reduce the poaching liquid 
until thickened. Serve over polenta.

A good blend
Ganache is a fat-in-water emulsion. 
Technically, fat and water don’t mix, 
but when emulsifi ed, tiny droplets of 
cocoa butter from the chocolate and 
droplets of butterfat from the cream 
become dispersed and suspended in 
a syrup consisting mostly of water 
from the cream as well as melted sugar 
from the chocolate. Th e emulsifi ed 
chocolate and cream remain blended, 
creamy, and smooth. Cream itself is 
also a fat-in-water emulsion, consist-
ing of butterfat globules suspended in 
water. Casein, a group of proteins in 
milk, acts as an emulsifi er in cream, 
helping to keep the fat and water from 
separating. In ganache, casein also 
helps to keep the chocolate and cream 
emulsion smooth and creamy.
What’s ganache? 
Ganache is a velvety emulsion made by combin-
ing chocolate and heavy cream. When chilled, its 
consistency may be fi rm and nearly chewy; when 
warm, it fl ows. Depending on the ratio of choco-
late to cream, ganache can have a wide range of 
textures, allowing it to serve as the rich center of a 
truffl  e, a cake fi lling or decoration, a pastry glaze, a 
whipped mousse, or a chocolate sauce.
Th e ratio of chocolate to 
cream determines the 
consistency of ganache. 
At a ratio of two parts 
chocolate to one part 
cream, ganache cools to a 
fi rm and smooth texture, 
perfect for rolling into 
chocolate truffl  es. Change 
the ratio to equal parts 
chocolate and cream, 
and you’ll have medium-
consistency ganache, just 
right for glazing and fi lling 
cakes and pastries. At one 
part chocolate, two parts 
cream, you’ll have liquidy 
or soft  ganache, which 
can be whipped into a 
mousselike consistency. 
For the greatest precision, 
measure the chocolate and 
cream by weight rather 
than volume.

When chocolate meets cream
To make a uniformly smooth and creamy ganache, always add 
chopped or melted chocolate to warm cream, rather than the 
other way around. Stir with a rubber spatula for a bubble-free 
result that’s perfect for truffl  es and cake glazes. Or let it cool and 
set, then use a whisk to whip air into the ganache and make it 
fl uffi  er and thicker for cake fi llings and frostings. 
 Many classic ganache recipes call for adding hot cream to a 
bowl of chopped chocolate and stirring until smooth. However, 
this mixing method can cause the ganache to separate, giving 
you tiny specks of chocolate within the ganache. If you do add 
hot cream to chopped chocolate, it’s essential to let it stand 
undisturbed for a minute or so to give the chocolate a chance to 
melt before gently stirring. Th is will help to prevent tiny specks 
of chocolate from appearing in the fi nished ganache.
Chocolate is sensitive to tempera-
ture changes. Th e cocoa butter in 
chocolate melts at a relatively low 
temperature range of 87°F to 91°F, 
just below body temperature. 
Melted cocoa butter solidifi es again 
around 68°F. Notice that the dif-
ference between solid and melted 
chocolate can be as little as 20°F. 
For this reason, it’s best to melt 
chocolate gently and gradually with 
low and slow heat. To melt it evenly 
and smoothly, chop the chocolate 
into small, uniform pieces about 
½ inch square.
Ganache has the added 
advantage of warm liquid cream 
to help envelop the chocolate in 
even heat as it melts. Simply stir 
fi nely chopped chocolate into the 
warm cream until they blend into 
a smooth, creamy emulsion. Aft er 
mixing chopped chocolate into hot 
cream and stirring it until smooth, 
the fl uid mixture may still be quite 
warm. Due to chocolate’s tempera-
ture sensitivity, it’s also best to allow 
fl uid ganache to set and fi rm up 
gently and gradually. As it cools, 
cocoa-butter crystals begin to form 
and the ganache begins to set.
If you cool ganache too quickly, 
the cocoa butter doesn’t form as 
many crystals; when returned to 
room temperature quickly, cooled 
ganache may develop a greasy 
appearance. To ensure smooth, 
creamy ganache, cool it gradually.
Mind the temperature 
If your ganache looks grainy and curdled, the 
emulsion has broken. The fat is separating from 
the watery liquid, usually because there isn’t 
enough liquid to hold the amount of chocolate 
solids suspended in the mixture. To fix broken 
ganache, warm the mixture over a hot water 
bath while whisking vigorously. If that doesn’t 
work, vigorously whisk in a small amount of 
room-temperature milk or even a liqueur. Don’t 
use cream to restore your ganache, because 
the mixture already contains too much fat to 
come together.

Why the cocoa percentage matters 
Chocolate liquor, a key component in chocolate, is a mixture of cocoa 
solids and cocoa butter. When you’re making ganache, the higher the 
percentage of chocolate liquor in the chocolate, the richer and more 
chocolatey the fi nished fl avor will be. Chocolate around 70 percent, 
usually called bittersweet or dark, is ideal. Above 75 percent, the cocoa 
solids can absorb so much liquid from the cream that there is not 
enough left  to keep all the solids and cocoa butter suspended in emul-
sion, making the ganache prone to breaking and becoming greasy. 
As for cocoa butter, dark chocolate and white chocolate both contain 
about the same percentage, around 20 percent. But white chocolate has 
no cocoa solids (only milk solids), so when white chocolate melts, there 
are fewer solids to absorb the liquefying cocoa butter, which can make 
white chocolate ganache especially prone to greasiness if it’s overheated 
or heated too quickly.

Roast a chicken. It doesn’t get sim-

When it comes to roast chicken, it’s 
the details that separate the great from 
the ordinary. Heed these four simple 
pieces of advice for the best roast  
chicken you’ll ever make.
Always start with the best possible bird. You 
can’t go wrong with 3- to 3½-lb. kosher air-
chilled chicken. They’re terrific and easy to  
find almost everywhere these days. Air-chilling 
yields extremely tender meat with concentrated 
chicken flavor. 
Even though it’s a bit harder to find, the 
blue-footed chicken, or poulet bleu, is one of 
my favorite roasting birds. The meat is  
extremely tender and has big flavor. Originally 
from Canada, poulet bleu were virtually impos-
sible to find in the United States until recently. 
Lucky for us, flocks of blue-footed chickens are 
now being raised in California. 
Another roaster I love is Jidori chicken, 
a domestic mixed breed that’s sustainably 
raised and prized for its robust flavor. These 
hormone- and antibiotic-free birds are fed an 
all-grain diet and are never frozen.
Although they can be a bit pricey, these spe-
cialty chicken breeds offer clear differences in 
flavor, and I urge you to seek them out to find 
the one you prefer, even if you decide to save it 
for a special occasion.

Let the bird rest twice. Allowing the chicken 
to sit, salted or in a spice rub that includes salt, 
for at least 20 minutes before cooking is an 
important step on the path to perfect roasting. 
This first rest gives the salt time to absorb into 
the chicken, tenderizing the meat while drying 
out the skin so that it can crisp delightfully in 
the oven. The second rest happens after you 
remove it from the oven and should also last 
for at least 20 minutes. During this time, the 
juices redistribute throughout the meat instead 
of ending up on your carving board.
Last, but certainly not least, always remember 
to tent the chicken with foil if it’s browning  
too quickly in the oven. Burning the skin will  
ruin both the aesthetic and the flavor of the 
finished bird.

passage. Every cook should know how to do 
it—and do it well.

While pho may grab the limelight,

Restaurant chefs have the luxury of 
throwing all the saucepans, bowls, 
whisks, sheet pans, and skillets that 
they’ve used to create just one finished

If you can manage to create all the 
flavors and textures that you crave in a 
single pan, though, then why wouldn’t 
you? More and more, that’s the way I

Sweet or savory, Dutch babies are easy-to-make oven 
pancakes that are perfect any time of the day.
BY ERI N JEA N N E M CDOWE L L
If you haven’t tried making a Dutch baby, it’s 
time you do. This oversize puffy oven pancake is as 
comforting and homey as it is scrumptious and spectacu-
lar, making it well worth getting to know. 
A simple two-step technique gives this pancake its 
distinctive puffed-up appearance. First, the batter gets 
poured into a hot buttered skillet on the stovetop. Then, 
you pop the skillet into a hot oven, which causes the 
batter to inflate like a popover. Once you remove the pan 
from the oven, the Dutch baby deflates, creating crisp, 
puffy edges and a flat, slightly chewy center. 
Traditionally, Dutch babies are a breakfast treat, but 
Dutch babies aren’t just morning fare anymore. With 
a few tweaks, they can easily become lunch, dinner, or 
dessert. Certainly, it would be nice to start the day with a 
breakfast Dutch baby featuring a bacon-and-egg topping 
that says good morning in the best possible way. It would 
be equally delightful to enjoy an herb-packed Dutch baby 
topped with creamy mushrooms and leeks for lunch. And 
a weeknight supper is much sweeter with a chocolate 
Dutch baby topped with raspberries and whipped cream. 
The process is virtually foolproof, so you can’t go wrong 
with these recipes. And if you’re anything like me, once 
you’ve tried one version, you’ll want to taste them all. 
Erin Jeanne McDowell is a food stylist and recipe developer. Her 
latest cookbook is The Fearless Baker.

Brown butter, or beurre noisette, literally translates to 
hazelnut butter, which is an apt description for its nutty 
fl avor. Th ough brown butter is easy to make, timing is 
everything, and you should be prepared to engage all 
your senses. You’ll be able to see, hear, and smell when 
your brown butter is ready.
WATCH: Start by heating pieces of unsalted butter 
over low heat until completely melted. Increase the 
heat, and bring the butter to a gentle boil. As the butter 
cooks, it will turn a toasty golden brown. Th at’s one 
sign that the butter is done.
LISTEN: As the butter boils and the water in the 
butter begins to evaporate, it will start to hiss and spit. 
When the water boils away, the sound will die down, 
and the milk solids in the butter will start to brown.
SMELL: As the milk solids start to caramelize, the 
butter will develop an intoxicating sweet and nutty 
aroma.
When the butter has reached a beautiful caramel 
color, remove the pan from the heat, and immediately 
pour the butter into a heatproof bowl. You want to stop 
the cooking process quickly to prevent the butter from 
burning. At this point, you can use it immediately or 
refrigerate it until fi rm.
While brown butter plays an important role in 
myriad classic French recipes, you don’t need to stop 
there. Brown butter can enhance the fl avors of many 
dishes, including a springtime pasta with peas and 
mint (p. 76). It also weaves magic in desserts, 
intensifying the fl avors in an almond Bundt cake 
(p. 78) and pecan-studded shortbread (p. 77). 
Once you master this simple technique, you’ll 
defi nitely score major brown(ie) points with the nutty, 
complex fl avor of beurre noisette.

Beurre noisette
This is the French name for brown 
butter, made by melting unsalted 
butter, cooking it gently to evaporate 
the water and then over increasing 
heat to encourage the milk proteins 
floating on top or sunken to the bot-
tom of the pan to develop a golden-
brown color and nutty flavor and 
aroma. Favorite companions include 
eggs, pastas, fish, cookies, and cakes. 
Beurre noir
Black butter takes brown butter to 
the very edge, just short of burn-
ing. Beurre noir is often paired 
with sweetbreads, as well as with 
fish and seafood dishes, includ-
ing skate, in French cuisine. Chefs 
often add lemon juice or vinegar 
to the pan to flavor the butter and 
stop the browning. 
Burro marrone
Italian for brown butter. Cooks 
in Italy love brown butter, most 
famously in the beloved, brilliant 
dish of ravioli or pasta with brown 
butter and fresh sage. 
Clarified butter
This culinary treasure is made 
by stopping short of browning 
the milk solids. Unsalted butter 
is melted and then skimmed or 
strained to remove any traces of 
milk solids floating on the top or 
adhering to the bottom of the pan 
in which the butter was melted. 
Chefs and cooks use it to provide 
butter’s rich flavor to ingredients 
cooked at higher temperatures 
than whole butter can endure. 
Drawn butter
Drawn butter is essentially melted 
butter, made as an accompa-
niment for steamed or boiled 
seafood. This luscious sauce is 
simplicity itself. Unsalted butter is 
melted and sometimes mixed with 
fresh lemon juice.
Ghee
A treasured ingredient in South 
Asian cuisines, ghee is cow’s milk 
butter melted and then slowly 
simmered to develop its toasty 
flavor and aroma. The resulting 
product remains shelf stable in 
tropical heat and is revered in 
Hindi kitchens for use in religious 
ceremonies as well as for cooking 
and seasoning food. 
Samneh
The same process used to make 
ghee is used by cooks in the Arab 
world to make samneh. These 
two clarified butters share the 
process of melting unsalted but-
ter, cooking off the water, and 
then simmering it for an extended 
time, up to 20 minutes, to allow 
for fuller but gentle browning and 
a rich, toasty flavor. Samneh is 
cooled and strained so that the 
finished product has no browned 
bits, though it retains a toasty, 
nutty flavor. Cooks throughout the 
Arab world use samneh in pastries 
and sweets, including baklava, as 
well as in savory cooking, including 
fried halloumi, and with rice and 
other grains.

You say pastrama,

Pastrami came to America with the Romanian Jews who

food,” and the food being preserved was generally beef.

On p. 21, we feature a knock-your-socks off Gruyère 
popover. A pop what, you say? 
Brought over by British settlers, it’s America’s 
answer to Britain’s Yorkshire pudding, a 17th-century 
muffin of sorts, cooked with a puddle of beef fat 
plopped into the middle of the batter before baking. 
It seems to have popped up here in the mid-
1800s via settlers from Maine. According to food 
historian Evan Jones, these cooks, missing their 
Yorkshire puddings, created a new version with 
ingredients such as garlic and herbs, baked in 
custard cups greased with drippings from roasted 
meats, thus creating “individual balloons of crusty 
meat-flavored pastry.” This resulted in a lighter 
flavor and texture and a different shape from its 
British cousin. Indeed, the name’s origin derives 
from the fact that the batter “pops over” the rim.

To start, heat the oven to 
350°F. Meanwhile, heat the 
pan on the stovetop until hot. 
1 With a thick wad of 
paper towels, spread 1 to 
2 tsp. of lard, bacon grease, 
or solid vegetable shorten-
ing all over the inside and 
outside of the pan. Apply a 
very thin coating of fat—too 
much grease, and you’ll end

rack of the heated oven for 
1½ hours. Slide a baking

1 In the Fine Cooking

French breakfast
A mild radish with an elongated shape,

They are prized for their spectacular

HISTORY
Why don’t they 
call them German 
pancakes?
They are delicious, but their name 
is inaccurate. How did a German 
oven-baked pancake come to be 
called a Dutch baby? The story is 
that in a Seattle restaurant, Manca’s 
Café, the owner’s daughter used 
the German word for German—
Deutsch—to describe the pancake. 
This sounded to Americans much 
like Dutch. The pancakes became 
so popular that the restaurant 
trademarked the name in 1942. 
Round or globe
This is the radish most commonly

and you’ll usually find a mix of heat 
levels within the same bunch. Try them

Black Spanish
Deep ebony on the outside with a 
pure white interior, this radish is very 
spicy. Cooking, however, mellows 
the bite. Try pickling them to use as 
garnishes for meats, sides, canapés,

Horseradish

Watermelon

Daikon

TECHNIQUE
How to slice an avocado safely
Slicing into an avocado and removing the pit can be tricky. Far too often, it ends in cuts  
and Band-Aids. To help you avoid this unfortunate fate, follow these simple steps.        
Lay the avocado on a cutting 
board, and using a chef’s knife, 
cut lengthwise around the 
center of the avocado, rotating 
the avocado on the board as 
you cut around. Separate the 
two halves.
Remove the large seed by 
scooping it out with a spoon. 
Stay as close to the seed as 
possible. When the seed is 
loose, use the spoon to pop  
it out.
Set the avocado halves on 
the board, flat side down. 
Slice each half in half again 
lengthwise to create quarters.
Starting from the pointy tip 
of the avocado quarter, lift 
the skin away from the flesh, 
and pull to peel off. Repeat 
with the other quarters. Slice 
or dice as desired.
