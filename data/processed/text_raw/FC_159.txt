Playing with Fire

IF YOU HAVEN'T ALREADY FIRED UP
THE GRILL (OR SMOKER), IT’S HIGH
TIME YOU HEAD TO THE BACKYARD
(or deck or patio). The great summer
barbecue season is upon us, and it has
never tasted better or had such diverse
inspirations.

To bring you some of the best bar-
becued foods, we went straight to the
masters. Aussie and author of Hardcore
Carnivore Jess Pryles shows us how
to smoke fall-off-the-bone tender ribs
(p. 50), with dishes like sweet chili, ginger,
and soy baby backs; and peppercorn-
spiced beef ribs. Smoking ribs may sound
intimidating (we thought so, too, at first),
but the best thing about Jess’s technique is
that it involves a series of simple steps you
can easily master to reap amazing results.

Atlanta chef Todd Richards and author
of the award-winning cookbook Soul: A
Chef's Culinary Evolution in 150 Recipes,
reveals his secrets to making outstanding
bold barbecue sauces (p. 56). He gives
us six delectable versions, including an
interpretation of his dad’s vinegary classic
and an inventive strawberry sauce (one
of my favorites in this collection). And
vegetable guru and cookbook author Su-
sie Middleton gives grilled vegetables the
star treatment in a collection of bright
and flavorful dishes. Mains like grilled
maple-tamari shiitake with noodles
(p. 70) and grilled zucchini, pepper, and
feta bruschetta (p. 73) can easily take
center stage on the dinner table.

We've got sides covered, too, with a slew
of slaws from Deb Perelman, the creator of
SmittenKitchen.com. You'll never look at
coleslaw the same way again after tasting one
of her creative takes (p. 62). And for dessert,
turn to p. 82 for the collection of irresistible
mix-and-match ice cream sandwiches from
award-winning pastry chef Emily Luchetti.
Imagine enjoying an almond butter-choco-
late chip cookie with orange ice cream or a
fudge cookie paired with coconut ice cream
on a warm summer evening.

Beyond the barbecue and grill, author
and food writer Grace Young shows us
how to cook hot and fast in a wok (p. 76).

Top: Quick and easy swordfish kebabs
from the grill (p. 24). Bottom: A selection of
the juicy grilled vegetables featured in our
“Sure Fire” feature on p. 66.

Now’s the time to capture the flavors of
peak-season produce, and stir-frying is
a foolproof way to do so. And as always,
you'll find lots of fresh ideas for easy
weeknight meals (p. 23) and tips and
advice from our test kitchen (p. 91).

This issue has everything you need to
create a summer's worth of exceptional
casual get-togethers and simple warm-
weather meals. Our hope is that you'll
have a burning desire to explore these
pages, tag a heap of recipes, and then
head outside to play with fire.

—Kathy Kingsley, editorial director

Perfect Pan Pizza

Square Pies to Make at Home,
from Roman, Sicilian and Detroit,
to Grandma Pies and Focaccia
By Peter Reinhart

(Ten Speed Press; $22)

This celebration of one of pizza's underappre-
ciated permutations is the latest must-read
by bread-making legend Peter Reinhart
(author of 11 books, including American

Pie, his first deep dive into pizza). Pan
pizza—the crusty style beloved from Chi-
cago and Detroit to Rome, Genoa, Tuscany,
and Sicily—is garnering fresh interest from
the pizza cognoscenti as a category ripe for
reinvention. Here, Reinhart illustrates how to
make pan pies equal to anything at a pizzeria,
offering easy methods for turning out flavor-
ful, crackly crusted pies at home. The bottom
line is that it’s all about the crust. That’s why
pan pizza affords home cooks a real shot at
greatness. Forget the 1,000°F wood-fired oven;
an ordinary range will do just fine. When you
start with one of Reinhart’s three meticulously
tested dough formulas (White Flour, Whole
Grain Country-Style, or Naturally Leavened),
pan-pizza perfection is well within reach.

Start Here. The Classic Red Stripe. This
Detroit-style pie—buttery crust, molten cheese,
tomato sauce—is so tasty that it could be the
beginning and end of your pan-pizza adventure.

Let’s Make Ramen!

A Comic Book Cookbook
By Hugh Amano and Sarah Becan
(Ten Speed Press; $19.99)

Whether you take it into the kitchen or to
the beach, this is hands down the coolest
cookbook of summer: a lighthearted volume
that gives ramen the full graphic-novel treat-
ment. If ever there was a culinary topic that
merited this distinction, ramen is most defi-
nitely it. The co-authors—chef Hugh Amano
and artist Sarah Becan—playfully obsess
(without seeming to obsess) over the slur-
pable dish that’s become a global fixation. In
bold comic format, they expeditiously cover
the basics: the history of ramen, ramen cul-
ture, how to enjoy ramen, and an overview
of all the ingredients and equipment you'll
need to master making a steaming hot bowl.
After that, it’s all about cooking. The book’s
40-plus easy-to-follow recipes illustrate step-
by-step how to make each component of
just about every variety of ramen. Sprinkled
throughout the pages are tasty tidbits on
Japanese culinary traditions, meaty morsels
of wisdom, and insights from some of the
world’s best ramen chefs and most passion-
ate fans.

Savory Snack. Ajitsuke Tamago. Soaked ina
flavor-bomb marinade, these soft-boiled eggs
are an essential ramen component, anda
delicious snack in their own right.

Thank You for Smoking

Fun and fearless recipes cooked with
a whiff of wood fire on your grill or smoker
By Paula Disbrowe (Ten Speed Press; $30)

Smoke. It’s universally associated with meat
on the grill or in the pit, but it’s actually a
seasoning that can go way beyond barbe-
cue. Smoky granola? Yes. Smoked almond
butter? Absolutely. Smoked borscht? Why
not? These are but a few of the surprises that
grill master Paula Disbrowe serves up in her
latest book, a collection of 100 recipes that
elucidate how smoke can drift into every part
of a meal, even the cocktails and sweets. The
key, Disbrowe explains, is balance. A recipe
with a focal smoked element—for example,
Smoky Shrimp with Lemon Orzo, Green
Olives, and Feta—should be offset with fresh,
vivid flavors. If you consider smoking syn-
onymous with slow, think again. Disbrowe’s
method is essentially grilling—that is, quick.
Adding a whiff of smoke to ingredients and
recipes is just a matter of tossing wood chips
or chunks onto hot coals. With this book at
the ready, there's nothing to stop you from
stocking your larder with smoked spices,
salts, and grains; or from smoking a couple
of chickens, eggplant, or whatever your heart
and stomach desire. Including dessert.

tion of a vinaigrette. Or you can blanch
them quickly, no more than a minute or
two, so that they maintain their crisp-
tender texture.

Other preparations include stir-frying
and pickling. Deep-frying them in tempura
batter makes for an addictive, salty treat that
gives popcorn a run for its money.

Choosing and storing

When choosing sea beans, look for
stalks that are green, never red, which
can denote woodiness. They should be
no longer than 4 inches; any longer, and
the stems become fibrous. You'll find the
most tender sea beans during their peak
season from May to September.

Because sea beans grow in tidal seabeds
and salty marshes, they require thorough
cleaning to remove the grit that clings to
them. Soaking the beans in cold water is a
great way to rid them of their sand.

As their delicate appearance suggests,
sea beans have a relatively short shelf
life and don't weather transport well.
Luckily, they can be found naturally in
coastal waters the world over. Store them
wrapped in a damp paper towel in a zip-
top bag in the refrigerator for no more

than a week. —Nancy Duran

Growing sea beans
Did you know that you can grow
sea beans in your backyard?

Sea beans love loose, sandy soil
and full sunlight. Be sure to water
them with a solution of water
and sea salt. Generally, 1 pint of
water to 1 tsp. sea salt (never
table salt) is recommended.

I GREW UP IN A BARBECUE-OBSESSED HOUSEHOLD, WHERE Dap’s

NOTHING BEATS HIGH HEAT FOR ENHANCING
THE FLAVOR OF VEGETABLES. I learned this years ago
in my first cooking job at Al Forno restaurant in Providence,
Rhode Island, where all the ovens and grills are wood-fired
and the produce is sourced from local farms. It was that
happy confluence—fresh veggies meeting sizzling heat—that
forever influenced how I prepare vegetables: hot and fast.

The best place to do so? On the grill. That fiery heat
caramelizes veggies in a flash, which really amps up their
sweetness. As a grilling devotée, I’m delighted to share my
passion with you. I ask but one thing in return: that you
get to know your grill. Every grill is unique, with its own
cool spots and hot spots. When you use your grill often,
you'll get to know all its quirks, along with the best ways to
work with them.

Getting to know your grill will also help you to get top
results from my recipes. Even though I developed the reci-
pes that follow with great care, it’s likely that you'll need
to adjust the cooking temperatures and times, the two
variables that can wreak havoc on a recipe.

Gas grills are common, so I wrote these recipes with gas
grills in mind. They'll also work on charcoal grills, though
the cooking times will be different. A gas grill functions
best when covered, allowing the hot air to circulate around
the food as it does in a convection oven and making up for
the fact that gas burners don’t emit as intense a heat as a
charcoal fire.

Grilling requires attention. When you're grilling veg-
etables, as opposed to meat, it’s OK to peek under the
cover often to see how the veggies are cooking. To help
determine doneness, look for sensory clues, like browning,
aroma, and textural changes. Know, too, that you'll prob-
ably have to move veggies around to equalize their time
spent on hot and cool spots.

My list of grilling equipment (sidebar right) is short. On
this subject, I think less is more. And my tips for grilling
different types of vegetables (see p. 74) are guidelines. With
time, you'll come up with your own grilling hacks, like my
discovery that turkey lacers make great skewers for onion
slices. My hope is that you'll use these recipes, tips, and
inspirations to build your own grilled-vegetable repertoire.

DON’T GET ME WRONG. I LOVE ALL MY KITCHEN
EQUIPMENT, BUT NUMBER ONE ON MY LIST IS DEFI-
NITELY MY WOK. It’s the workhorse in my kitchen. I love it
for many reasons: Its spacious surface allows liquids to reduce
quickly. Its concave shape makes stir-frying fast and easy. And
its deep well radiates intense heat, transforming its sloping
sides into a dynamic cooking surface. Simply stated, it’s the
only vessel that can stir-fry anything and cook it quickly, in a
healthful way.

I see wok cooking as a way of life, both timeless and timely
and to be used every day. Stir-frying in a wok works culinary
magic that transforms ingredients. I can’t think of another
cooking technique that makes less seem like more, that allows
a small amount of food to feed many even while keeping meat
to a minimum and accentuating an endless variety of vegeta-
bles. Serve a stir-fry with rice or noodles, and you always seem
to have enough food to feed a crowd.

Of course, a great stir-fry depends on great ingredients,
especially in-season veggies. When a vegetable is in season,
it has so much flavor that you barely need to add any season-
ings. Combined with high heat and super-fast cooking, the
technique of stir-frying makes the most of each ingredient’s
inherent flavor and texture.

As you use your wok, the patina deepens and acquires
a darker hue. A well-seasoned wok allows you to use less
cooking oil, because the carbon steel has been transformed
into a rustproof, naturally nonstick surface. Even without
the added fat, you'll notice your ingredients brimming with
intense flavors.

The recipes that follow are an introduction to the wonders of
the wok. The Sweet and Sour Pork with Fresh Pineapple (p. 80)
and the Kung Pao Chicken (p. 79) reach back to my Cantonese
roots, but I’ve given each a modern flair. The Farmers’ Mar-
ket Vegetable Stir-Fry (p. 78) takes advantage of all the great
produce available at farmers’ markets this time of year. And
the Stir-Fried Garlic Shrimp with Tomatoes and Basil (p. 81)
proves that a wok ist just for cooking Chinese food.

I hope these recipes will inspire you to use your wok every
day to stir-fry—and, of course, to steam, poach, and braise.
Remember, the depth and breath of a wok run to the sky’s
edge. Wok on!

Whether you're looking for a chill snack
ora cool finish for a cookout on the deck,
nothing beats ice cream sandwiches.

There’s no question that ice cream
sandwiches are delicious all year-round
(who would say no to a snickerdoodle
and pumpkin ice cream sandwich in
November?), but in summer, they truly
are the perfect dessert.

Why? Well, there’s the obvious:
They’re cold. Cooling off on a hot
day just by indulging in ice cream that’s
nestled between two cookies is pretty
hard to beat.

Beyond that, they’re make-ahead. You
can prepare both the cookies and the
ice cream early in the day (or even days
in advance) before temperatures rise.
Store the baked cookies in the freezer so
that they'll be ready to go when it’s time
to assemble dessert. Once made, ice
cream sandwiches will hold up well for up
to three days—or longer—in the freezer.
(If you prefer a softer treat, time is on
your side. For more on long-term stor-
age for ice cream sandwiches, see Test
Kitchen, p. 92).

BY EMILY LUCHETTI

Another plus: The ice cream compo-
nent is easy to prepare. | start with classic
vanilla ice cream because it’s easily
transformed into flavors that comple-
ment the cookie part of the sandwiches.
Adaptation is as easy as mixing in
espresso powder, or orange zest, ora
quick-cooking peach jam. | like to make
my own vanilla with a rich egg-yolk base,
but if you’re short on time, buy a super-
premium vanilla (such as Haagen-Dazs,
Ben & Jerry’s, or something from your
favorite local ice cream shop).

Another argument for the perfection
of ice cream sandwiches: They’re incred-
ibly versatile. While the classic vanilla ice
cream sandwiched between soft choco-
late wafers certainly has its place, there’s
no reason to stop there. Maybe that
fudgy chocolate cookie can hold toasted
coconut ice cream and feature a crunchy
coconut coating. How about ginger cook-
ies holding vanilla ice cream marbled
with mashed raspberries? Perhaps you'd

prefer brown sugar cookies sandwiching
espresso ice cream, or an almond butter-
chocolate chip cookie with orange ice
cream and crunchy cacao nibs. Or you
could try something novel, a blueberry
muffin halved and filled with fresh peach
ice cream.

As a sweet tooth, | have one more
reason for finding these treats perfect.
They’re actually two desserts in one:
luscious ice cream and flavorful cookies.
(In fact, for most of these recipes, you
may have cookies or ice cream left over. |
assure you that is not a bad thing.)

This summer, make the most of
the season with your own ice cream
sandwiches. They're the coolest dessert
around.

Emily Luchetti is a James Beard award-winning
pastry chef and the author of A Passion for Ice
Cream, Stars Desserts, Four Star Desserts, The

Fearless Baker, and more.

Grill vs. barbecue:
what'’s the difference?

Although these terms are frequently used interchangeably, they actually
refer to two different styles of outdoor cooking.

Grilling is high-heat cooking usually performed outside. Food is
cooked over direct heat with the lid open so that it sears, cooking from
the bottom of the food, not all around it.

Conversely, barbecue is all about low and slow cooking in the great
outdoors. The temperature is low, and the food cooks slowly; because the
lid remains closed, the heat circulates around the food, much as it does in

a convection oven.

Grilling is for lean steaks, vegetable slices, and fish fillets. Barbecue, on
the other hand, is best suited to tough meat cuts with lots of connective

tissue, cuts such as brisket, pork shoulder, and, of course, ribs.

Don’t muddle this up

Muddling is an essential bartending technique used to
release flavor from herbs and other ingredients. Do it
right, and you'll draw out the herb’s aromatic oils. Do it
wrong, and you'll pull out its bitter notes. You may be
tempted to vigorously muddle the leaves, thinking this
will release more flavor, but resist that urge. There are
many compounds in an herb leaf besides the ones related
to their aroma and flavor. Some, such as chlorophyll, will
lend undesirable grassy flavors to your drink. To avoid
this, make sure you don’t overwork the leaves. Muddle
just until they are lightly bruised and crushed, never torn
or pulverized. You'll know it’s time to stop muddling once
you smell the herby aroma. —Lisa Lahey

Tamari vs. Soy Sauce

Soy sauce adds a pleasing, salty note to Grace Young's
fresh stir-fries on p. 76. If you follow a gluten-free diet,
however, you may want to substitute tamari (though you
should double-check the label to make sure it’s completely
gluten-free). While both soy sauce and tamari are made
from fermented soybean paste, only soy sauce is also
fermented with roasted wheat. Tamari is thicker, richer,
and tangier than most soy sauces, though even with soy
sauce, there is a range from Chinese to Japanese styles,
light and dark, and high and lower sodium. Regardless
of these variations, you can use soy sauce and tamari
interchangeably with just a slight difference in flavor and
texture. —Joanne Smart

A visual guide to Summer squash

Thin-skinned and succulent, summer squash are
harvested immature, when the seeds are tiny and tender,
and they’re available from late spring through fall. The
vibrant orange blossoms are a fleeting early-summer
delicacy, but from high summer onward, the squash
themselves can be enjoyed raw, stewed, stir-fried,
stuffed, grilled, gratinéed, and more.

Choose squash that are small to medium-size with glossy,

bright-colored, tender skin. The squash should feel heavy for
its size. A few nicks and scratches are fine, but leave bruised
or wrinkled squash behind. Avoid jumbo specimens because
they tend to have watery flesh and large seeds.

Below are some varieties that you might see at the farm-

each variety has a cunningly distinct texture and flavor, so it
makes sense to sample widely and find your favorites. —L.L.
