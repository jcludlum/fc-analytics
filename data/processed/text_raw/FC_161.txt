If you crave juicy, ﬂavorful 
chicken, this simple method 
for sear-roasting is for you. 
The combination of tomatoes, 
beans, marinated kale, and an 
easy aïoli, makes an  
irresistible 30-minute meal.   
Serves 4 
4 boneless skinless chicken 
   breasts (about 1½ lb.)
Kosher salt and freshly ground  
   black pepper 
2 tsp. chopped fresh thyme 
8 oz. baby kale (about 10 lightly  
   packed cups)
4 Tbs. olive oil 
2 Tbs. fresh lemon juice 
½ cup mayonnaise
1 Tbs. Mutti® Double Concentrated 
    Tomato Paste 
½ cup freshly grated Parmigiano  
    Reggiano, divided
1 garlic clove, minced
14 oz. can Mutti® Cherry  
   Tomatoes, drained
1 cup canned cannellini beans,  
   drained and rinsed
½ cup pitted olives, such  
   as Kalamata and Castelvetrano, 
   coarsely chopped
Heat the oven to 450°F. 
Sprinkle the chicken with  
1½ tsp. salt, ½ tsp. black pep-
per, and 1 tsp. of the thyme. 
In a large bowl, toss the kale 
with 2 Tbs. oil, 1 Tbs. lemon 
juice, and ½ tsp. salt and let sit 
while you prepare the aioli. 
For the aïoli, combine the 
mayonnaise, tomato paste,  
¼ cup of Parmigiano cheese, 
the garlic, and remaining 1 Tbs. 
lemon juice in a food proces-
sor and process until pureed. 
Transfer the mixture to a bowl, 
season with salt and pepper to 
taste, and add cold water as 
needed, 1 Tbs. at a time, for 
desired consistency. 
Heat a large ovenproof  
skillet over medium-high 
heat for 1½ minutes. Add the 
remaining 2 Tbs. oil and once 
it’s shimmering hot, add the 
chicken evenly spaced. Cook, 
undisturbed, until browned 
around the edges and a corner 
easily releases when lifted  
with a pair of tongs, about  
3 minutes. Turn the chicken 
and remove the pan from the 
heat. Sprinkle the tomatoes and 
beans around the sides and 
transfer the skillet to the oven. 
Roast the chicken until a 
thermometer inserted into the 
thickest part registers 165°F, 
5 to 7 minutes. Remove from 
the oven.  
Arrange the kale and 
chicken with the tomatoes 
and beans on a serving  
platter. Sprinkle with the 
olives, the remaining  
Parmigiano cheese, and 
thyme. Serve with the aïoli  
on the side.  
Seared Herbed Chicken with Tomato Aïoli and Kale

NOTE FROM THE EDITOR
If you’re like me, you’re a sucker 
for soul food. Th e mere mention 
of fried chicken, collard greens, and 
cornbread is enough to make my mouth 
water. But the idea of combining the big, 
satisfying fl avors of soul food with the 
homey comforts of Th anksgiving? Th at’s 
pure dining heaven.  
So when Carla Hall, chef, author, 
and former co-host of ABC’s Th e Chew,
agreed to create a Th anksgiving menu 
with a nod to her culinary roots (p. 44), 
I knew we were in for something special. 
Carla has been eating soul food all of her 
life and cooking it her whole career. As 
she says, “I don’t just know soul food. 
Soul food is in my soul.” Carla’s recipes, 
including Pimento Cheese, Pan-Seared 
Turkey, Spoonbread Dressing, Creamed 
Kale, and melt-in-your-mouth Angel 
Biscuits, are a delicious testament to her 
love for this S outhern cuisine. 
If it’s not your turn to make the whole 
Th anksgiving meal this year, and instead 
you’ve been tasked to simply bring a side 
dish or two, this issue is packed with 
plenty of inspiring ideas from former FC 
senior editor Joanne Smart. I’m partial to 
the Butternut Squash and Leek Galette 
(p. 68), but the Creamy Caramelized 
Onions with Sage (p. 70) and Shredded 
Brussels Sprouts Salad with Spicy Toasted 
Walnuts and Currants (p. 70) are also 
amazing. Th e best part is that these sides 
can all be made ahead. Or if you’ve been 
assigned dessert, turn to p. 78, where au-
thor and baker extraordinaire Samantha 
Seneviratne shares four of her favorites, 
any of which would be welcomed (and 
devoured) at a holiday or family gather-
ing. No one will be able to resist her Cof-
fee Crème Bundt Cake (p. 79) or French 
Apple and Fig Tart (p. 83).
While Th anksgiving might be on every 
cook’s mind right now, most of us still 
need to get dinner on the table during 
the week. To that end, contributing editor 
Tony Rosenfeld shows us how to quickly 
sear skirt steaks with four fl avorful ways 
to serve them—the diff erence is all in the 
sauces (p. 52). Th ese tender morsels go 
from pan to plate in less than 30 minutes, 
so they’re a real boon to the weekday 
cook. And as always, you can fi nd more 
quick meals in our Make It Tonight sec-
tion on p. 31.
Whether you’re cooking Th anksgiving 
dinner for a crowd or Th ursday-night 
dinner for your family, this issue has all 
the recipes and inspiration you’ll need. 
And if you can add a little soul to your 
table while you’re at it, even better. 
—Kathy Kingsley, editorial director

Hello, future oat milk drinker! Once you try the oatsome deliciousness of our milks, you’ll be hooked. Oatly Barista, made 
from liquid oats, is delicious in coffee or over granola, and is fabulous in baked goods, such as the vegan carrot cake below. 
Serves 10 to 12
Oatly Vegan Carrot Bundt Cake with Vegan Cream Chese Glaze
FOR THE CAKE
 
1⁄4  cup organic golden ﬂaxseed  
 
 meal
 
3⁄4  cup Oatly Barista Oatmilk
 
12  oz. (about 11⁄2 cups) organic  
 
 cane or granulated sugar 
 
10  oz. (about 21⁄4 cups)  
 
 all-purpose ﬂour
 
1  oz. (about 1⁄4 cup) cornstarch
 
1  tsp. kosher salt
 
1  tsp. ground allspice
 
1  tsp. ground Aleppo pepper
 
3  oz. sweetened shredded  
 
 coconut (about 1 cup)
 
3  oz. raw walnuts, coarsely  
 
 chopped (about 1 cup)
 
81⁄2 oz. carrots, grated on the  
 
 large holes of box grater  
 
 (about 3 cups)
 
3⁄4  cups sunﬂower or other  
 
 neutral oil
 
1  tsp. pure vanilla extract
 
2  tsp. baking powder, preferably 
 
 aluminum-free
 
 Baking spray with ﬂour
FOR THE GLAZE
 
6  oz. vegan cream cheese-style  
 
 spread, at room temperature  
 
 (about 3⁄4 cup)
 4 to 6  Tbs. Oatly Barista Oatmilk
 
3⁄4  cup confectioners’ sugar, more 
 
 as needed
 
1  tsp. pure vanilla extract
 
 Pinch kosher salt
MAKE THE CAKE
In a small bowl, combine the ﬂaxseed 
meal and Oatly Barista Oatmilk, and 
let sit until thickened, whisking oc-
casionally, 15 to 20 minutes. 
Meanwhile, combine the sugar, 
ﬂour, cornstarch, salt, allspice, Aleppo 
pepper, coconut, and walnuts in the 
bowl of a stand mixer ﬁtted with the 
paddle attachment. Mix on low speed 
until combined. 
In a separate medium bowl, 
combine the carrots, oil, and vanilla, 
and stir with a wooden spoon. Add 
the carrot mixture and the ﬂaxseed 
mixture to the stand mixer bowl. Mix 
on low speed until the mixture is well 
combined, scraping down the bowl as 
needed. Let sit in the stand mixer for 
15 minutes. 
Meanwhile, position a rack in the 
center of the oven, and heat to 350°F. 
Sift the baking powder over the 
cake batter, and mix on low, 30 to 45 
seconds. Generously spray a 12-cup 
standard Bundt pan with the baking 
spray, coating all folds and the center 
funnel. Scrape the batter into the pan 
with a silicone spatula, and smooth 
the top. Put the pan on a rimmed bak-
ing sheet, and bake until the cake is 
golden and a cake tester inserted in 
the cake comes out clean, 55 minutes 
to 1 hour. 
Transfer the pan from the sheet 
tray to a rack, and let cool in the pan 
for 1 hour. Turn the pan over onto a 
plate, and tap lightly with a spoon to 
release the cake. Carefully remove the 
pan, and let cool completely before 
glazing. 
MAKE THE GLAZE
Put the cream cheese spread in a me-
dium bowl, and beat with an electric 
hand mixer until creamy and soft. Add 
2 Tbs. of the Oatly Barista Oatmilk, 
and beat until combined. Add half the 
confectioners’ sugar, and beat until 
combined. Add an additional 2 Tbs. of 
the Oatly Barista Oatmilk and the re-
maining confectioners’ sugar, and beat 
until smooth, scraping down the bowl 
occasionally, until the mixture is very 
thick and coats the back of a spoon. 
Adjust the consistency to your liking 
by adding more Oatly Barista Oatmilk 
or confectioners’ sugar.
Drizzle the glaze over the cake. 
Let sit 30 minutes or until glaze sets 
before serving.

this all-ocasion carrot 
cake you’ll make again 
and again. For optimum 
results, use a standard 
light colored 12 cup  
metal Bundt pan with a

What we’re cooking now
Fine Cooking editors and a reader share 
some delicious ideas for in-season ingredients.
AT THE MARKET
Bircher Muesli with Pomegranates
Soak 2 cups of oats overnight in the fridge in a 2-cup mixture of milk and sweetened 
condensed milk. Add 2 grated apples, fresh pomegranate seeds, and halved hazelnuts. 
In the morning, stir the mixture (it’ll be thick), then mix in a bit of vanilla yogurt 
until it’s as creamy as you like it. Top with fresh berries or any fruit of your choice. 
 
 
 
 
 
                         —Helle Stork, Copenhagen, Denmark
Quince-Apple Tart
Simmer wedges of peeled, cored 
quince in simple syrup with a stick of 
cinnamon until they’re translucent and 
just tender. Thaw a sheet of frozen puff 
pastry, and with a paring knife, score 
a ¾-inch border. Brush the border 
with milk. Arrange the drained quince 
slices, along with an equal amount 
of peeled, sliced apples, inside the 
border. Sprinkle all over (including the 
border) with turbinado sugar. Bake at 
375°F until the pastry is golden-brown 
and the apples are tender. 
                                                   —Sarah Breckenridge
Golden Beet and Purple 
Peruvian Potato Salad
Cut purple Peruvian potatoes and 
a golden beet into chunks, and toss 
separately with olive oil, salt, and pep-
per. Roast separately in a hot oven until 
tender. Toss the potatoes and beets 
in a large bowl. Cut a red bell pepper 
into chunks, and add to the potatoes 
and beets. Stir in thinly sliced scallion 
greens, chopped fresh dill, a little may-
onnaise, a little sour cream, and salt 
and pepper to taste.               —Chris Hoelck
Oregano Dressing
Combine lemon juice, a coarsely 
chopped shallot, grated garlic, honey, 
and fresh oregano leaves in a blender, 
and purée until mostly smooth. With 
the blender running, drizzle in olive oil, 
and continue to purée until emulsified. 
Add salt and pepper to taste. Use to 
dress green salads, roasted vegeta-
bles, or as a bright marinade for steak, 
chicken, or fish. 
      —Diana Andrews
Chunky Mashed Potatoes 
with Upland Cress
Boil unpeeled baby Yukon Gold pota-
toes in well-salted water until they’re 
easily pierced with a fork. Lightly mash 
(leaving the potatoes chunky) with 
plenty of butter, some heavy cream, 
and salt and pepper to taste. Chif-
fonade a bunch of upland cress leaves, 
mince a few chives, and mix into the 
potatoes.                     —Kimberly Y. Masibay
Porcini Mushroom Farro
Sauté a sliced shallot in a mix of 
butter and olive oil until softened. 
Add halved porcini mushrooms 
and lemon thyme leaves, and con-
tinue cooking until tender. Transfer 
to a large bowl, add a large handful 
of coarsely chopped fresh flat-leaf 
parsley, and toss in cooked farro. 
Season to taste with salt and pep-
per, and toss again. Drizzle with olive 
oil, squeeze over some lemon juice, 
sprinkle with shaved pecorino, and 
serve with lemon wedges.                    
                                                        —Nancy Duran
Delicata Squash 
and Coconut Soup
Melt some butter in a large saucepan. 
Add cubed delicata squash, chopped 
onions, minced garlic, a little ground 
cardamom and cumin, and salt and 
pepper to taste, and cook for about 
5 minutes. Add warm vegetable broth 
and coconut milk, and simmer until the 
squash is tender. Transfer to a blender 
and purée. Return to the pot to reheat. 
Serve garnished with pumpkin seeds 
and flaked coconut.      —Kathy Kingsley

When Thanksgiving Day rolls 
around at Carla Hall’s house, 
you’re not about to see a big 
roasted turkey as her table’s 
centerpiece. That’s because before 
she even starts to cook the bird, she’s 
broken it down into pieces, maybe 
even cooking it in a few different ways 
to give her friends and family some 
variety. “Everyone wants to re-create 
that Norman Rockwell painting with 
the whole bird,” Hall says with a laugh. 
“But I like to mix it up. I use the bones 
for my gravy and for stock, because 
I am not cooking on the day after 
Thanksgiving.” The former Top Chef 
fan favorite and star of The Chew may 
do a dry salt brine with gremolata for 
one part, a wet brine with herbs for 
another, and for yet another a spicy 
grilled version that’s inspired by the 
“hot chicken” at Prince’s, an institution 
in Hall’s hometown, Nashville, where 
many in her family still live. “At soul 
food restaurants,” Hall says, “you’d 
have turkey and gravy and layer it.”
At Hall’s house, you can layer or 
not. But Thanksgiving is always about 
soul food. Along with that turkey

simply everyday cooking. When she 
left home for Howard University, for 
modeling stints in Europe, and later

Southern food, soul food; so what’s the difference? 
“Black cooks,” says Hall. Soul food was born from 
slavery and emancipation, some dishes based on 
West and Central African or Caribbean foods, oth-
ers combining French and Portuguese flavors, and 
others grounded in whatever seasonal (and mea-
ger) ingredients were available to them, South and 
North—mostly vegetables and, for special occasions, 
meat. What these black cooks made, says chef and 
culinary historian Michael Twitty, was “edible jazz.” 
“Soul food isn’t a monolith,” says Hall, who is 
proud to have been named culinary ambassador for 
the Smithsonian National Museum of African Ameri-
can History and Culture, where the offerings at its 
Sweet Home Café cover the gamut of American soul 
food. Greens might be a little sweeter in North Caro-
lina, for example; and in the Sea Islands of Georgia 
and South Carolina, grits may be made not with milk 
or cream but with the natural juices and starches from 
smashed dry corn kernels native to the region.
Of course, Hall’s putting her own spin on these 
classics, in some cases making them lighter and faster 
to prepare, encouraging those not in the know to add a 
little soul to the table. So for a Thanksgiving celebration, 
traditional mashed potatoes get a little buttermilk, the 
relishes are chowchow and cranberry-pear, and a sweet 
potato pie sits right next to a Southern pecan with a 
wee bit of bourbon. And if someone wanted to bring a 
pot of gooey macaroni and cheese, you wouldn’t hear a 
complaint. This is a celebration, after all.
Thanksgiving is a time when everyone pitches in. 
“My mom is the true matriarch,” Hall says, “but I’m 
thankful that my family allows me to be the culinary 
matriarch. I encourage everybody, especially the 
kids, to choose and cook a dish. We lose our culture 
if we don’t share it with the young people. Last 
Thanksgiving, my nephew picked my recipe for 
molasses chicken wings. Now, that’s not a traditional 
Thanksgiving food, but it’s what he wanted to make. 
And I’d never seen him that engaged. At that point, I 
know I’ve done my job. I just get out of the way.”

results are tenfold. The heat brings out a lemon’s juice 
and the essential oils in its skin, caramelizing and reduc-
ing them into a syrupy, charred sweetness that adds an 
intoxicating flavor to just about anything it touches. 
The recipes here all start with the easy process of 
roasting lemons (see p. 63). Try the Roasted Lemon 
Shrimp Scampi with Linguine (p. 60) for an easy week-
night meal. The syrupy juice coats the shrimp and pasta 
with an irresistible tangy sweetness. Roasted Lemon 
Chicken Thighs and Potatoes (p. 61) combines deep 
lemon flavor with a touch of honey, and if you’re looking 
for something sweet, the Roasted Lemon Tart (p. 62) 
uses minced peel in the crust and juice in the filling for a 
double dose of roasted lemony goodness.
But don’t stop here. Have some fun experimenting 
with the almost endless ways to use roasted lemons 
with chicken, fish, pork, salads, and vegetables, and 
even in drinks —a roasted lemonade, perhaps?

Potato Pie, Italian Style
Creamy, comforting, and crazy delicious, this dish wins as a main or a side.
B Y LY N N E C U R R Y
When it comes to beloved side dishes, 
mashed potatoes is the hands-down favorite in my 
family. This buttery, fluffy root vegetable purée is the 
star of the dinner table whether I’m serving meatloaf, 
roast chicken, wine-braised pork, or a Thanksgiving 
turkey. But I’ve ofen thought that this comforting dish 
deserves to be more than just a side and instead belongs 
in the center of the plate. 
A layered potato pie is the perfect solution—and not 
just any toss-it-together baked dish but one inspired by 
the Italian potato pie called gatto di patate. 
The recipe that follows features panko-dusted, 
Parmesan-flavored mashed potatoes that hold a layer of 
sautéed chard and creamy fontina cheese. (There are also 
two variations to choose from, one with savory crum-
bled sausage and provolone and the other with sweet 
caramelized onions, fragrant rosemary, and scamorza 
cheese.) Once unmolded from the pan, it presents like a 
layer cake. You can prepare the pie in advance, because it 
reheats perfectly and slices beautifully. And it’s nearly as 
fast to make as mashed potatoes, with some additional 
hands-off baking time. 
These spectacular mashed potato pies may be built 
from humble ingredients, but they’re sure to make a 
statement on your table for any occasion or, dare I sug-
gest it, even on a weeknight.

Couscous Stews
A star of North African cuisine, these casual dishes express 
their rich cultural origins in three delicious versions.  BY JAMIE SCHLER
Couscous is the most emblematic dish of morocco, 
Algeria, and Tunisia—collectively known as the Maghreb. It’s a sub-
stantial, magnificent centerpiece on any family table or festive buffet. 
Though it’s generally referred to simply as couscous, this traditional 
dish is much more than the fluffy, steamed grains of the same name. 
A traditional couscous features meat, fish, or vegetables in a fragrant 
broth, cooked until exceedingly tender, and then topped with creamy 
chickpeas and plump sweet raisins and served over the couscous grains 
that lend their name to the whole concoction. 
Couscous stews are cooked with enough liquid that a substantial 
amount of broth is left in the final dish. This broth, ladled onto each serv-
ing, is used to lightly moisten the couscous grains or to generously bathe 
the dish, as each guest desires. 
North African cuisine is unscripted. It is informal at its heart, and meals 
are often prepared by a cook who has never used a cookbook or a written 
recipe. It’s a no-holds-barred, anything-goes cuisine that invites improvisa-
tion, as long as you understand the underlying rules and traditions from 
which it derives. The three countries of the Maghreb boast dozens of varia-
tions of couscous, each influenced by the particular region from which it 
hails. Depending on where it originates, the couscous may be reddened 
with tomatoes or simply sauced with broth, it may be meatless, or  
it may feature the fish of the local coastline. It may be savory-sweet or 
strictly savory.
The most common and best-known couscous, a dish of the Berber 
people of North Africa, features seven vegetables: carrots, turnips, onions, 
zucchini, tomatoes, pumpkin (or other squash), and eggplant, along with 
chickpeas. When this copious, soulful dish is served with lamb, chicken, or 
spicy merguez sausage, it is aptly named couscous royale.
Once you understand the basic preparation method and stock your 
cupboard with the staple ingredients, it’s just a question of expressing your 
own personal take on a classic by using what’s in the market that day and 
what your own favorite meat, fish, and vegetables happen to be. 
Jamie Schler is an award-winning writer specializing in food and culture. 
Her first cookbook, Orange Appeal, features the fruit of her Florida child-
hood, the orange. She now lives in Chinon, France, where she owns a hotel, 
makes a lot of jam, and writes.

The couscoussière
Many are familiar with the TAGINE, the 
North African meat and vegetable dish

sauce. The word tagine not only refers to 
the food but also to the terra-cotta cone-
shaped lidded dish in which one both 
cooks and serves the dish. As it hap-
pens, couscous also can be prepared in 
a specially designed cooking vessel, the 
COUSCOUSSIÈRE. This large, double-

stainless steel, aluminum, or tin but can 
also be found in copper and even clay.  
Resembling a double boiler, it is used

my husband (who learned North African

slice of meat that is more tender and less 
chewy to the bite. Going against the grain

A very short history of maple syrup
HISTORY
Native Americans in the North-
east were making syrup from 
the sap of the sugar-maple tree 
(Acer saccharum) long before 
Europeans settled the land. 
Maple syrup was their all-purpose 
flavoring and a dietary staple; in 
fact, it was so prized, it was used 
as currency. When Europeans 
arrived on the continent and 
learned about this sweet natural 
resource, they added something 
important to the process of 
making it: iron and copper kettles. 
(Native Americans boiled the 
syrup by dropping red-hot stones 
into thick wooden containers 
full of sap.) Metal kettles made it 
much easier to boil the sap down 
to syrup. 
By the early 1800s, maple sap 
was being made into both syrup 
and sugar. It was prized by Quak-
ers and abolitionists as an alterna-
tive to slave-made cane sugar. 
The maple-syrup industry grew 
after the Civil War, when tin cans, 
metal tree-tapping spouts, and 
evaporator pans entered the pic-
ture. Most early syrup producers 
were dairy farmers who “sugared” 
during the off-season to supple-
ment their income. 
                      —Kimberly Y. Masibay

Rethinking biscuit shape
Food waste is a hot topic these days, and rightly so. Even as people 
go hungry, Americans discard an estimated 150,000 tons of food 
every day. On a smaller scale—not to mention because I’m a 
cheapskate—I hate to waste food I’ve made, which can be a source 
of frustration when I’m making a batch of biscuits. Generally, aft er 
you punch out the fi rst batch of round biscuits, you can gently 
reassemble the dough and cut out a second batch, but they’re 
never quite as tender nor do they rise as high as the fi rst. And then 
there’s the dough that ends up being discarded aft er the second 
round of shaping and cutting.
To get around the issue of tossing all that potential biscuit deli-
ciousness, I make square (or square-ish) biscuits. I pat the dough 
into a rough square or rectangle, and then I use either my chef’s 
knife or a bench scraper to make strong, clean cuts through the 
dough to create roughly the number of biscuits the recipe yields. 
As when making round biscuits, the clean cuts are important; you 
don’t want to compress the dough, which can inhibit the biscuits’ 
rise in the oven.
Since I’m not discarding any dough, the biscuits I make this 
way end up a little larger and occasionally require slightly longer 
to bake. In my house, though, no one has griped about a square, 
slightly larger biscuit. If you’re like me and you want to savor all 
the biscuit dough, try this technique. You may fi nd that it’s hip to 
be square.                                                                   —Chris Hoelck
Canned chipotle
When a recipe such as the Spicy-
Smoky Green Beans on p. 67 calls for 
1 Tbs. of chipotles in adobo, what’s a 
cook to do with the rest of the can? 
Here are four great ideas: 
Save it. Purée the chipotles and 
adobo sauce; spoon 1 Tbs. portions 
into containers or ice cube trays; 
cover; and freeze.
Make chipotle butter. Purée 
the left over chipotles and adobo, and 
mix into soft ened butter. It’s delicious 
on everything from steak to steamed 
veggies to grilled corn on the cob.
Make a wet rub. Purée the 
remaining contents of the can and 
mix with a bit of oil; something 
acidic, like lemon or lime juice; 
something sweet, like honey, maple 
syrup, or sugar; and salt. Use as a rub 
or marinade for poultry or meat. 
Spice up the basics. Chop or 
purée the remaining contents of the 
can, and use to spice up everything 
from scrambled eggs to store-bought 
mayo, ketchup, mustard, hummus, 
salsa, and sour cream.

In her soul-food Thanksgiving on 
p. 44, Carla Hall offers a great alter-
native to preparing a whole turkey. 
She sears, then roasts off individual 
turkey pieces. However, if you’d like 
to go the more traditional route 
and serve a whole bird, here are the 
most popular preparation methods 
you’ll have at your disposal.
OVENROASTED: This classic 
preparation is the one most people 
turn to on the big day. If you do, be 
sure to have a good-quality meat 
thermometer on hand. Accurate 
temperature measurement is key to 
achieving the juiciest bird.
GRILLED: Give it a try! Grilling 
gives turkey delicious flavor, and—
bonus—cooking the turkey outside 
frees precious oven space for sides 
and pies. Essentially, grilling involves  
re-creating the convection atmo-
sphere of an oven. Indirect heat is the 
way to do this. On a charcoal grill, 
that involves pushing the coals to one 
side of the turkey and positioning the 
bird over a drip tray. On a gas grill, 
all it takes is lighting a burner on one 
side of the turkey. In both cases, the 
lid should remained closed as much 
as possible. 
DEEPFRIED: This technique 
requires some special equipment, 
namely a tall turkey fryer, and 
buckets of peanut oil. However, the 
results are amazing. Turkey comes 
out crisp on the outside and moist 
on the inside. Cooked properly, it 
shouldn’t be at all greasy.

Perfecting your poultry 
The big day has arrived, and the turkey 
will be center stage. You know your Thanksgiving 
Day guests will be silently grading you on three 
criteria. Your turkey absolutely must feature crisp 
browned skin, the breast meat must be juicy, 
and of course, the bird must be full-flavored. 
Ensure that you achieve this successful trifecta by 
following these guidelines. 
For the crispest skin possible, thoroughly dry the 
turkey. After drying, let the turkey sit, uncovered, 
in the fridge for several hours before cooking. Be 
sure to cook the turkey raised off the base of the 
pan (either on a rack or on a bed of vegetables). 
That way, dry oven air can circulate around the 
bird as it cooks, keeping it crisp all over. 
To achieve the juiciest white meat possible, 
brine the turkey to infuse it with moisture, and 
season it with the salt in the brine. Also, butter 
the breast before cooking. Best practice is to slide 
butter under the skin of the breast. While you’re 
at it, tuck fresh sage and/or thyme under the skin 
and into the cavity so that they infuse the bird with 
their herbal notes. 
And last, start with the best-quality turkey you 
can ﬁnd. The quickest path to a great-tasting 
turkey is to start with an all-natural bird.

make-ahead gravy 
I don’t think I’m going out on a limb 
here when I say that gravy is the 
very best part of the Thanksgiving 
meal. But how do you make deeply 
flavored, velvety gravy when you 
don’t oven-roast a whole turkey? 
I’ve got you covered with this handy 
make-ahead gravy. And because 
it’s make-ahead, you won’t have 
to grapple with the last-minute 
scramble of preparing the gravy 
after you’ve taken the bird out of 
the oven and your guests are eager 
to dig in to the holiday meal.  Makes 
4 cups each stock and gravy
FOR THE TURKEY STOCK
 
3  lb. turkey or chicken wings
 
1  large yellow onion, chopped
 
2  medium carrots, chopped
 
2  medium ribs celery, chopped
 
2  fresh bay leaves
 
  Handful of fresh sage and 
thyme
 
1  tsp. kosher salt
FOR THE GRAVY
 
4  oz. (8 Tbs.) unsalted butter
 
2   medium shallots, minced 
(about 13 cup)
 2¼    oz. (½   cup) all-purpose flour
 
4   cups turkey stock, at room
temperature
 
¾    tsp. kosher salt
 
¼   tsp. freshly ground black pepper
MAKE THE TURKEY STOCK
Position a rack in the center of the 
oven, and heat the oven to 425°F. Put 
the wings in a large, deep roasting pan. 
Roast, flipping once halfway through 
cooking time, for 40 minutes. Transfer 
the wings to a large Dutch oven or other 
heavy-duty pot. Add about ½   cup water 
to the roasting pan, and using a wooden 
spoon, scrape up any browned bits 
from the bottom of the pan. Pour the 
browned bits and drippings into the pot 
with the wings.
To the pot, add the onion, carrots, 
celery, bay leaves, sage, thyme, and 
10 cups water. Bring to a simmer over 
medium-high heat, then lower the heat 
to low and simmer gently for 2½   hours, 
skimming off any foam that rises to the 
top. Season with the salt, then strain, dis-
carding the solids. 
MAKE THE GRAVY
Melt the butter in a deep skillet over 
medium-low heat. Add the shallots, 
and cook gently, stirring, until softened 
but not colored, about 2 minutes. Sift 
the flour over the shallots, stirring to 
incorporate. Cook, stirring, until the roux 
is lightly golden, about 5 minutes. 
Slowly add the stock in ladlefuls, 
stirring to incorporate. When it is all 
added, cook, simmering gently, until 
thickened, about 8 minutes. Add the salt 
and pepper. Use immediately or allow to 
cool, then refrigerate, covered, for up to 
5 days. When you reheat the gravy, you 
may need to add a few tablespoons of 
stock or water to achieve the desired 
consistency.

Slicing boneless 
turkey breast
If you roast a boneless turkey breast, try carving it 
crosswise instead of lengthwise. You’ll end up with 
slices that are similar in size and easier to manage 
than longer lengthwise slices. 
To cleanly slice through the crisp skin, position 
the breast skin side down on the cutting board. 
Saw through the meat, then press down fi rmly 
with the knife when it hits the skin so that you get 
a clean cut through the skin and so that it stays 
attached to the meat. (See p. 94 to learn how to 
bone a turkey breast.)                                      —D.A.

Turkey flip
When oven-roasting a turkey, a great method for 
achieving juicy results is to position the turkey
breast side down for the ﬁ rst part of roasting. When

TEST KITCHEN
To prepare Carla Hall’s delicious Pan-Seared Tur-
key on p. 49, you need to either break down a turkey into 
pieces yourself or have your butcher do it for you. In the Test 
Kitchen, we found that once the turkey was broken down, it 
made sense to take the turkey breast off the bone so that it 
was easier to carve into clean slices that presented beautifully 
at the table. Here’s a quick look at how to remove the breast 
meat from the bone.
Boning a turkey breast
Lay the breast skin side down on a cutting board. Cut the 
backbone away with poultry shears. Next, locate the wish bone 
in the V-shaped neck end of the breast. Using a boning knife, 
scrape the meat away from the bone and remove the bone.
Remove the rib cage and breast bone by cutting between the 
ribs and the meat at one edge of the breast. Scrape the knife as 
close to the rib bones as possible, and as the bones come free, 
pull back on them to make cutting between the bones and meat 
easier. Continue until you reach the breast bone.
Scrape the point of the knife along the breast bone and use 
your thumb to free the meat. Be careful not to cut through the 
meat and skin at the ridge of the breast bone (the meat is very 
thin here).
Once you bone half the breast, scrape along the other side of 
the breast bone and along the ribs until the entire rib cage and 
breast bone can be freed in one piece.                          —Bruce Aidells
