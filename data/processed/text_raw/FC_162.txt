Goat Cheese with Crostini
Serves 4 
2 Tbs. extra virgin olive oil; more for the baguette
1 3 cup grated carrot (on medium holes of  
   box grater)
¼ cup finely chopped sweet onion
¼ cup finely chopped pitted Castelvetrano olives
1 14-oz. can Mutti® Finely Chopped Tomatoes (Polpa)
Kosher salt and freshly ground black pepper
1 7-oz. wheel of goat cheese brie, rind removed 
   or 4 oz. goat cheese
4 tsp. mixed herbs, such as basil,  
   parsley, and oregano
¼ tsp. granulated garlic
Sliced baguette, brushed with olive oil and toasted
 
Position a rack in the center of the oven  
and heat the oven to 450°F. 
Put the oil in a medium saucepan over 
medium heat until the oil shimmers. Add  
the carrot, onion, and olives, and cook until 
the vegetables begin to soften and the oil 
turns light orange, stirring occasionally, about 
3 minutes. Add the tomatoes, ½ tsp. salt, and  
¼ tsp. pepper, and continue to cook, stirring  
occasionally, until the mixture is heated 
through, 2½ to 3 minutes.
1 large egg, lightly beaten with 1 Tbs. water
½ cup caramelized onions
1 Tbs. coarsely chopped mixed herbs,  
   such as thyme, basil, and sage
Flaky sea salt
Balsamic glaze (optional)
 
Position a rack in the center of the oven 
and heat the oven to 400°F.
In a small bowl, combine the cherry toma-
toes with the oil, ¼ tsp. granulated garlic,  
¼ tsp. salt, and ¼ tsp. pepper. Set aside. In a 
medium bowl, combine the ricotta with 1 tsp. 
lemon zest, the remaining ¼ tsp. granulated 
garlic, ½ tsp. salt, and a few grinds of pepper. 
Set aside.

NOTE FROM THE EDITOR
DURING THE FLURRY OF THE HOLI
DAYS, it’s easy to lose sight not just of the 
season, but of the year that has passed. For 
me, 2019 was a whirlwind, and I’m happy for 
this opportunity to refl ect on everything I 
have to be grateful for, and the many resolu-
tions on my list for next year.
I’m Heide Lang, the new editor in chief 
and creative director of Fine Cooking. For 
the past decade, I’ve owned, taught at, and 
developed recipes for Th e Fig Cooking 
School in Milford, Conn., which I opened 
aft er more than 20 years as a producer, 
reporter, and investigative journalist. Named 
aft er my daughters, Francesca, Isabella, 
and Gabrielle, Fig celebrates innovative 
home cooking in the tradition of family and 
togetherness, so it’s no surprise that Fine 
Cooking has always been my favorite culinary 
magazine. Th is year, I’m grateful not just for 
my family and business, but also for fi nding a 
platform that allows me to be part of the con-
versation about home cooking in America. 
Th is position combines my passions for food 
and journalism, and is completely aligned 
with my values and vision. I’m so grateful 
for the fabulous editorial team, the tireless 
test kitchen staff , and the support of Th e 
Taunton Press as I join this legendary, inspi-
rational franchise.
Looking ahead, I have many resolutions 
to share for the new year. I look forward to 
bringing you the answers to your most press-
ing questions, and to ensuring that every 
recipe, photograph, and article fuels your 
journey. I commit to bringing you foolproof, 
delicious, and enlightening recipes that chal-
lenge you to consider food—and your capa-
bilities—from new angles. I’m also excited 
to announce that we are developing new 
online and print departments that will make 
Fine Cooking more interactive. Our team will 
be revolutionizing the website, which will 
soon feature a host of brand-new videos and 
special features. 
As I begin working on the exciting 
changes we plan to bring you in 2020, I’m 
New Beginnings
@finecooking
Heide Lang

Make a batch (or more!) of this ﬂ avor-packed snack to get in the 
mood to watch some mystery programs—you won’t be able to

SERVES 8 TO 10
3 Tbs. ghee or butter
2 Tbs. Madras curry powder
Kosher salt
2 Tbs. vegetable oil
1/2 cup popcorn kernels, about 3 3/4 oz.
2 cups packaged potato sticks 
1 cup packaged freeze-dried or roasted peas
In a small saucepan, melt the ghee over medium-low heat. Add the curry 
powder and 1 tsp. salt and cook, stirring occasionally until fragrant, 1 to 1 1/2 
minutes. Set aside while you make the popcorn.
Put the oil and 2 or 3 popcorn kernels in a large Dutch oven or other heavy-
duty pot. Partially cover and heat over medium-high heat until the kernels pop. 
Add the remaining popcorn kernels. Cover and shake the pot back and forth 
constantly once the heavy popping starts. After about 3 minutes, the popping 
will slow down (you’ll know this when you can count to 5 without hearing a 
pop). Remove the pan from the heat, and give it a ﬁ nal shake. Transfer to a 
large bowl. 
Stir the curry mixture, spoon it over the popcorn, and toss until well coated. 
Add the potato sticks and peas, and toss until combined. Serve.

MIXED BERRY-LEMON CURD FOOL
When it comes to watching British TV comedies, no other 
dessert makes more sense than a berry fool. Easy to make 
and simply irresistible, you may need more than one. 
SERVES 4 
2 cups heavy cream
2 Tbs. confectioners’ sugar
1 tsp. pure vanilla extract
2 1/2 Tbs. mixed berry jam 
2 1/2 Tbs. prepared lemon curd
8 shortbread cookies, 4 left whole, 4 crushed
8 oz. fresh blueberries, about 2 cups
6 oz. fresh raspberries, about 1 1/3 cups
Chill the bowl and beaters of a stand mixer for about 10 minutes. Add the 
cream, confectioners’ sugar, and vanilla to the bowl and beat with an electric 
mixer on high speed until stiﬀ  peaks form, about 1 1/2 minutes. Divide the 
whipped cream evenly into two separate bowls. Gently fold the jam into one 
bowl, and the curd into the other, until both are well combined. 
Spoon 1 Tbs. of the crushed shortbread cookies into the bottoms of four 
16 oz. glasses. Divide the berry cream among the glasses and smooth the 
tops evenly. Top each with 1 Tbs. of the crushed shortbread cookies, 10 to 11 
blueberries, and 5 to 6 raspberries. Divide the lemon curd cream among the 
glasses and smooth the tops evenly.  Divide the remaining crushed shortbread 
cookies evenly among the glasses, top each with 10 to 11 blueberries, and 5 to 
6 raspberries. Refrigerate covered in plastic until ready to serve, up to 3 hours. 
Serve with a whole shortbread cookie.

Escarole and White Bean Sauté
Tear two heads of escarole into pieces. Wash thoroughly, leaving a bit of moisture on the 
leaves. Heat olive oil in a stockpot over medium heat. Add chopped garlic, minced anchovy

Aromatics
These flavor builders are 
the foundation of countless 
dishes in most every cuisine.
BY JAMES PE TERSON
KITCHEN BASICS
Aromatics are the ingredients that 
form the deep but subtle background fl avor 
of a dish. Th ey’re the ingredients that cooks 
the world over reach for when they begin to 
prepare a recipe. And they’re generally the 
fi rst ingredients added to the pan at the 
start of cooking. 
In Europe and America, the aromatics 
of choice are usually carrots, onions, and 
celery and/or celeriac, along with garlic. 
Less frequently used vegetables include 
turnips, parsley root, and fennel. Within 
this basic framework, each cuisine 
features variations in the type 
and ratio of vegetables used 
that, in turn, diff er from 
region to region.
Th e French have 
mirepoix (onions, 
carrots, celery, 
and sometimes 
turnips). Italians 
rely on a blend of 
aromatics called 
soff ritto (onions, 
carrots, celery, 
and oft en pro-
sciutto). Portu-
guese cooks make 
refogado (onions, 
tomato, and garlic). 
Add red bell peppers to 
the refogado, and you’ve 
got a Spanish sofrito. Eastern 
cuisines feature their own
unique combinations. Th ink garlic, 
ginger, and scallions in many regions of 
China. And variations abound throughout 
Latin America, like Puerto Rico’s recaito, 
which boasts onions, green bell peppers, 
cilantro, aji dulce peppers, and garlic. 
As these fragrant ingredients cook, they 
contribute a complex vegetal fl avor base to 
underpin a fi nished dish.
How and when?
Using aromatics is simple, so it’s hard to go 
wrong. But there are a few things you can 
keep in mind if you want to take a more 
nuanced approach. Consider the role of 
the aromatics. Will they express their own 
unique freshness and delicacy, as in a lively 
court bouillon (vegetable broth) or vegetable 
fricassée, in which each vegetable gets added 
to the stew according to its cooking time?

The granddaddy 
of aromatic bases, 
mirepoix is classically 
defined as two parts 
onion to one part each 
celery and carrot.

Or will the fl avors quietly meld together 
during cooking to create a subtle back-
ground in which no ingredient dominates: 
for instance, in a longer-cooking broth or 
stock? If you’re making a long-cooking 
stock, there’s no need to chop the vegetables 
at all; and if you’re in a time crunch, don’t 
even bother with peeling. 
Another consideration is how you cut 
the aromatics. Root vegetables are oft en 
fi nely diced, with the size of the dice 
dependent on the cooking time. Th e fi nest 
dice, brunoise, is cut about ⅛ inch on each 
side. Such fi nely diced vegetables cook very 
quickly, and they are almost always left  in 
the fi nal dish. Larger dice, called mace-
doine, is about ¼ inch on each side and is 
used in dishes with longer cooking times. 
Some dishes may contain vegetables cut 
into matchstick-size pieces—julienne—
about ⅛ inch thick or even thinner on each 
side. When deciding how to chop vegeta-
bles, consider the formality of the meal. 
It’s no big deal if a soup containing 
roughly cut root vegetables appears 
at a casual dinner, but for a special 
occasion, aromatics should be 
evenly diced or julienned. 
Diff erent cooking techniques have 
diff erent eff ects on chopped aromatics. 
Diced carrots, for example, poached in 
a small amount of liquid become tender 
without browning while diced carrots 
sautéed in oil become browned and sweet 
as they cook. If the chopped aromatics 
are going to be part of a braised dish, they 
usually “sweat” fi rst in a small amount of 
fat. Sweating and sautéing are two diff erent 
processes that produce completely diff er-
ent results. Sweating—slow cooking in fat 
while partially covered—causes the juices 
in the vegetables to release slowly. Sautéing 
quickly browns the outside of the vegetable

France during l’heure de l’apéritif is 
something special: Packed cafés bright with 
conversation and golden light. Bottles and glasses and 
small bites littered across tables. Friends and lovers and 
friends who might become lovers meeting to celebrate 
the start of the night. But this early-evening practice of 
drinks and snacks doesn’t have to mean a night out on 
the town. In fact, French apéritif culture can be just as 
magical at home.
Th e key to throwing cocktail hour the French way is to 
keep the preparations simple and to put in the bulk of the 
eff ort long before the doorbell rings.

A holiday roast must be delicious, 
showstopping, and memorable—a 
true centerpiece. I’m here to show you how 
it can be all those things without being compli-
cated to make. A few top-notch ingredients and 
the right techniques are all you need to create a 
perfectly cooked, supremely flavorful roast with 
an extra-special must-have feature: a killer crust.
Let’s start with the technique
It’s always wise to season a roast thoroughly with 
salt in advance of cooking. Salt is the primary fla-
vor enhancer for all types of meats, and the earlier 
you salt a roast, the more that salt flavor perme-
ates throughout.
When salt makes contact with meat, it initially 
attracts moisture to the surface. Then, over time, 
the water reverses course, and the salty liquid  
is drawn into the depths of the roast, just like  
a brine. 
This is why the three recipes that follow—two 
for beef and one for pork—each instruct you to 
season the meat at least four hours and up to two 
days in advance. 
Contrary to popular belief, there’s no real point 
in leaving a large roast at room temperature for 
an hour or two because it doesn’t measurably 
increase the internal temperature of the meat. It 
actually takes 10 hours for a 4½-lb. pork roast to 
come to room temperature. Also, bringing meat to 
room temperature does not speed cooking time. 
So while it’s been a standard instruction for roast 
recipes for ages, a sizable body of evidence now 
shows that you absolutely can roast straight from 
the fridge. A cold start also helps to maintain even 
internal temperatures while cooking to achieve 
that perfect uniform medium-rare finish.
The final crucial roasting technique I rely on for 
any large meat cut is low and slow cooking. Each 
of these recipes employs low-temperature roasting 
(below 400°F). This allows the meat to cook more 
evenly from center to edge, so you end up with a 
super tender roast that’s cooked to the doneness 
you prefer. Hitting that perfect doneness doesn’t 
need to be a big mystery. Using an instant-read 
thermometer takes away the guesswork. 
Now, about that crust...
Meat crust forms in the oven due to a chemical 
process called the Maillard reaction, in which 
amino acids in the proteins along with simple sug-
ars react to heat over time at temperatures 300°F 
and above. To accentuate this natural crusting 
reaction, I add another layer of crust built from 
everyday ingredients.
Taking inspiration from salt-crusted versions, I 
give my prime rib roast a twist by seasoning it like 
porchetta and coating it with egg white and then an 
herb-salt mixture for the final crispy crust. For the 
pork roast, I rely on dark brown sugar in a spiced 
sweet-and-sour crust reminiscent of mostarda. And 
for the tenderloin, I adapt frico, the baked Italian 
cheese crisps, to encase the beef within a crispy, 
savory Parmesan and black-pepper crust.
Avoid the truss
Trussing these roasts with string would interfere 
with the gorgeous crusts that make them special. 
You’ll get the most magnificent results if you skip 
the truss.
Any one of these three holiday-worthy roasts 
would be a showstopper on your table. And each 
perfectly tender slice will deliver morsels of crust 
that flavors every succulent bite.

herb and sea-salt crusted prime rib
This recipe takes inspiration from porchetta. A garlic-and-
herb rub is inserted into incisions in the meat, followed by a 
flaky-salt-and-herb crust bonded with whipped egg white. 
Reverse-searing is ideal for a large roast like this: I used an

rib and then a final blast of oven heat to brown the crust.

citrus-crusted pork rib roast
A bone-in roast looks more impressive than a stan-
dard pork loin; the meat is marbled (just like prime 
rib) and is exceedingly tender. This crust is inspired by 
mostarda, so it includes citrusy flavorings—including 
juniper berry, coriander, bay leaves, and lemon peel—
on top of an orange-marmalade glaze. Slow-roasting 
ensures that the crust browns without burning the 
sugars and that the roast comes out super moist.  
Serves 8 to 10
 
 Olive oil, as needed
 
1   8- or 10-bone pork rib roast, chine  
bone removed (about 5 lb.), bones frenched
 
  Kosher salt and freshly ground black pepper
 
13  cup orange marmalade 
 
1  Tbs. Dijon mustard
 
3  Tbs. dark brown sugar
 
2  Tbs. ground coriander
 
2  Tbs. ground juniper berries
 
1  Tbs. dried lemon peel
 
7 bay leaves, finely ground (1 tsp.)
 
½  tsp. ground allspice
Line a large rimmed baking sheet with a silicone baking mat or 
parchment. Oil a large flat rack that fits inside the baking sheet.
Using a sharp thin-bladed knife, trim all the fat cap on the 
top of the pork roast to expose the meat. Put the roast on 
a large piece of parchment, pat dry with paper towels, and 
season thoroughly with 1 Tbs. salt and 1½ tsp. pepper. 
Wrap the exposed bone ends with foil. Combine the mar-
malade with the mustard in a small bowl, and brush evenly  
all over the roast.
In a small bowl, combine the sugar, coriander, juniper ber-
ries, lemon peel, bay leaves, and allspice, and blend well. Coat 
the pork all over with the spice mixture. 
Put the roast on the prepared rack, and press any of the 
spice mixture that has come loose onto the top and sides of 
the roast. Cover with parchment, and refrigerate for at least 
4 hours or up to 2 days. 
When you’re ready to cook the roast, position a rack in 
the center of the oven, and heat the oven to 325°F. Put the 
roast directly into the oven from the refrigerator. Cook until 
an instant-read thermometer inserted into the thickest part 
reads 135°F and the crust is deeply browned, 75 to 90 min-
utes. Remove from the oven, and let the roast cool uncovered 
for 20 to 30 minutes to set the crust. Transfer to a cutting 
board, remove the foil from the bones, and carve between the 
bones to make chops for serving.

There is something special 
about a gratin; serving one 
turns any meal into a feast. 
Th e luxurious aspect of a gratin makes 
it an ideal choice for entertaining. In my 
experience, guests are always fi lled with 
great anticipation for the meal to come 
when they see a gratin emerge from 
the oven.
Th e history of this classic dish is 
long. Th e name gratin comes from the 
French word gratte, meaning “scraped.” 
Th is was originally a reference to the 
thin, tasty crust formed at the bottom 
of a pot or pan that could be scraped 
up and eaten. Nowadays, the term is 
used for baked dishes that have a golden 
crust on their surface, an eff ect oft en 
created by sprinkling grated cheese 
and breadcrumbs on top and broiling 
aft er baking. Key to a gratin’s charm 
is the combination of textures that it 
off ers: the melting, tender center, oft en 
enriched with cream or cheese, con-
trasting with that appealing crisp crust, 
created as a protective layer as it bubbles 
away in the oven.
Th e best-known example of a gratin 
in French cuisine is potatoes a la Dau-
phinois, a rich, creamy potato
dish from the region of Dauphine. 
While some recipes for Dauphinois 
call for eggs or cheese—or indeed both 
eggs and cheese—to be used in addition 
to cream, Elizabeth David in the classic 
French Provincial Cooking opted simply 
for cream, observing: “And if it seems 
to the thrift ily-minded, outrageously 
extravagant to use half a pint of cream 
to one pound of potatoes, I can only say 
that to me it seems a more satisfactory 
way of enjoying cream than pouring 
it over tinned peaches or chocolate 
mousse.” Another fi ne example of a 
potato gratin dish is Sweden’s Jans-
son’s Temptation. Th e source of the 
appealing name remains a mystery, 
incidentally, as no one knows who 
Jansson was. Th is much-loved classic 
of Swedish cuisine is traditionally 
served as a main course rather than as 
an accompaniment. Made from fi nely 
chopped potatoes, it’s a simple, homely 
meal fl avored with fried onions and

While both of these classics call for 
potatoes, the gratin is actually a versatile 
casserole that can be made from a variety of 
ingredients. As the following recipes dem-

Making a gratin involves a certain amount 
of preparation, especially if the peeling and 
slicing of the vegetables are done by hand.

and is best treated as an opportunity to slow

cream or a more time-consuming roux-

been assembled, it can be put in the oven and 
left  alone to cook, allowing you ample time 
to get on with the rest of the meal or simply 
await your gratin while you relax by a fi re. 
One last bonus of gratins, especially for 
entertaining: Th ey are the ultimate make-
ahead dishes. Th ey can be assembled ahead 
and refrigerated overnight before baking 
on the day, or they can be baked ahead and

“Evelyn dropped off the  
rugelach!” my mother texts 
my sister and me to announce 
the annual cookie delivery from a 
dear family friend just in time for 
Hanukkah. Any plans are forgone 
as I rush to my mother’s, where a 
giant aluminum tray packed with 
dozens of cookies filled with dark 
chocolate or cinnamon and wal-
nuts awaits, ready to be divvied up 
among our family. 
While chocolate chip cookies 
are great and a buttery shortbread 
is lovely, for me nothing stands up 
to a tray of freshly baked rugelach. 
This Ashkenazi-Jewish confec-
tion has been near and dear to my 
heart for as long as I can remem-
ber. Everyone has that one sweet 
from their childhood that brings 
up all those fuzzy nostalgic vibes, 
and mine is rugelach. The classic 
features a cream-cheese dough 
coated with anything from choco-
late, jam, or nuts before it’s cut 
and rolled to make a flaky cookie 
singing with flavor. 
While most tend to keep it 
traditional when it comes to their 
fillings, I like to spice things up with 
my rugelach (both my pumpkin-
spice and my hot pepper jelly 
rugelach are game changers). Let 
me remind you that it is completely 
kosher to get a little creative with 
the fillings, and yes, that includes 
venturing into the realm of savory. 
Just as these cookies make the ideal 
sweet finish to a meal or nibble 
with a cup of coffee, the dough is 
versatile enough to stand up to just 
about any savory filling you can 
dream up. These savory versions 
make for addictive party apps.
If it’s your first time trying 
your hand at this Jewish pastry, 
I’ll share a few tips. Rugelach is a 
perfect recipe to prep in advance. 
Make sure your dough is properly 
chilled so that you don’t have any 
issues rolling the cookies up clean-
ly. (I like to make a few batches of 
the dough and freeze them to have 
on hand for last-minute entertain-
ing or a late-night sweet tooth.) 
Same goes for the fillings. You 
want them to be easily spreadable 
so that they don’t tear the dough. 
They also shouldn’t be warm, or 
you’ll end up with a mess as you 
roll and bake.
While I’m not sure if Evelyn 
would approve of my modern 
take on rugelach compared to her 
classic beauties, there’s definitely a 
place for both in my kitchen—and 
in my stomach.

While the garnishes make the cake look spectacular,

I call a “wrapping” technique to assemble the cake. 
After baking the sponge cake in a large rimmed bak-
ing sheet, I cut it into five strips. To train the shape 
of the innermost strip of cake and avoid cracking in 
the final product, I roll up one strip in paper towels 
while it’s still warm, just as you would when you’re 
making a jelly roll. I then roll the remaining strips 
up in a tight spiral together to train them. The ends 
of these strips are cut on an angle to create beveled 
edges; this step helps the strips to lie flat as the cake 
is assembled. 
I start building the cake by brushing the inner-
most strip with soaking syrup and then coating  
it with white chocolate-espresso ganache. After  
this center piece is rolled and positioned on a plate,  
I brush, fill, and wrap the remaining four beveled 
cake strips one at a time around it. The technique 
sounds complicated, but as you’ll see, it’s an easy-to-
follow process.

The bartender’s arsenal
Whether you like your martini shaken or stirred, you’ll need 
to set up a well-equipped bar to properly prepare cocktails at 
home. To help you organize the required tools of the trade, 
here’s a list of must-haves.
Jiggers are the measuring cups of the drinks trade. Get your  
proportions right by using a jigger clearly marked with the 
most common liquid amounts called for in cocktail recipes. 
A shaker brings a drink together. Shakers can be made of stain-
less steel, glass, or even copper. Fill with ice and cocktail ingre-
dients, shake vigorously to combine, and strain into a glass. 
While some shakers come equipped with built-in strainers,  
it’s always useful to have a Hawthorne strainer as well. These  
bar strainers are designed to fit snugly on the top of a standard 
mixing glass. The metal spring around the edge works to hold 
back ice when pouring a finished cocktail into a serving glass.
For cocktails that need stirring, rather than shaking, use a  
bar spoon. These long-handled spoons are designed to reach  
the bottom of a mixing glass. And the spoon end can scoop up 
any garnishes you wish to transfer to the serving glass. 
One of the simplest yet most integral tools in the  
bartender’s arsenal is a common bottle opener.  
Use it to open tonics and other mixers. 
Muddlers are used to crush herbs, fruits, and other 
solid ingredients in cocktails, eliciting their essen-
tial oils and adding flavor to the final drink.

The croque
monsieur
This beloved French bistro 
sandwich is a decadent mix of 
crispy white bread, thinly sliced 
ham, and gooey melted cheese, 
most commonly Gruyère or Emmentaler. 
The name translates to “bite” and “mister.” The crispy bite refers to 
the toasted white bread (or pan de mie) that should always be used in 
building it. One legend has it that the sandwich originated in a Paris bras-
serie on the fashionable Boulevard des Capucines. When a customer 
asked where the ham had come from, the chef pointed to the local 
butcher and said, “It’s that guy’s meat.” Hence, the mister in the name 
croque monsieur.       
Crème fraîche vs. sour cream 
Both are creamy and tangy. Both 
can be used to create dips (as in 
Rebekah Peppler’s luscious Cara-
melized Onion and Mushroom 
Dip on p. 63), to thicken sauces, 
and to create tender-textured 
baked goods. 
At heart, though, these are two 
very diff erent foods. For starters, 
crème fraîche boasts an indulgent 
45 percent butterfat content, while 
sour cream has a mere 20 percent 
butterfat. Th at distinction gives 
crème fraîche its creamier, more 
velvety texture, and the higher fat 
content protects it from curdling 
when added gradually to dishes 
that are being heated. And while 
sour cream is oft en thickened 
with rennins or gelatin, crème 
fraîche comes by its texture 
naturally. 
Sour cream, as its name would 
suggest, features a sharper tang 
than crème fraîche, making it a 
great choice for mellowing out 
spicy dishes. It’s best dolloped on 
fi nished dishes, off  the heat.
A fi nal diff erence between 
the two? Price. All that added 
butterfat gives crème fraîche a 
heft ier price tag. It can be up to 
three times more expensive than 
sour cream.

When it comes to beef, tenderloin is one of the priciest cuts.

How to trim a beef tenderloin
With a boning knife, start at the 
narrow end of the chain and cut 
along the seam, making long, 
shallow cuts to control your knife 
path so that there is less chance of 
cutting too far in. 
Locate the chain, the long piece 
of muscle that runs the length of 
the tenderloin. Using your fingers, 
gently separate the chain from the 
tenderloin as much as possible.
Turn the knife around and cut off 
the end of the silverskin that’s still 
attached. Repeat until all the silver-
skin is gone.
Starting about 1 inch from one end of 
the silverskin, push the knife under a 
strip of silverskin about ½ inch wide. 
Hold the silverskin taut, angling the 
knife slightly upward as you slide it 
down the tenderloin to free it.

HISTORY
Chicken cacciatore 
(the hunter’s stew)
While most modern chicken cacciatore recipes 
are for chicken stewed in a rich tomato sauce, 
the traditional dish does not, in fact, feature 
tomatoes. During the Italian Renaissance, when 
hunters first developed this recipe to satisfy 
their appetites at the end of a long hunt, toma-
toes hadn’t even been introduced to Italy from 
the New World. Instead, hungry hunters would 
cook down dark-meat pieces of rabbit in a stew 
of wine and vinegar (white wine in northern Italy 
and red wine in the south). To the stew, they 
would add earthy mushrooms and herbs that 
were plentiful in the forests through which they 
rode. What hasn’t changed is how hearty and 
satisfying this dish feels on a cold winter day.

I recently found myself in a bit of a farro rut. It seemed to be making 
an appearance in every salad, soup, and side I churned out of my 
kitchen. In order to change things up, I started using freekeh in its 
place. To my delight, it proved a fine substitute. Made from durum 
wheat that’s harvested young, freekeh is pleasantly chewy to the bite 
and has a nutty flavor, not unlike farro. But because it’s roasted when 
it’s processed, it also boasts a uniquely smoky taste. For quicker 
cooking, choose cracked freekeh, which cooks in about 20 minutes, 
over whole, which can take as long as 45 to 50 minutes to soften.

Peeling celery root
Celery root, aka celeriac, is not the prettiest 
vegetable, but it is downright delicious. Peel-
ing back its hairy, knobby exterior is easy.
1 Start by trimming both the stem and stalk 
ends to create a level surface on which to rest 
the root. Th en, using a chef’s knife, slice off  any 
knobby protuberances by slicing down in strips, following the natural 
curve of the root. 2 Finally, use a vegetable peeler to remove any 
remaining skin. Be sure to have acidulated water on hand as this root 
browns easily.
And in case you were wondering, celery root is indeed the root of a 
celery plant. It’s not, however, the root of the celery variety that you’ll 
fi nd in most supermarkets.
