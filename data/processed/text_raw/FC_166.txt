NOTE FROM THE EDITOR
Now’s the Time
Suﬃce it to say the summer of 2020 is unlike any that’s come before. COVID-19 kept us all at home and 
inside for months. Now, with the warmer weather, socializing outdoors (and at a distance) is more popular 
than ever—and that involves cooking and dining, too. 
With local farm stands bursting with just-picked produce and gardens overﬂowing with vegetables and 
herbs (seems everyone has a green thumb these days), late summer is a dream time for cooks. Tis issue 
capitalizes on all the season has to oﬀer—from sizzling grilled dishes to vibrant sides to sweet desserts. Dine 
alfresco with author Anna Gass’s regional Italian menu (p. 42) that celebrates the season with bold, fresh 
ﬂavors. Her blueberry sauce for grilled ﬁlet mignon is incredible. Take a cue from Jessica Bard (p. 35) on how 
to use imaginative trios of fresh herbs to create ﬂavors that will boost your recipes to delicious new heights. 
And if you thought the slow cooker was only for winter soups and stews, think again. Tis versatile appliance 
is also a handy tool for summer meals (p. 54). It won’t heat up the kitchen, and it turns peak-season 
ingredients into delectable meals—that’s a win-win! Plus, we have small-batch jams with tantalizing ﬂavor 
combinations, such as peach-rosemary and grape tomato-cayenne (p. 60); refreshing zero-proof drinks  
(p. 24); inventive takes on arugula (p. 50); and much more. For the proverbial cherry on top, Jessie Sheehan 
shares her recipes for no-churn ice creams (p. 70) that are rich, creamy, and downright swoonworthy.
Because things around the world are not the norm, we’ve also highlighted some of the national 
organizations that are helping to feed those in need during the COVID-19 pandemic and beyond. Tese 
tireless groups step up to ﬁght food insecurity through expanding food pantries, delivering meals, feeding 
frontline workers, and helping farmers keep farming. Turn to page p. 26 to learn how you can support their 
important missions.
Enjoy!
Kathy Kingsley

In Season
Eggplant
T e time is now to cook with peak-
season eggplant. T e many varieties 
available in late summer provide 
opportunities to savor this versatile 
produce in soups, salads, dips, and more.
A member of the nightshade family 
(which includes tomatoes, peppers, 
and potatoes), eggplant—a fruit—is 
cooked and eaten like a vegetable. 
Eggplant varieties range in color from 
pale green and lavender to ﬁ ery orange 
and bright white, and they are largely 
interchangeable in recipes. Shapes 
vary from long and skinny to bulbous, 
oblong, and round. Most commonly 
grown and consumed are deep purple 
globe (American) eggplants; their thick 
skins can turn leathery when cooked, so 
recipes of en call for thin strips or slices. 
Other varieties include Asian (Chinese 
or Japanese), which have tender skins 
and creamy ﬂ esh. T ey are either long 
and slender or small and round. Italian 
eggplants are similar to globe, but 
smaller, and are available in a wide range 
of colors. Indian eggplants (small or 
baby) are smaller and less bitter than 
their counterparts.

Miso Eggplant
Slice 2 Asian eggplants in half lengthwise. 
Brush lightly with sesame oil, and roast until 
tender. Meanwhile, whisk white miso, warm 
water, mirin, granulated sugar, sesame 
oil, and granulated garlic. Spoon the glaze 
evenly over the cut sides of the eggplant, 
return to the oven, and broil until lightly 
charred in places. —Diana Andrews
Late-Summer Vegetable Gratin
Slice an Italian eggplant, a few tomatoes, 
and a couple of zucchini. Sprinkle the 
vegetable slices with salt and black pepper, 
and arrange in a baking dish. Drizzle with 
olive oil and top with panko, 
Parmigiano-Reggiano, and 
chopped fresh flat-leaf 
parsley. Bake until the 
vegetables are tender 
and the topping is 
browned, about 
25 minutes.
—Sarah Opdahl
make » 
Smoky Eggplant Dip
Grill a globe or Italian eggplant until soft 
and charred. Scoop the flesh into a food 
processor, along with a little bit of the skin, 
some tahini, lemon juice, cumin, salt, and a 
touch of honey. Purée until smooth.
—Sarah Breckenridge
Marinated Eggplant with Herbs
Cut an Italian eggplant into ½  -inch-thick 
slices. Put the slices on a large rimmed 
baking sheet lined with paper towels, and 
salt lightly. After 30 minutes, blot dry with 
paper towels. Fry the eggplant in olive oil 
until lightly browned on both sides and 
fork-tender. Transfer to a large rimmed 
baking sheet. Drizzle lightly with extra-virgin 
olive oil, season with kosher salt and freshly 
ground black pepper, and sprinkle with 
chopped tender fresh herbs, such as flat-
leaf parsley, basil, chives, and thyme. Let 
marinate for 30 to 60 minutes, then use as 
a pizza topping, in salads, or as a sandwich 
filling. —Chris Hoelck
Caponata
In a large nonstick skillet, cook garlic and 
shallots, then add a cubed globe eggplant 
and kosher salt. Cook until browned and 
softened. Add a can of tomatoes and cook, 
seasoning to taste with more salt. Add 
tomato paste, and cook until the caponata 
has a chunky consistency. Finish with 
fresh basil, capers, or olives and a drizzle 
of balsamic glaze. Serve on grilled chicken 
breast, fish, steak, or bread. —Jodie Delohery
Eggplant, Quinoa, and Arugula Salad
Cube 2 Asian eggplants, toss with olive oil, 
salt, and black pepper, and roast at 400°F 
until tender, about 20 minutes. Toss cooled, 
cooked quinoa with olive oil, white wine 
vinegar, salt, and pepper. Line serving plates 
with arugula and top with quinoa, then the 
eggplant. —Kathy Kingsley

T ough available year-round, eggplant is at its peak from mid- to 
late summer. Look for eggplant that is evenly ﬁ rm; it can be bitter 
when either under- or overripe, so choose fruit that springs back 
slightly when touched. It should have smooth, shiny, unwrinkled 
skin with no brown streaks or spots, and a healthy green top. It’s 
best to handle eggplant carefully, as it bruises easily.
store »
Store eggplant at room temperature away from direct sunlight. 
Do not store it in the refrigerator unless your kitchen is very hot 
or if you won’t be using it within a couple of days. You can store it 
in the crisper section of the refrigerator 5 to 7 days. 
prep »
Depending on the recipe, eggplant may or may not need peeling. 
If you salt eggplant ﬂ esh and let it sit for 30 minutes before 
cooking, the salt will draw out water and help collapse the 
air pockets in globe eggplant’s spongy ﬂ esh. T is makes the 
eggplant less apt to soak up lots of oil during cooking. Eggplant 
is best when completely cooked. At that point, it will be at its 
most ﬂ avorful and will meld beautifully with other ingredients.
Persian Eggplant Stew
Fry globe eggplant and Roma tomato slices 
in olive oil, then drain and set aside. In a 
Dutch oven, cook onions, then add garlic 
and beef stew meat. Season with turmeric, 
allspice, chili powder, cinnamon, salt, and 
pepper. Cook until meat is browned on all 
sides. Add tomato paste and water to cover. 
Cover and cook over low heat for 2½ hours, 
adding more water if needed. Nestle the 
eggplant and tomato slices into the stew and 
cook until heated through. Season to taste 
and serve over basmati rice. —Sahar Cox

Filipino Tortang Talong
Grill or broil a whole Asian eggplant until the 
skin is charred and blistered. Eggplant should 
become soft and may burst, which is OK. 
Peel the eggplant when cool, press the flesh 
flat with a fork, and sprinkle generously with 
kosher salt and pepper. Dip the eggplant in 
beaten eggs, and fry in a few tablespoons 
of oil until brown on both sides. Serve with 
ponzu or soy sauce and rice. —Joann Umeki

Menu
Summer Supper  
Starring
Fried Chicken  
Sandwiches
I KNOW SUMMER is all about grilling. And 
believe me—I love grilling, too. But come 
late summer, when the nights begin to cool 
a bit, I’m ready for something different. 
Some texture and crunch. Some oily 
goodness. That’s when I know it’s time to 
treat myself and my family to fried chicken 
sandwiches. Yeah, yeah, your kitchen 
might get a little hotter when you heat 
the oil for frying, but that’s why the gods 
invented air-conditioning—or at least a fan. 
And after one bite of these tender, crackly, 
chicken thighs topped with a sweet-hot 
butter and tangy pickles, you’ll hardly 
notice those beads of sweat. 
The rest of the supper, except for 
dessert, requires no additional heat and 
uses up your garden’s (or your friend’s 
garden’s) goodies. Start with a super-easy 
cold cucumber soup. Clean-tasting and  
refreshing, it won’t weigh you down before 
the main course. 
As for the main course, believe me 
when I say what you want to fry up are 
chicken thighs. Between their deeply 
flavored dark meat and all those nooks 
and crannies for holding the batter, thighs 
are simply the best part of the bird for 
the frying pan. And they cook quickly, too. 
Then there’s the butter that goes on top: 
It take seconds to whip up, but it packs a 
lot of wow. Make the extra effort and buy 
some flaky sea salt to add to it. That little 
bit of crunch makes all the difference. 
Served with the sandwich is an easy 
salad you can adjust to what you have on 
hand. I like spinach, corn, and tomatoes, 
but you could swap in another green or 
even sliced green beans in place of the 
spinach. As for the corn, if it’s prime-
picked, you may not even have to cook it. 
Just taste it raw, and if you like it, skip the 
boiling. A little blue cheese in the salad 
pairs perfectly with the hot (though not 
quite Buffalo hot) chicken.
Finally, dessert. (Yes, you can fit it in 
after that sandwich, which really isn’t that 
big.) An easy cobbler filled with summer’s 
best fruit rounds out the homey menu. It’s 
simple on purpose, because that’s what 
summer eating is all about—even when 
you’re not grilling.

Put the chicken legs in a wide Dutch oven 
or deep saucepan. Distribute the garlic 
halves, quartered onions, star anise, garlic, 
cinnamon, bay leaf, chiles, orange peel, 
lemon peel, cilantro stems, peppercorns,

cover by 1½ inches, and bring to a boil

vinegar to taste, and loosely cover with foil.

liquid is bubbling and the onions soften, 
about 30 minutes. Uncover, increase 
oven temperature to 375°F, and continue 
to cook until top is golden brown, about 
20 minutes. Remove from the oven, and 
let cool slightly. Serve warm or at room

cilantro leaves.

happier than cooking Italian food for family and friends. 
While I love experimenting and enjoying cuisines from 
all over the world, preparing Italian food makes me feel 
at home. When hosting guests in the summer months, 
I also embrace the Italian tradition of eating alfresco. For 
this menu, I encourage you to spruce up an exterior dining 
area, ﬁ re up the grill, and take your party outside.
Because Italy is a country deﬁ ned by a broad diversity 
of landscapes and climates, each microregion specializes 
in and honors a unique cuisine that has evolved 
over the centuries and is based on the availability of local 
ingredients and livestock (or seafood). Eating farm 
(or boat) to table has been and continues to be the 
Italian way of life.
For this menu, I celebrate the country I love by 
showcasing the dishes I’ve enjoyed during my annual 
trips there. To streamline the prep and cooking and allow 
you to spend maximum time with your guests, I have 
also signaled the components that can be made ahead. 
Some can be made the day before, some just before 
company arrives, and others added to the grill as guests 
are walking in the door.  
I’m looking forward to my next trip to my beloved 
homeland, but for now, I will open up a good 
Montepulciano (a delicious Tuscan wine and my favorite 
pairing with ﬁ let mignon), enjoy the summer breeze, 
and celebrate the dishes of la cucina Italiana.

ALL HAIL THE SLOW COOKER, the perfect small appliance for summer!  
What, you say? Te slow cooker is not just a winter thing, best saved for cold January 
days when there’s nothing better than a hearty stew to fend oﬀ the chill of a polar 
vortex? Of course, that aspect of slow-cooking is legit, and there’s nothing like loading

I think the slow cooker has a chance to play an unexpected role in the summer. For

also a great way to turn peak-season ingredients into delicious meals with minimal 
prep time. Case in point: ratatouille (p. 58), whose ﬂavor only gets better as

making a cheesecake is deﬁnitely not a traditional use for this appliance, its low,  
even heat creates the perfect environment for a rich cheesecake  
served with sweet macerated peaches (p. 59). Afer you enjoy these dishes, 
I bet your slow cooker won’t take a seasonal hiatus again.

Storing fresh herbs
For tender herbs like cilantro, chives, dill, 
mint, tarragon, and parsley, discard their twist 
ties or rubber bands. Trim the lower parts of 
the stems, rinse under cold water to remove 
dirt and bacteria, then give them a whirl in 
a salad spinner until very dry. Roll the herb 
bundles in paper towels and store them in 
zip-lock bags in the crisper drawer of your 
refrigerator. Our current favorite way to store 
basil is in a jar with a bit of water that just 
reaches the bottoms of the stems. Cover the 
bouquet on the countertop with a plastic bag.
Hardy herbs like rosemary, sage, oregano, 
bay leaves, and thyme will last longer than 
tender herbs but should remain dry and 
refrigerated until ready to use.

Cheesecake in a

There are lots of great reasons 
to use a slow cooker to make

even heat while steaming the

HISTORY
Did you know that stuffed eggs have 
been around since the days of ancient 
Rome? They were so popular that they 
were always the first course served when 
entertaining the elite class. Yolks were 
mixed with ingredients such as oil, wine, 
broth, nuts, raisins, sauces, and herbs. 
Stuffed eggs made their way across 
Europe by the 15th century, although they 
make an appearance in cookbooks as 
early as the 13th century. The word deviled 
began to be used in the late 1600s in Great 
Britain to describe dishes that included 
spicy ingredients, although the term was 
 banned in some parts of the world to avoid 
being associated with the devil.

homemade chocolate shell
You can’t knock a good pour of hot fudge over a scoop of 
ice cream, but nothing brings back childhood memories 
like the magic of a crisp, chocolate-shell topping.
Makes about 1 cup
 
8  oz. milk chocolate, finely chopped
 
3 Tbs. coconut oil
 
 Pinch kosher salt
In a small saucepan, combine the chocolate and oil. Melt over low 
heat, stirring frequently, until smooth and uniformly liquid. (Or 
put the chocolate and oil in a microwave-safe bowl. Microwave 
at 50-percent power in 30-second increments, stirring every 
30 seconds, until smooth and uniformly liquid.) Stir in the salt. 
Transfer to a resealable container. Use at room temperature on 
top of your favorite ice cream or frozen yogurt. Store at room 
temperature for up to 14 days.

Capers
Piquant capers are the cured unopened buds of the 
caper shrub. Because the shrub grows along the 
Mediterranean coast, it’s no surprise that capers are a 
key ingredient in many Italian and Greek dishes. They 
add a great bite of tangy flavor to other dishes, too, 
including the deviled eggs on page 36.
Capers are available either salted or in brine. If 
salted, they should be rinsed before use; if in brine, 
capers should be drained. Capers are sold by size; 
typically, the smallest variety—nonpareil—are preferred.
Try capers in pasta salad, with pan-fried fish fillets, or in 
tartar sauce. Keep in mind that even though they’re small, 
they bring a big hit of flavor. A little goes a long way.
