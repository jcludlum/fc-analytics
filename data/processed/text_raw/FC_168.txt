EDITOR’S LETTER

Why Cooking Is Still Important

The past twelve months have been challenging at best. We'll spare you the clichés (“unprecedented” and
“uncertain” times are phrases that may see themselves out in 2021), but as we approach the one-year mark of
the pandemic, there's something we know for sure: Cooking still matters.

For much of 2020, we relied on standbys to feed, fuel, and comfort ourselves and others: Big batches of
Bolognese were delegated to freezer bags so we always had something homemade on hand; chicken noodle
soup was divied into quart containers to be left on a friend’s front porch on a dime; decorated brown sugar
shortbread cookies were popped in the mail to celebrate the special moments, even if we couldn't celebrate in
person; and of course there was banana bread—loaves and loaves of banana bread.

We also ordered takeout. A lot of takeout. And we never felt better about it. Not just because it was our
way of supporting favorite local restaurants whose doors had shuttered for dine-in, but also because every
time we called for carry-out, we got the greatest culinary ideas.

This issue is inspired by the best of both cooking at home and getting to-go. Some techniques, like pan-
searing (p. 28) and braising (p. 46), might be familiar, but our test kitchen tricks will help you raise the bar.

Others might encourage you to try something new. Take inspiration from “Soup's On’ (p. 70) and grab
some baby bok choy for the Pork Ramen (p. 73), and then use it again for the Coconut-Curry Short Ribs
(p. 48). If youre feeling wanderlust after a year without travel, you can master paella (p. 76), and bring the
taste of Spain to your home kitchen. And if you're craving big, bold, punchy flavors, check out “Pucker Up”

(p. 54). There were audible “yums” as those in our office gobbled up the Roasted Lemon and Ricotta Flatbread
(p. 56) for lunch post-photo shoot.

Coming back to a few familiar favorites and honing some of our home-cooking skills has been a security
blanket for all of us here at Fine Cooking in the hailstorm that was 2020. But expanding our cooking
repertoires through new ingredients and flavors has been like opening a window and taking a big, deep
breath of much-welcome fresh air. We hope the recipes on these pages do the same for you.

Roasted, pickled, or eaten raw, this versatile root intrigues
with a splash of color and a hit of sweet, earthy flavor.

Beets

While you can find them in the
produce aisle year-round, the season
for this root peaks from November
through the late winter months.

The reddish-purple beets are the
most common, but you can also

find white, golden, and pink-and-
white striped (Chioggia or “candy
cane”) varieties. Regardless of color,
all are similar in flavor, with their
characteristic sweetness due to a high
sugar content. Beets have two distinct
parts—the root and the leafy green
top—and the latter is actually a close
relative to chard that can be prepared
similarly. But whichever you eat,
both have impressive nutritional
power, with the roots boasting high
levels of folate and manganese, and
the greens containing high amounts
of vitamin C.

the vegetable to be boiled first before
roasting, but we find the step unnecessary.
Just keeping the vegetable whole keeps it

WHAT IS BRAISING, REALLY? At its most basic, braising is
gently cooking tough cuts of meat in liquid at low temperatures. The

process breaks down the collagen that holds the meat together. And

you can apply the same technique to hearty vegetables, too. All you

need is a large heavy pot with a tight-fitting lid—a Dutch oven is an

excellent candidate for the job—and then follow a few simple steps.

You'll start by browning the meat, searing all the sides to develop

a dark golden-brown crust. During the process, those caramelized

flavors transfer to the cooking fat. Once the meat is browned,

youll remove it to make room for any combination of vegetables,

aromatics, herbs, and spices. As they sizzle in the fat, their flavors

will amplify and build complexity.

Next, it’s time to deglaze the pot. At this point there will be lots of

browned bits at the bottom of your pot. This is braising gold. Pour in

liquid (broth, wine, etc.), and use a wooden spoon to scrape up the

stuck-on bits. Now your work is pretty much done. Return the meat

to the pot, cover, and pop it in the oven for a low, slow cook. As the

meat cooks in this moist environment, the tough connective tissues

melt away to yield fall-from-the-bone tenderness.

STEPS TO
SUCCESS

1. BROWN THE MEAT

The goal is to brown the pieces
and get crispy bits stuck to the

Don’t rush the sofrito step—

intense flavor. Cook until thick
and darkened; it should look like

3. DEVELOP A FILM

As the rice absorbs the liquid,

a film will develop over the top.
This film traps steam so the rice
cooks evenly.

Unlike risotto, the paella must be
left alone once the rice is added.
To make sure the rice cooks
evenly, rotate the pan every

few minutes.

5. CRANK THE HEAT

The socarrat, or crust, develops
as the rice cooks, buta little blast
of heat at the end ensures a crisp
brown layer on the bottom.

6.LETIT REST

Allow 10 minutes to rest under
foil before serving. The rice

will continue to absorb any
remaining moisture as it stands.

Spice Spotlight

There are one-note spices, and then there’s za’atar. The
multifaceted Middle Eastern spice offers so much. It’s bright,
aromatic, woodsy, and toasty, and just like salt, it helps to
coax flavor out of food. Typically, it’s made with a mix of
dried thyme, oregano, marjoram, sumac, toasted sesame

seeds, and salt. You can find it in large supermarkets and

it’s widely available online, but you can also easily make

your own. In fact, you can even experiment with different
proportions to find the blend that best fits your taste. Start
with a combination of 2 Tbs. sumac; 1 Tbs. each dried thyme,
dried marjoram, and dried oregano; 2 tsp. toasted sesame
seeds; and % tsp. kosher salt, and go from there. Once you’ve
found your ideal blend, you can sprinkle it over just about
anything, like hard-boiled eggs, hummus, roast chicken—
you name it.

Homemade
Ricotta

Homemade ricotta has a creamy texture
and fresh flavor that the stuff in the tub
simply can’t rival. And this simplified
method is easy and foolproof. The
acidity from the white vinegar creates
those luscious curds, but lemon juice
works as well. The ricotta will keep in the
refrigerator for up to 1 one week. Try it
smeared on pizza or flatbread, make

a batch of lemon ricotta pancakes, or
swap it for your morning yogurt. To make
it, heat 4 cups whole milk and 1% cups
whipping cream over medium-high heat
just until boiling, stirring occasionally.
Remove from the heat, and stir in 2 Tbs.
white vinegar and 2 tsp. kosher salt.
Let stand 20 minutes. Line a colander
with a few layers of 100 percent cotton
cheesecloth, and set it over a large bowl.
Pour in the milk mixture. Let stand 1 hour.
Lift the ends of cheesecloth around the
cheese and squeeze gently. Discard the
whey. Transfer the cheese to a storage
container, cover, and chill up to 1 week.

Ramen

The story of ramen goes back long before people
were slurping salty, squiggly noodles out of
Styrofoam cups. While most consider ramen
Japanese in origin, scholars actually trace its roots
back to China. How it made its way there is still up
for debate, but what is agreed upon is that it earned
its original popularity when Tokyo's Rai-Rai Ken
restaurant opened in 1910, popularizing a Chinese
take on a soba dish called “shina soba.”

Ramen’ rapid rise was cut short due to food
shortages during World War II, but years later,
in 1958, Momofuku Ando revived the public’s
interest with Nissin Chikin Ramen, a shelf-stable
reinvention of the dish that cooked in 3 minutes
thanks to flash-fried noodles. Fast-forward to today,
and the ramen cult craze continues among both
home cooks perfecting their own recipes and trendy
noodle shops selling bowls for upwards of $18.

Orange Crush

Oranges have been hybridized for centuries, and you can find more
varieties than ever on supermarket shelves—especially during their
seasonal peak from winter into early spring. Most can be used

interchangeably in recipes, but each has unique characteristics that
make them good candidates for particular uses and dishes. Here's a

guide to a few of the most common:

NAVEL

Grown in California, Florida,
and Arizona, this popular type of
orange is widely available year-
round. It has a sweet, seedless flesh,
and a bright orange skin that’s easy
to peel, making it a great choice for
snacking. The circular hole on the
blossom end resembles a human
navel, hence the name.

VALENCIA

Named after the orchard-rich
groves of Valencia, Spain, Valencias
are available in the early spring
through summer. Many prefer this
variety for juicing, because they
tend to be juicier than others, and
their sweet-tart flavor is bright and
well-balanced. Plus, their thin, deep
golden skin can be difficult to peel.

CARA CARA

Discovered in Venezuela in the
mid-1970s, this cross between
two navel varieties is now grown
largely in California. They have
a pinkish-red flesh, and compared
to traditional navels, they tend
to be sweeter and less acidic with
notes of strawberry
and raspberry.

BLOOD

Anthocyanins—an antioxidant that
studies show could have an anti-
inflammatory effect—give this fruit
its brilliant red flesh. Blood oranges
have a thicker exterior, so they’re
easier to peel, and they’re prized
for their subtle berry flavor. Use in
place of other oranges to add an
extra layer of flavor and color.

Paella Pan

If you find yourself making paella on the regular, it might be time to
fa investina paella pan. Luckily, it doesn’t need to be a particularly costly
investment—you can find pans at just about every price point. Paella
pans are wide and shallow, which allows for maximum surface space. This
is important because the larger the surface, the more liquid can evaporate
from the rice to form acrisp, caramelized bottom crust (soccorat).
Pans range anywhere from 4 inches for a mini single-serving to 60 inches,
which can feed up to 300. For most home cooks, a 16- to 22-inch pan will do
the job. You'll also find pans made from three different materials. Carbon
steel is traditional, but does need to be seasoned and requires some
additional maintenance. Stainless steel doesn’t require any extra
upkeep and will last a lifetime, but does tend to be more costly.
Last, there’s enameled steel, which is light, affordable, and
easy to clean. The only downside is that the pan can chip if
banged around too much.
