Sign of the Times

Broad-leaved ramps. Alabaster asparagus spears. Spongy, cone-shaped
morels. Is it any coincidence that some of the most prized produce gems
make their appearance in the spring? Perhaps they are a little gift from
nature, a thank you for enduring the dark, seemingly endless days of winter.

In this issue, we're celebrating all of those culinary cues that remind us
of the hope and optimism that this season often brings. For example, Chef
Bryant Terry invites you to come out of hibernation—in more ways than
one—with a lesson in foraging. Inspired by a Saturday stroll in his Bay Area
neighborhood, he shares a plant-based menu (p. 60), featuring ingredients
found growing near his home, like wild spring onions, chamomile, and
lemon balm.

Other dishes feature more familiar ingredients prepared in clever new
ways. On p. 68, Rachel Gurjar explains why you should be cooking your
pasta in the style of risotto, offering a collection of weeknight recipes that
can be adapted to spotlight your favorite seasonal flavors. (Oh, and they
only require one pot, too!) Plus, frequent contributor Ivy Manning explores
the world of eggs (p. 48), offering up simple, yet refined dishes that feature
not only chicken eggs, but duck and quail as well.

And although we might not be gathering en masse just yet, we're still
looking forward to marking holidays and special occasions. You could
try a genius new take on ham (p. 24) as the star of a simple brunch menu,
bake a beautiful homemade cake for mom (p. 82), or wow your family by
baking babka from scratch (p. 56) thanks to Fine Cooking alum Samantha
Seneviratne's expert guidance.

But however you decide to take advantage of this change of seasons, we
hope these pages offer plenty of delicious inspiration.

In Season

The bright green stalks are one of the first signs of spring. But don’t miss
out on their pale cousins, whose season is even more fleeting.

White Asparagus

Its appearance is so brief that if you
blink, you might miss it. But it’s
worth seeking out this vegetable
delicacy during its short window

of availability. Popular in Europe—
especially Germany—the white ;
variety has a subtle sweetness and

a more tender texture and milder
flavor than green. But the white
variety isn't genetically different from
its green counterpart. Rather, the
spears’ pale color is due to the fact
that they are grown under mulch or
thick tarps that prevent sunlight from
reaching them, so they don’t form

the chlorophyll that makes the plants
green. And because they are more
labor-intensive to grow, they tend to
be significantly more expensive than
green asparagus.

You can find white asparagus at farmers markets and well-stocked
grocery stores from April to June. Look for plump, firm, fresh-
looking stalks with compact tips, and avoid those that appear
brown or yellow, or are wrinkled, bruised, or slimy. The skin
should be smooth.

store »

Store bunches as you would spring bouquets. Trim the stem ends,

remove the band holding the bunch together, and stand the spears
in a tall cup or jar with enough water to keep the ends moist. They

will keep in the refrigerator up to 7 days, but serve as soon as
possible for the best flavor.

prep »
White asparagus has a tough, bitter skin that needs to be peeled.
Use a vegetable peeler to strip away the outermost layer, then snap
off the woody stem by bending the lower end of the stalk. It will
break naturally and you can discard the tough end. You can use
them interchangeably with recipes that call for green asparagus,
but because they have a mild taste, opt for simple preparations
that allow their subtle earthiness and mellow flavor to shine.

Short Order

Shortcakes are more biscuit than
cake, and bear little resemblance

to the packaged sponge cakes
masquerading as shortcakes in the
supermarket aisle. True shortcakes
are tender and flaky, and—much like
biscuits—the key to baking them is
in the details.

First is how you handle the
fat and flour. Freezing the butter
and shortening before handling it
ensures a crumbly cake. (If the butter
comes to room temperature, your
dough will be flat and textureless.)
And when you rub the butter and
shortening into the dry ingredients
with your fingers, you want to
maintain pea-size chunks of fat. As
the cakes bake, those chunks will
melt, creating little air pockets that
contribute to a light, fluffy texture.

The second important factor is
how you handle the dough. Put
simply: Less is more. Overmixing
or overkneading can lead to tough,
dry, bricklike shortcakes. Stir in the
cream mixture just to the point at
which you still see a few flecks of
flour, and knead just enough to bring
the dough together.

Last, to give your shortcakes a
dark golden finish, brush the tops
with both egg whites and cream
before popping them in the oven.
Most recipes call for one or the other,

TOSSING A FEW EDIBLE FLOWER PETALS into both sweet and savory dishes
adds a colorful flourish and makes something as simple as corn chowder a thing of
beauty while also adding a final note of complexity that takes it to the next level.

Nasturtiums, with their assertive, peppery bite, are one of the most widely available,
but clove-scented dianthus, spicy phlox, and lemony marigolds are also popular
choices. And don’t forget the flowers from herbs like oregano, cilantro, or chives, which
all hint at their respective plants. Some edible flowers are an acquired taste, so take a
little nibble before adding them.

You can grow your own, or find a wide variety seasonally at your local farmers
market or gourmet grocery store. Store them in the refrigerator in a lidded container
lined with a damp paper towel or in their original clamshell packaging, but try to use
them as soon as possible, as fresh cut flowers will mold and wilt quickly. Just make
sure the flowers are edible—not all are meant for consumption, and never eat flowers

treated with pesticides or fungicides, whether grown commercially or at home.

EGG SMARTS

Although these recipes were developed with
specific eggs in mind, chicken, duck, or quail
eggs will work for all with simple adjustments.
Quantities for both chicken and duck eggs are
interchangeable. If substituting quail eggs,
three or four will replace one chicken or duck
egg. Cooking times will be longer for duck
eggs and shorter for quail.

CHICKEN EGGS

Egg cartons include a dizzying array of
information about how the laying hens are
raised and fed, so it’s easy to get confused.
The USDA grades eggs on quality. The highest
is AA, followed by A and B. AA eggs are the
freshest and have firm whites and high, round
yolks. Beyond that, the best-tasting eggs come
from responsibly raised hens. Choose eggs
from “pasture-raised” or “certified humane”
hens for the best flavor. Unlike standard eggs
from battery-raised caged or most “free-
range” hens that live in cramped barns witha
tiny door to a small outdoor space, pastured
birds enjoy ample access to the outdoors.The
shells’ color varies by the breed of chicken and
has no real bearing on quality or taste.

DUCK EGGS

Sizes vary, but duck eggs are larger, sometimes
as muchas 50% larger, than a large chicken
egg. They have more robust shells, higher fat
yolks, and whites higher in protein, so they can
become rubbery if overcooked. Duck eggs

are delicious poached, fried, scrambled, and
hard-boiled if carefully done. The firm whites
help baked goods like cakes rise higher, while
the higher-fat yolks make gorgeous custards
and ice creams. They also make soufflés more
stable. Sold under the same USDA regulations
as chicken eggs, you needn’t worry about
their safety.

QUAIL EGGS

Soldin clear plastic boxes in some grocery
stores and many Asian markets, these tiny
brown-and-cream speckled eggs taste a lot
like chicken eggs, but the ratio of yolk to white
is higher, so they have a creamier mouthfeel.
Their diminutive size makes them a popular
finger food—try them hard-boiled and dipped

in fancy salt or sesame seeds on a grazing

board. They’re also served raw on top of sushi
and steak tartare. The inner membrane is
more substantial than chicken eggs, so be
prepared to carefully pull off the shell after
piercing (see Test Kitchen for details on how to
open raw quail egg shells successfully).

ALTHOUGH MY WIFE grew up in Berkeley, we both agreed
to raise our family in Oakland, California, because of its ethnic
diversity. We bought a house in the Laurel District, one of the
most culturally diverse Bay Area neighborhoods. We quickly
discovered that that community not only has a diversity of
human beings, but it also has a diversity of wild food sources.
Laurel was an agricultural area of orchard farms and ranches
in the early 20th century because of its sunny climate and rural
landscapes. So it was no surprise to find a bounty of fruits,
common weeds, and leaves whenever we walked around. Like
many things, however, after living here for 12 years, I took it
for granted that we could make a whole meal around foraged
food from our neighborhood.

A few days after I was asked to create this menu, I saw the
abundance of edible plants with fresh eyes while taking a post-
dinner walk with my family. Blackberries, dandelion, pineapple
weed (wild chamomile), fennel, prickly pears, lemons, loquats,
and figs surrounded me. That walk inspired me to craft this
meal using foraged ingredients. If I had to describe this menu,
Id say it’s Northern California with an eye on the American
South and a nod to Cantonese cooking. I knew that grilled
spring onions with grits would be a nice anchor around which
I could build a plant-based meal, especially since wild spring
onions grow in abundance in the Bay Area.

As I thought about pairing other dishes, I imagined the grits
being a blank canvas to add different sides to—much like jook,
a Chinese rice porridge served with several accompaniments.
The Roasted Wild Mushrooms, Sautéed Miner’s Lettuce, and
Ramp-Parsley Pesto can stand on its own or top the grits if
you like. The Sparkling Lemon Balm-Infused Chamomile Tea
came together pretty quickly using wild chamomile and lemon
balm we harvested from a small park nearby. We replanted
both in our garden and used them to make a simple syrup
for this refreshing drink. My culinary director, Amanda Yee,
collaborated with me to create Southern Pine Needle Biscuits
with Orange Marmalade, a pillowy biscuit using blissfully
aromatic pine needles (the biscuits are great for scraping up
the remnants of grits at the bottom of the bowl). I've worked
with prickly pears in the past and knew they would make a

TIP

Bean counter

Dried beans benefit from some sort of
preliminary rehydration to soften them
before cooking. The most common way

is to soak in room-temperature water for
several hours or overnight. The water will
first enter the bean through the hilum
(where the bean was attached to the pod
stem), and only after some of the water
has been absorbed by the seed will it start
soaking through the seed coat. The warmer
the water, the faster the bean absorbs it, so
alternatively, you can use the quick-soak
method: Rinse the beans several times,
discarding any “floaters.” Bring the water
to a boil, reduce the heat, and keep at a low
simmer for 2 to 10 minutes. Turn off the
heat, cover, and let stand 1 hour. For either
method, you should have 3 to 4 cups of
water per cup of beans. Count on getting

2 to 2% cups of cooked beans for every cup
of dried beans.

TIP

First coat

For a flawlessly crumb-free frosted cake, the
secret lies in applying a “crumb coat.” If you use
a little bit of frosting to seal the cake’s surface
and secure loose crumbs, your final layer of
frosting will go on much more smoothly. Brush
off any loose crumbs with a dry pastry brush,
spread a thin layer of your frosting over the
entire surface, and fill in any cracks or divots.
Refrigerate the cake for 5 to 10 minutes to let
the frosting set, then spread on the remaining
frosting in a smooth, crumb-free coat.

TECHNIQUE

All cracked up

Quail egg shells don’t crack as easily as chicken or duck because the
shells are leathery, and the yolks might break as a result of the tap-and-
crack method. Instead, cut it open using a paring knife. Push the tip into
the long side of the shell about one-third of the way down from the top.
Saw gently, then pull off the top of the shell. Alternatively, you can snip
off the top using quail egg scissors, which are available from a number
of sources online.

Shaped by the past

The dense European-style cakes baked

in a Bundt might feel old world, but the
signature fluted tube pan that gives them
their shape is actually quite modern. Created
in 1950 by David Dalquist, co-founder of
the Minneapolis-based cookware company
Nordic Ware, the distinct aluminum
pan was first cast for a local Hadassah
Society (an American women’s Jewish
organization) that wanted to recreate a
traditional Austrian ring-shaped cake called
akugelhopf. The pan garnered a fair amount
of interest after that, but it wasn’t until 1966
that it went mainstream thanks to a gooey
“Tunnel of Fudge” cake that won second
place in the 17th annual Pillsbury Bake-Off.
