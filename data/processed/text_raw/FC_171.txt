EDITOR’S LETTER

Saving Summer

Our tomato gardens are overflowing with sun-ripened heirlooms, we're up
to our ears in fresh sweet corn, and the peaches have never been juicier.
Bumper crop season has arrived—and we're here for it.

Are you hitting up the farmers market on the regular or constantly
searching for ideas about what to do with your weekly CSA? A fresh Swiss
chard salad, blistered green beans, and grilled zucchini steaks (“Peak Season,’
p. 34) highlight the best of summer cooking, reminding us that simple
preparations and ingredients that amplify a just-picked vegetable’s natural
flavor are often the best way to go.

But sometimes not cooking at ail is the way to go! The most blistering
days call for whirling up a chilled soup to keep you (and your kitchen) cool.
And once you try our riff on gazpacho, which gets a touch of sweetness
and depth from watermelon (p. 48), you might never want it any other way.

We know that come January you'll be craving a serious dose of warmth
and sunshine, so why not put your freezer to work now so you can enjoy a
taste of summer on those cold, dreary days? Sure, you can freeze your excess
of raw corn and berries (and were sure to do that). But as Ali Rosen shares
in “Freeze the Day” (p.50), you could also make and freeze corn fritters and
blueberry hand pies to fry or bake up whenever you crave a summery treat.

And speaking of berries—and peaches, and cherries, and plums—how
about turning all those fleeting summer fruits into a cobbler? We've got
four jammy renditions (“Hot and Bubbly,’ p. 82) just begging to be gobbled
up with a melty scoop of good vanilla ice cream.

Green beans, zucchini, tomatoes, corn, berries, stone fruits—it’s a great
time to enjoy great fruits and vegetables. And we're here to help you make
the most of the season’s produce smorgasbord.

FRESH FIGS HAVE A SWEET,
honeyed aroma and soft, jammy flesh
studded with small, crunchy seeds.
They grow on Ficus carica trees in
Mediterranean climates, and in fact
the trees are among the earliest known
domesticated plants dating back
11,000 years. There are hundreds of
fig varieties—Black Mission, Brown
Turkey, Calimyrna, and Kadota are
a few of the most popular in the U.S.
Their natural syrupy taste makes
them delicious on their own, but they
are also a fantastic partner for salty
cured meats and rich sharp cheeses
and therefore a welcome addition
to any cheese and charcuterie plate.
Tangy ingredients like yogurt, creme
fraiche, and mascarpone make nice
accompaniments, and vinegar, citrus,
and honey also play well with the
fruit. And don’t forget about baking:
Asimple tart or galette is a lovely way
to showcase these beauties.

SHOP

Look for figs that are heavy for their
size with smooth, unbroken skin. The
fruit should be soft and yield to gentle
pressure—a little wrinkle and squish
is a good thing. Avoid very firm figs,
which will not ripen further, and steer
clear of those that show any signs of
creamy liquid at the top or mold spots.

STORE

Fresh figs have a very short shelf life, so
you ll want to use them within a day or
two. Keep at room temperature with
a bit of space around the fruits for air
to circulate, or store in the refrigerator
to keep them fresh for an extra day
or two longer. But let them come to
room temperature before eating, as
they taste better that way.

PREP

There's no need to peel figs before
using—just give them a rinse under
cool water, discard the tough stem,
and enjoy.

BURNT TOAST AND OTHER DISASTERS
by Cal Peternell, William Morrow Cookbooks; $29.99

For anyone who spends significant time in the kitchen, the
occasional mishap—regardless of culinary experience—comes
with the territory. Perhaps that element of risk is even part of
the fun of cooking itself.

In his latest cookbook, Cal Peternell, whose best-selling
Twelve Recipes has been deemed required reading for both
beginners and seasoned cooks alike, suggests that fine cooking
can be achieved even in the least of fine circumstances. Sure,
he offers some more rudimentary suggestions for how to turn
burnt toast or mushy rice into something delicious (think
onion panade or savory spiced rice pancakes), but it’s not just
a book about rescuing seemingly ruined dishes.

Rather, his personal anecdotes and casual preparation
instructions (stir in a big handful of this, a couple of these
if you have them, etc.) give home cooks permission to tap
into their own culinary ingenuity, especially when the
conditions seem far from perfect. Even the most seasoned
cooks sometimes arrive home to find nothing to eat but a
can of chickpeas, a couple of eggs, and a few herbs at the back
of the fridge. And yet Peternell reminds us that with enough
confidence and finesse, these scrappy Quickfire Challenge-like
moments in the kitchen can actually be the ones that bring
out our most creative inner chefs.

IN LATE AUGUST INTO SEPTEMBER, there's a good
chance you might find yourself with more tomatoes, zucchini,

you are plucking vegetables from your backyard garden,

ollo al mattone means chicken under a brick. Literally. Imagine
what a brick, heated in the oven (or the embers of a fire) can do
to sizzle the golden skin of a chicken. It’s an old Tuscan idea, and
it’s a good one. You may have seen it on menus or in books—the
dish usually features a half chicken or a spatchcocked whole
chicken. Either a preheated brick or a cast iron pan with a
heavy weight placed inside it does the trick. Part of the magic

of cooking this way is that the intense heat and pressure of the

weight increase the cooking speed of dark meat, which remains
juicy under the crisp skin.

In the kitchen of California's Chez Panisse Café, I learned to do this with the whole
chicken leg. We deboned the legs as part of our prep shift, and then cooked them al
mattone on the line. At first it seemed like a challenge, but it’s really a simple technique.
We had a stack of cast iron skillets on the back of the stove, and wed use them or a foil-
wrapped brick, which we kept in the hot oven, to press the chicken when the orders
came in.

Well, I took to this habit. Years later, I was a house sitter for my aunt and uncle in upstate
New York. Once, after they returned home, I got a phone call: “Hi. Um, we're just curious:
What is the significance of the brick at the back of the stove?” I had made grilled cheese
sandwiches and forgotten to move the brick. These days at my house, a foil-wrapped
brick is a permanent fixture. We use it often, for grilled cheese and other panini, and of
course chicken—the foil is replaced frequently after heavy use.

Lately, I’ve been playing around with vegetables cooked this way. I thought of the
crisp, flattened and fried artichokes (carciofi all giudia) served in Roman trattorias, and
Francis Mallmann’s smashed potatoes a la plancha. My simple versions took well to the
brick treatment and came out crisp and brown on the outside, tender in the middle. The
artichokes are trimmed and then poached in a simple court bouillon, and can be left
to cool in the liquid for up to 3 days. The potatoes can be boiled ahead anytime, or just

PEACH-THYME COBBLER

Classic peach cobbler gets a fragrant
upgrade from fresh thyme in both the
filling and topping. Individual servings
ensure no one fights over the last
scoop. SERVES 6

FOR THE BISCUITS

1 cups all-purpose flour

3 Tbs. chopped fresh thyme; plus
more sprigs for garnish

1 Tbs. sugar
1+ tsp. baking powder
1% tsp. kosher salt
1/3 cup butter, cut into pieces
4% cup milk
FOR THE FILLING
4 cup sugar
1 Tbs. cornstarch

6 fresh peaches, peeled, pitted, and
sliced, or two 16-o0z. pkg. frozen
peach slices (6 cups)

MAKE THE BISCUITS

Preheat the oven to 400°F. Ina
medium bowl, stir together the flour,
thyme, the 1 Tbs. sugar, the baking
powder, and salt. Using a pastry
blender, cut in the butter until the
mixture resembles coarse crumbs.
Make a well in the center of the flour
mixture. Add the milk all at once.
Using a fork, stir just until the mixture
is moistened.

Turn the dough out onto a lightly
floured surface. Knead by folding and
gently pressing it just until dough
holds together. Pat or lightly roll the
dough into a ¥%4-inch-thick circle.

Cut into rounds using a 2-inch
round cutter, then cut each round
into quarters.

MAKE THE FILLING
In a large saucepan, combine the
Ye cup sugar, 1/4 cup water, and the
cornstarch. Add the peaches. Cook
and stir over medium heat until
slightly thickened and bubbly.
Transfer the hot filling to
6 individual baking dishes or a 2-qt.
baking dish. Arrange the dough
triangles on top of filling.
Bake until the biscuit topping is
golden brown, 20 to 25 minutes.
Garnish with the fresh thyme sprigs.

This decidedly grown-up cobbler
takes the dessert to happy hour. A still
or sparkling rosé gives the filling a

In amedium bowl, combine the

MAKE THE FILLING

In an extra-large skillet, combine the
wine, honey, and 2 Tbs. flour. Stir in

Kewpie
Mayonnaise

So what's the deal with Kewpie and what
makes it different than regular mayo? The
Japanese mayonnaise, which was first
manufactured in 1925, is different from
American mayo in that it’s made only
with egg yolks (rather than whole eggs),
rice or apple cider vinegar (rather than
distilled white vinegar), and contains no
added sugar. Its umami-rich flavor and
creamy, luscious, smooth texture have
earned it a cult-like following, especially
among chefs. You can spot it in its red-
capped clear tube bottle adorned with red
type and emblematic baby logo, and it's
available at Asian markets and most large
grocery stores.

Cleaning
Mushrooms

Mushrooms grow in compost
or in not-so-appetizing rotting
logs or leaves, so before you
cook with them, they usually
require a bit of cleaning. But
the fungi are like sponges. They
absorb water, and when they’re
water-logged, they don't brown
well. There are different
schools of thought on how to
clean mushrooms. One method
is to use a paper towel, kitchen
towel, or soft brush to wipe
away any dirt. But if your
mushrooms are particularly
dirty, it may be best to very
gently wash them. Give them a
brief rinse, one at a time, under
running water, then blot away
any surface moisture with a dry
towel. This should remove any
grime without completely
soaking them.

Perfecting the Egg Sandwich

Turn your Pokegg!—the Japanese egg sando-
meets-poke (p. 71)—into an eye-catching creation.

The hallmark of the Japanese egg sandwich (popularized in the U.S. by
Los Angeles restaurant Konbi) is three perfectly cut eggs in three
perfectly cut sandwich portions. The key starts with lining up your
eggs. Place them horizontally in the center of your bread slice with the
narrow tips pointed toward the crusts and the wider portions pointed
toward one another in the middle.

Spoon the tuna mixture around and on top of the eggs, and place the
other slice of bread on top. Then, using a serrated knife, cut the
sandwich vertically into thirds. A bit of your tuna mixture might
smoosh out, and that’s okay—just gently spoon it back in before tipping

your sandwich pieces on their sides to reveal your masterpiece.

Smart

An immersion or hand
blender is a helpful tool

for making pureed soups,
smoothies, sauces, and
more. It's easy to maneuver
and takes up minimal storage
space. And although most
immersion blenders

have about half the
horsepower of the
average countertop
blender, the quality ones
are just as effective. Plus,
they can be used right in the

pot, glass, or measuring cup for easy
clean-up. It’s our top-choice gadget for
small jobs like making mayonnaise or
pesto and emulsifying vinaigrettes. And
it's great for making lump-free gravy or
pureeing fresh or canned tomatoes into
a sauce in seconds. Many models come
with whisk or chopping attachments,
which add to their versatility as well,
and more are now available in cordless
designs for greater portability.

INGREDIENT

DANDELION
GREENS

Dandelion greens can be found at farmers markets
and well-stocked supermarkets, but you may not
have to go to the store to find them. You can pick
the leaves off dandelions in areas that are lightly
trafficked by people and animals, and where you are
confident the plants have not come into contact with
weed or insect killers. If you're foraging, bear in mind
that smaller leaves from younger plants will be more
tender and less bitter. Whether you pick them or buy
them, look for firm stems and green leaves with no
yellow or brown spots, wilted areas, or holes. Trim
the tough stem ends up to where the leaves start,
and rinse thoroughly just before using.

TIP

Removing corn
kernels from
the cob

Removing corn kernels from the cob
can be messy—they like to bounce off
the cutting board and end up scattered
all over the counter and floor. To keep
those kernels in their place, insert the
tip of the ear of corn into the center hole
of a Bundt pan. Cut the kernels away
from the cob in long downward strokes,
letting them fall into the pan.
