EDITOR’S LETTER

Comfort Zone

The cool signs of fall encourage us to seek out warm comforts—chunky knit
sweaters, oversize throws, wooly socks—simple pleasures that help ease the
transition from summer to winter. We crave the same in the kitchen—hearty
soups and stews, hot and bubbly casseroles, spice-laden cakes—familiar
favorites that invite us back into our homes after a season spent outdoors.
This issue is all about the simple cozy dishes we know and love. But we're
not afraid to upgrade our favorites.

Were thinking beyond potatoes with a collection of hot, cheesy, and bubbly
gratins (p. 38), fine-tuning brisket so it comes out meltingly tender (p. 10),
and incorporating the last hurrah of harvest fruits into an Oktoberfest menu
starring Roasted Chicken with Grapes (p. 25). Our baked treats are also
getting an aromatic breath of fresh air thanks to a collection of desserts
from Vallery Lomas featuring cardamom (p. 44).

And of course there is the pinnacle of comfort-food holidays (or let’s face
it, any food-centric holidays): Thanksgiving. If you're like us, you've got a few
familiar favorites that you always fall back on making (or would be banished
from the table for not making). But that certainly leaves plenty of room for
anew dish or two.

If youd like to break out of your usual turkey routine, we invite you to try
Ariane Daguin’s French-style poached and roasted technique (p. 66), which
is guaranteed to deliver a juicy—never dry—centerpiece bird. There's always
room for a new side or two, and Carrie Morey’s biscuit-centric Southern
dishes are sure to please (p. 70). And what's Thanksgiving without apple and
pumpkin pies? Roxana Jullapat elevates the classics, incorporating heirloom
whole wheat flours into the pie shells (p.78).

‘These comforting dishes might not be your go-to fall favorites just yet,
but we have no doubt they will be soon.

than their summer squash
counterparts, and a favorite ingredient
for warming cold-weather meals.
Some—like delicata and acorn—merely
need to be split, seeded, and roasted
with butter and spices. Others, such as
pumpkin and butternut, are perennial
favorites for whirling into velvety soups,
pies, and purées. Sweet spices such as
cinnamon and cloves enhance the
squash’s natural sweetness, as do toasty
earthy spices such as fennel, cumin,
chile, and curry powder.

SHOP

Choose winter squash that feel heavy
for their size, and avoid those with any
soft spots, bruises, or nicks.

STORE

One of the many advantages of
winter squash is their long shelf life.
Store in a cool, well-ventilated spot
(ideally at a temperature of between
50°F and 60°F). Hard-shell squash—
acorn, butternut, honeynut, hubbard,
kabocha, kuri, pumpkin, and spaghetti
squash—will keep up to 2 months;
thinner-skin delicata will last up to
2 weeks.

PREP

To peel or not to peel? That depends
both on the squash and how you plan
to use it, Technically, all squash skins
are edible, but not all eat pleasantly.
In general, honeynut, acorn, and
delicata are best left unpeeled.
When you roast, grill, or bake, their
skins get tender enough to easily
chew. For thicker-skin varieties like
butternut and kabocha, peeling is
usually preferred. But regardless of
the variety you're preparing, you'll
want to remove the seeds first. Use
a sharpe knife to carefully cut the
squash in half, then use a spoon to
scape away the pulpy fibers.

LIFE IS WHAT YOU BAKE IT
by Vallery Lomas, Clarkson Potter; $29.99

As Vallery Lomas puts it, sometimes you have to turn life’s
lemons into lemon curd. And it’s the perfect metaphor coming
from someone whose career has been marked by adversity.

In 2017, Lomas won the third season of The Great American
Baking Show only for it to be abruptly canceled after the first
episode. But instead of drowning in a puddle of tears, she
made the most of a tough situation, and her debut cookbook,
Life Is What You Bake It, is a celebration of resilience and
fortitude as much as it is about breads, cakes, and doughnuts
(though there are plenty of those, too).

“At its core, this book is about how I overcame extreme
and potentially career-defining disappointments to live out
my dreams,’ she writes.

Stories and recipes from her time on The Great American
Baking Show are sprinkled throughout—a welcome treat for
fans who never got to witness Lomas’ magic on TV—including
the Lemon Surprise Tart that earned an enthusiastic, “Wow!”
from Paul Hollywood in the finale.

But just as welcome are the recipes and stories that pay
tribute to family and generations of Black women who came
before her, and the values they instilled.

“These women exhibited absolute, unshakable resilience,
and it seemed I had acquired that, too.”

How to achieve a Jewish-style
brisket that’s moist and tender—
never dry and tough—for
Hanukkah, or any occasion.

--- Page 13 ---
BRISKET MIGHT BE A TOUGH
cut of meat, but when cooked low
and slow, especially with moist
heat, it’s not tough to transform
it into a meltingly tender main
dish for Hanukkah dinner or any
holiday celebration.

There are a few steps that
make the difference between a
juicy, flavorful brisket and one
that’s tough and dry. First, choose
a cut that has nice marbling
(intramuscular fat) and hasn't been
trimmed too much (ideally youd
have about 4 inch of external fat).
Fat equals flavor, and it helps to
baste the meat as it cooks. Leave a
bit, and you can easily trim before
serving. Second, it’s important to
add the smallest amount of liquid
possible. Add too much, and a
braise, which concentrates flavor
in both the meat and the sauce,
becomes a boil. Last, braising a
brisket is a marathon, not a sprint.
Low and slow is the name of the
game. Luckily, most of that time
commitment is hands-off.

This recipe calls for a simple yet
classic combination of flavors—
lots of onion, a bit of tomato, and
white wine (though a dry red
would be equally appropriate). An
accompaniment of ginger-pickled
red onions is a decidedly modern
addition, and they offer a pleasant
zip to complement the richness of
the beef and sauce.

Because of the simplicity of the
original preparation, leftovers—of
which you'll have plenty—become
easy to turn into all sorts of dishes
on day 2 (or 3 or 4). Chop it into
chunks, and add some canned
tomatoes, beans, and spices, and
you have a rich chili. Toss bite-size
pieces in a hot skillet with potatoes,
bell peppers, and jalapenos and top
with a few fried eggs, and you've got
a craveworthy hash for brunch. You
could even combine it with some
roasted root vegetables like beets
and carrots on a bed of arugula for
a hearty fall salad.

MAKE THE BRISKET
Preheat the oven to 325°F. Season the
beef with salt and pepper. Heat the oil
and schmaltz in a 12-inch skillet over
medium-high heat. Add the beef, and
brown well on both sides. Transfer to
a roasting pan, and set aside. Add the
onions and garlic to the skillet. Cook

and stir until the onions are
translucent, about 5 minutes. Stir in
the celery, bay leaves, tomatoes,
stock, wine, and tomato paste. Bring
to a boil, and boil gently, uncovered,
2 to 3 minutes. Pour the mixture
around the meat in dish. Cover with
parchment paper, then tightly with a
double thickness of foil. Roast until
meat is tender, 3 to 34/2 hours or
more. Remove the meat from baking
dish, and transfer to a cutting board.
Cover and let stand for 15 minutes.

MAKE THE PICKLED ONIONS

In a saucepan, combine the vinegar,
brown sugar, salt, and ginger. Bring to
a rapid simmer, stirring occasionally
until sugar is dissolved. Simmer,
uncovered, until syrupy, 5 to 7 minutes.
Add the onions, and simmer 1 minute
more. Remove from the heat, and let
cool completely.

MAKE THE SAUCE

Skim the fat from the cooking liquid,
and discard the bay leaves. Bake,
uncovered, until the sauce is thickened
slightly, 10 to 15 minutes more. Slice
the meat across the grain. Transfer to a
platter, and pour over the sauce. Top
with parsley and horseradish, and
serve with the pickled onions.

PAIR WITH MERLOT Almost any
relatively big red works with this

WHEN THAT FIRST COLD SNAP HITS, it’s a signal to change out strappy sandals for chunky
boots, fuchsia impatiens for yellow and maroon mums, and iced coffees for steaming mugs
of chai. And in that same fell swoop, we tend to hit pause on light, crisp salads in favor of
warm, cozy soups and stews. But before you do away with all salads until spring, perhaps
it’s time to reimagine your definition of salad altogether.

That’s where grains come in. Using grains as a salad base is a fantastic way to showcase
all the wonderful fruits and vegetables the fall season has to offer while giving a bit of heft
to otherwise lighter fare. And now there are so many to choose from. It’s hard to believe that
just a few years ago, the only way to get your hands on quinoa or farro was to hit up the bulk
bins at the natural grocery store. Whereas now, you can snag a profusion of grain and rice
varieties (microwaveable pouches even!) at just about any large grocery chain.

The basic blueprint for an autumn grain salad is fairly straightforward, and as such, it
leaves plenty of room for flavor experimentation—not unlike walking up to a fantastically
well-stocked fast-casual salad bar spot. Start with your grain, keeping size and texture in
mind. There are smaller grains like quinoa, bulgur, and freekeh, and larger grains like barley,
wheat berries, farro, and wild rice, each with its own distinctive bite.

Next, think about choosing a few seasonal fruits and vegetables to add to the mix. Apples,
pears, and grapes are all at their peak this time of year, as are the first signs of winter citrus.
As for vegetables, sturdy root vegetables (think beets, carrots, parsnips), winter squash
(especially butternut and delicata), and cruciferous (Brussels sprouts, broccoli, cauliflower)
should all be taken under consideration.

Nuts, seeds, dried fruits, and cheese aren't required, but can offer some intrigue in terms
of texture and flavor. (If adding nuts or seeds, a quick toast on the stove top does much to
heighten their flavor and is worth the couple of minutes.) You might also toss in a tender
green of some sort for added freshness. Baby kale, arugula, watercress, baby spinach, etc.
would all be a nice counterpart to the heartier grain and vegetable components.

Last, of course, is the dressing. Vinaigrettes—as opposed to creamier dressings—are the
clear winners for this type of salad. Keep it simple and find a pleasing balance of oil and acid
to brighten everything.

These salads are gold for anyone who thrives on meal prep or make ahead, so long as you
keep a few things in mind. You can easily cook your grains, roast or sauté any vegetables,
and whip up your dressing in advance. You can even toss that all together and pop it in the
fridge—but reserve a bit of dressing for serving time. The grains will want to soak up much of
the vinaigrette, so it’s best to have a bit extra on hand when youre ready to eat. Also, save any
nuts, fresh fruits, and greens until the last minute as the nuts will quickly lose their crunch,

the fruit will oxidize, and the greens will wilt if incorporated too far ahead.

crispy baked-and-broiled treatment. There are even
chicken and seafood gratins and pasta and rice
variations, too. It’s the kind of dish that will always
please a crowd. If you wonder, will it au gratin? The

answer is likely yes.

| LOVE READING ABOUT HOW different cultures employ spices in
their respective cuisines. There's such a variety of applications for
each and every spice. For example, you might think of cinnamon
as a spice reserved for sweet baked goods, but it's also commonly
used in North African tagines and slow-cooked savory stews.
Regardless of how people use their spices, there's one universal
truth: Spices make basic dishes extraordinary. As a professional
baker, I'm always developing new recipes and trying to find ways

to update the classics (mostly desserts, in my case). Spices play

a critical role in my culinary process, and one of my all-time
favorites—and most-used—is cardamom.

Native to India, cardamom is available internationally and
embraced by a wide array of cuisines. Harvesting cardamom pods
is extremely labor intensive, so they can be quite expensive. But
luckily, it's a strong spice, which means you only need a little
to make a big impact on your cooking and baking. If possible, |
recommend buying small amounts of cardamom at a time to make
sure you go through it before it loses its potency. Most of us keep
our store-bought spices far too long (me included!), and it makes a
real difference to have the fresh stuff around. | prefer to buy whole
green cardamom pods and grind them myself (you'll see how | do it
in my Pear Cardamom Coffee Cake, p. 52). Green cardamom (which

food world. A few years ago, that undisputed darling was kale (you know when
people start sporting t-shirts and tote bags enthusiastically proclaiming “Kale

Yeah” that a trend has peaked.) And although the fervent fandom may have
cooled off a bit, continued interest in the leafy green has proven the vegetable

nutritious, it’s also super versatile. A quick sauté with a splash of vinegar is a

of the cabbage family—curly kale (arguably the most common grocery-store
variety), Tuscan (also called dinosaur or lacinato), Russian Red (a variety with

or planters, but the pretty green, white, purple, and pink rosettes are edible
nonetheless). You can use the varieties interchangeably in these recipes—

you prefer. When shopping, look for bunches with small- to medium-size

them a good rinse to remove any grit before using, and stem them (simply
hold on to the larger stem end and pull off the leaves in a downward motion)

before chopping or tearing the leaves as desired.

CARVING 101

What good is producing the perfect bird if you're then
going to mangle the carving? That won't happen if you
follow these steps. But before you pull out your carving

knife, remember to let the bird rest. This allows time for the
juices to circulate, yielding moist, succulent meat.

1. REMOVE THE LEGS

Place the turkey breast-side-up on
a cutting board set over a rimmed
sheet pan (this will catch any
overrunning juices). Using a meat
fork to steady the bird, cut through
the skin that holds the leg to the
body and pull the leg out away
from the turkey. With the tip of your
knife, feel inside for the leg joint. If
you locate it correctly, there won't
be much resistance and it will be
easy to cut right through.

3. CUT OFF THE WINGS
Similarly to how you separated the
legs, find the joint and cut through
it. Since there is a lot of connective
tissue and not much meat on the
wings, it's not really worth trying to
do any additional carving. Simply
remove the wings at the joints, and
move on to the body of the bird.

2. SEPARATE THE THIGHS
FROM THE DRUMSTICKS

The easiest way to separate the
upper thigh from the drumstick is

to bend it backward, then insert
your knife in the joint until it breaks
(again, if you hit the joint right, there
shouldn't be too much resistance).
Set the drumsticks aside (they're best
left intact). For the thighs, slice along
the top, parallel to the bone, to
remove as much meat as you can

4. CARVE THE BREAST

Using your meat fork to steady the
breast, cut down along the breast
bone until you hit the rib cage.
Continue cutting, keeping the blade
of the knife as close to the bone as
possible, to remove the breast. Now
it’s time to slice. It's always best to
slice the dark meat before the white
meat because the latter dries out
faster. Simply place on the cutting
board and cut crosswise in long,
thin slices

Cooking and freezing big batches of whole grains is a great way to get a leg
up on weeknight meals. When youre pressed for time (or meal inspiration),

DIY Pumpkin Pie Purée

If you'd like to make your own pumpkin purée for pies,
soups, and other fall recipes, it’s best to use a small pie
pumpkin (also called a sugar pumpkin), which has a
sweet, dense flesh. Here's how to do it:

1. Using a large sturdy knife, halve a pie pumpkin
lengthwise. Use a spoon to scrape out the seeds and
stringy pulp. (Discard the pulp, but save the seeds for
roasting!) Cut each half in half. Arrange the pieces, rind
sides up, on a foil-lined baking pan. Bake at 375°F for

1 to 142 hours until tender, and let cool.

2. Once cool, use a spoon to scrape the pulp from
the rind, and discard the rind. Blend or process the
pulp until smooth.

3. Spoon the pulp into a fine-mesh sieve, and let drain
1 hour. Press the pulp gently to squeeze out any excess
liquid, and use as directed in place of canned pumpkin.

Nutmeg

There's a significant difference between
preground and freshly ground spices, and
this is particularly true for nutmeg.
Freshly grated smells and tastes sweet,
fragrant, and almost citrusy. By
comparison, preground can come across a
bit sharp and musky. Upon grating, whole
nutmeg releases its volatile oils, which
give the spice its aroma and flavor. But the
longer the ground spice sits, the more
those oils evaporate, which results in a
loss of flavor. Don’t get us wrong, there's a
place for both—preground is certainly
convenient—but it’s helpful to keep this in
mind, especially if a recipe calls for

freshly grated.
