EDITOR'S LETTER

A Tale of Two Seasons

‘This issue offers inspiration for two strikingly different seasons: one for glitz,
glam, and celebrations; the other for rest, hibernation, and comfort.

In December, we're whipping up big-batch punches that take a little of the
stress out of hosting yet will make your cocktail party the talk of the town
(“Cheers!, p, 28). Sweet and savory make-ahead breakfast casseroles (“Best.
Brunch. Ever.’ p. 30) are at the top of our Christmas morning wish list. A
dry-aged club roast, which is so inherently rich and flavorful, requires little
from the home cook to impress as the centerpiece of the holiday feast ("Roast
with the Most? p. 68). And an elegant, subtly sparkly collection of cookies,
including decadent Gilded Butter Tarts and Black Forest Marshmallow
Kisses (“Sweet & Sophisticated? p. 80), make for a dazzling box of sweets to
share with friends and family.

After the hullabaloo of the holidays has faded, come January we're leaning
into cozy comforts, That starts with a traditional Southern New Year's Day
menu of greens, black-eyed peas, and cornbread thought to bring wealth and
prosperity (“Dishing Up Good Luck; p. 24). Pati Jinich shares four flavor-
packed Mexican soups that are sure to become new go-tos for chilly winter
nights (“Sopa!? p, 58). And Mikkel Karstad will have you thinking differently
about the humble root vegetable with elevated dishes inspired by his native
Copenhagen (“From the Ground Upy p. 44).

Cheers to a happy, joy-filled holiday, and a restful and quiet start to the
New Year.

BLOOD ORANGES ARE A UNIQUE
gem in the citrus family—and it’s not
just their ruby-hue interior that makes
them so special, They are also less
acidic than more common varieties,
with overtones of raspberries and
strawberries, which makes them
sweeter than navels and Valencias.
Blood oranges are also unique in their
nutritional makeup, Anthocyanin, the
pigment that gives the fruit its crimson
color, boasts an anti-inflammatory
effect, which could help lower the
risk of heart disease and cancer. There
are two types you'll find most often:
Moros and Taroccos. Moros have a
dark-purple flesh and deep-reddish
rind; Taroccos (sometimes called “half-
blood oranges”) have a flesh that is less
so, and their rind is only tinged with
red. Use them while you can get them
during the winter months—they make
a beautiful addition to everything from
salads to cocktails to baked goods.

SHOP

Blood oranges can be more difficult to
find than other varieties but tend to
be available from December through
April. They're grown primarily in the
Mediterranean and California—they
thrive in temperate climates that have
both a hot season and cooler weather,
which brings out their brilliant color.
As with all citrus, look for fruit that
feels heavy for its size. If you can't find
blood oranges, regular navel or Valencia
oranges can be substituted.

STORE

Keep in the refrigerator, but let the fruit
warm up a bit at room temperature for
the most robust flavor. The juice can
be stored in a sealed container in the
refrigerator for up to 3 days,

PREP

Treat as you would any other oranges,
but keep in mind that their skins can
be a little harder to peel and cut into
segments. (For how to segment, see
Test Kitchen, p. 94.)

ART BOOZEL
by Jennifer Croll, Chronicle Books; $18.95

Appreciating a well-crafted cocktail is much like appreciating
a great piece of art. You notice shapes, colors, textures. You

might wonder about the mixologist’s backstory or source of

inspiration. It’s likely to evoke an emotion or trigger a memory.
So it's no surprise then that cocktail might imitate art from
time to time—and that’s just what Art Boozel does.

Admittedly, this book doesn't take itself too seriously. It's
filled with quirky illustrated portraits and is as much a whos
who of modern art (peppered with plenty of “who knew”
tidbits) as it is a cocktail book, but that’s all part of the fun.
It's the kind of book you whip out at a dinner party, letting
guests try on whatever artist's persona speaks to them via
coupe or highball,

Perhaps youd like to channel Japanese pop artist Yayoi
Kusama’s bright pumpkin sculptures with a gin-based drink
that includes pumpkin puree and fresh lime juice to balance
the sweetness. Or maybe it's a bit of mystery and intrigue
you crave. If so, you'll love The Banksy, a Campari egg white
cocktail featuring a bitter garnish that—you guessed it—
disappears as you drink it.

You likely won't soon find one of the concoctions in this
book on display at the Whitney or Guggenheim, but they sure

are a playful and creative way to raise a glass to modern art.

THERE'S SOMETHING MAGICAL
about the smell of cinnamon
rolls baking in the oven, There's
something even more magical
about biting into one. And this
recipe has a secret to making them
extra magical: mashed potato.
The addition is what gives these
light, fluffy rolls their perfectly
pillowy texture.

Adding mashed potato is a
trick used often in yeasted roll
recipes. Potatoes, which are rich
in potassium, cause the yeast
to rise more quickly than flour
alone, and the starch in the potato
retains water, which helps the rolls
maintain moisture and keep from
drying out. You'll heat the potato—
1 medium to large russet potato will
yield about a cup mashed—along
with milk (for extra richness),
butter, sugar, and salt before
incorporating flour and eggs for a
soft, elastic dough. Too much flour
creates heavy, dense rolls, so start
with the smallest amount listed,
then gradually work in more as
you knead. (You might not need
any extra flour at all.)

For the filling of this modernized
version, a fierce combination of
crystallized ginger and freshly
cracked black pepper accompany
freshly ground cinnamon. For the
most cinnamon-forward flavor,
try using Saigon cinnamon, which
tends to possess the fullest flavor.
And be sure your butter is soft
enough to spread easily—but not
to the point of melting—which
will more than likely leak out into
a puddle at the bottom of your pan.

If youd like, you can also make
these rolls the night before you
plan to bake them. Instead of
letting the unbaked rolls rise for
30 minutes, cover them loosely
with oiled plastic wrap, then chill
for up to 24 hours. When you're
ready to bake, let them stand at
room temperature for 30 minutes,
and then bake as directed,

MAKE THE ROLLS
In a large bowl, combine 1/2 cups of
the flour and the yeast. Set aside. Ina
medium saucepan, heat and stir milk,
mashed potato, the +/s cup butter, the
granulated sugar, and salt just until
warm (120°F to 130°F) and butter
almost melts, and add to the flour
mixture along with the eggs. Beat with
an electric mixer on medium speed for
30 seconds, scraping sides of bowl
constantly. Beat on high speed for
3 minutes. Using a wooden spoon, stir
in as much of the remaining flour as
you can.

Turn out dough onto a lightly
floured surface, Knead in enough of

the remaining flour to make a
moderately soft dough that is smooth
and elastic, 3 to 5 minutes total. Shape
the dough into a ball, Place in a lightly
greased bowl. Turn once to grease
surface of the dough. Cover and let
rise in a warm place until double in
size, 45 to 60 minutes.

Punch down the dough and turn out
onto a lightly floured surface. Cover
and let rest 10 minutes. Meanwhile,
lightly grease a 13x9-inch baking dish.
Set aside. In a small bowl, stir together
the brown sugar, cinnamon, ginger,
and pepper. Set aside.

Roll the dough into an 18x12-inch
rectangle. Spread the 4/2 cup butter
over the dough rectangle. Sprinkle the
brown sugar mixture over the butter-
spread dough rectangle, leaving 1 inch
unfilled along one of the long sides.
Roll up the filled rectangle into a spiral,
starting from the long side with the
filling. Pinch the dough to seal seams.

Cut the dough spiral into 12 equal
pieces. Arrange pieces, cut-sides
down, in the prepared baking dish.
Cover with a clean kitchen towel, and

“PEAS FOR PENNIES, greens for dollars, and cornbread for gold,” so goes
an old Southern saying. For many—especially in the South—the day after
the ball drops calls for a meal that’s in stark contrast to the glitz and glam
of the previous evening's festivities.

As legend has it, a New Year's Day supper of black-eyed peas, greens, and
corn bread is thought to bring prosperity and wealth in the year to come.
But how that came to be is anyone's guess. “Just as nobody is sure of the
origin of the name Hoppin’ John, no one seems quite certain why the dish
has become associated with luck, or New Year's,” historian Jessica B, Harris
wrote in a 2010 New York Times op-ed, “Prosperity Starts with a Pea?

What we do know is that it’s a humble meal that offers a certain sense
of comfort and casualness that feels quite welcome after weeks of holiday
parties and feasting, It’s simple to prepare, and much can be done ahead of
time (making the corn bread, blanching the greens, cooking the peas)—an
exceptional treat, especially if hanging in your PJs and watching the Rose

Bowl is the extent of your plans for the day,

AS MUCH AS PEOPLE outside of Mexico
seem to be familiar with tacos—to the point
of having weekly Taco Nights at home and
the trendy #tacotuesday on social media—
not many are aware of the breadth and
depth of the Mexican soup category. Yet
the regional history and culinary splendor
of Mexico is beautifully and deliciously
expressed in soups that can enrich
everyone's weeknight repertoire just as
much as tacos.

Savor what an afternoon in the late
summer rainy months of central Mexico
tastes like with the deeply rich and woody

Experience the hilly mountains of Sinaloa

knows as a beloved nurturing and satisfying
everyday Smoky Chipotle Vegetable Soup.

Aside from taking a culinary tour of
Mexico by tasting its different soups, you
will also start to discover what Mexican
soups have in common. For starters, a base
of a flavorful broth—be it chicken, beef, or

depth. Then comes a seasoned base that

dozens of fresh or dried chiles (not all of
them spicy and now widely available in the

tangy taste, the quajillo offering a peppy
and crowd-pleasing flavor, or the chipotle
giving both smoke and spice.

Last, we love to dress and garnish all of
our soups. This makes them not only look
beautiful, it also makes them fun and
accommodating as everyone can garnish
to taste, allowing a festive, warm, and
friendly spirit to shine through. And to boot,
as with most Mexican food, Mexican soups
can be stored and reheated time and again

WHAT IS DRY AGING?

The process uniquely intensifies beef's natural flavor while imparting tenderness.

Dry aging is a carefully controlled process in which
beef primals—whole muscle groups—are aged in a
temperature-controlled environment for a specific
length of time before they are trimmed and cut into
steaks or roasts.

By exposing the meat to air on all sides, dry aging

allows the moisture within the meat to slowly
evaporate over time, while natural enzymes slowly
begin to break down the molecular bonds. It's this
combination of moisture loss and tenderization that
improves the beef's flavor and texture. The longer it is
dry-aged, the more pronounced and concentrated the
flavor. Thirty to 35 days of aging is the most popular
sweet spot, resulting in a slice that is deeply beefy,
delicately nutty, and incredibly aromatic.

When it comes to seasoning, less is more. Because of

the inherent character imparted by dry aging, the meat
needs very little intervention from marinades and rubs,
and you'll want to exercise restraint when it comes to
sauces. A heavy pinch of kosher salt and a generous
grind of black peppercorns before cooking is truly all a
dry-aged cut needs, For a holiday table, we like to keep it
simple with fresh herbs and natural jus.

Pinpricks of marbling and fat benefit from the acidity
of wine, but like seasonings, the wine shouldn't
overpower the nuances of the meat, so seek out a
lighter-bodied bottle with subtle fruit. Pinot Noir or
Italian reds like Barolo and other Nebbiolo varieties are
excellent companions, as are California Cabernet,
Bordeaux, and Spanish blends. One rule: The older the
vintage, the better, Aged wine with aged steak is as
sympatico as Champagne and caviar.

AS A CANADIAN OF INDIAN DESCENT, Thanksgiving has never been my symbolic start to
the holiday season—that title belongs to Diwali.

Falling between mid-October and mid-November, as based on the Hindu lunisolar calendar,
itis legendarily the darkest night of the year. And as such we annually banish those shadows in
a number of ways. We light diyas (clay oil lamps with cotton wicks dipped in ghee or vegetable
oils) and wear sequins that catch the flicker of their flames, sending it across the room in
rainbows. We polish any silver and brass to mirrorlike reflection. We swag innumerable
rows of white lights from the eaves and in the trees until the house is a sparkling beacon to
welcome Lakshmi and Ganesha.

But even after the colorful rangoli designs have long faded, those lights remain throughout
December. Those rows of white lights linger to illuminate the tree and stockings hung for
Christmas, and the flicker of diyas makes way for the flicker of Hanukkah candles—one
celebration twinkling into the next.

This year, with hope for the return to these glimmering traditions, | am compelled to
embrace the optimistic exuberance of bedecking each possible moment, in all ways. | want
my days properly festooned from the mantel to the windows to the table to the cookie boxes
packed and ready for friends and family near and far.

It is time to get out the sprinkles, the dragées, every last ounce of glimmer and extra
embellishment. This year's cookies are going to be appropriately dressed up for the party.

I'm not talking about anything too difficult, but rather the baking equivalent of putting on
red lipstick for the evening. That means attiring Mexican wedding cookies in a coat of white
chocolate and pristine, fluffy coconut for a playful snowball effect. Perhaps even a dusting of
silver edible glitter for extra sparkle. Or taking a simple spiced cutout cookie—think speculaas
with nutmeg, cinnamon, and ginger, boosted by espresso—and doubling up the goodness
by making it a sandwich. Between the two sides, a swirl of coffee buttercream reinforces the
espresso note. And on top, a glittering cluster of candied cocoa nibs lends crunch with the
laquered sheen of sugar.

In anod to the country of my birth, there will be my in-laws’ version of a national treasure,
the Butter Tart. Imagine a miniaturized pecan pie with a tender-but-flaky crust and a rich
filling made with walnuts, raisins, even chocolate—left completely plain if you're a purist. I
do love them as they are with their delicate honeycomb at the surface of the filling. However,
this winter, in a move that suits a more festive mood, a dry brushing of pearl or luster dust
will grant a gently gilded effect.

Finally, no cookie tin can be without chocolate if] am in charge. So last but most assuredly
far from least, once-familiar cookie kisses return from a trip through the Black Forest, hiding
a dried cherry beneath its mound of chocolate-enrobed marshmallow. A flurry of luster dust
completes them with ease and elegance. Or you could opt for a bit of silver leaf pressed into
that chocolate—a gleaming nod to the brightest days of the season ahead.
