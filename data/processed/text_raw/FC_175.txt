EDITOR'S LETTER

Springing Forward

The spaces between seasons are times of transition, Recently we moved on
from the last quiet remnants of winter—with its palette of browns and grays
and its warming, comfort-inducing foods—to a resurgence of color and life.
This issue, we're celebrating a season of new beginnings and fresh ideas for
how we cook and eat.

Nothing announces the arrival of spring quite like two of the season's
favorite vegetables, When bunches of crunchy, multihued radishes and
sweet, slender green spires of asparagus begin to appear at farmers markets
and in CSA boxes, it's time to use them at every opportunity. Enjoy crisp,
peppery radishes in their classic raw state—as a canapé of cocktail rye
spread with caper-dill goat cheese topped with the thinly sliced roots
({p. 7). Or try something a little different—such as radishes roasted with
leeks and thyme, sautéed with butter and tarragon, or pan-seared with
crispy prosciutto (all on p. 7). Crisp breaded asparagus spears serve as the
real “meat” of a sandwich layered with prosciutto and slathered with lemon
aioli on a crunchy banh mi bun (p. 34).

Look to Kate Ramos’ innovative dishes (“Mexican Classics for a Plant-
Based Planet; p. 74) for recipes that cut out animal products of any kind
without sacrificing flavor. Von Diaz (“Not Just for Rice Anymore,’ p. 60)
meets the challenge of preparing quick, easy, delicious, and perfectly cooked
one-pot meals in a rice cooker, And Jessie Sheehan (“Banish the Butter, p.
82) demonstrates that baked goods made with olive oil, coconut oil, and
nut oils have some real benefits over those made with our beloved butter.

Here's to fresh food and ideas.

Brioche—the classic French
yeast bread rich and golden
with butter and eggs, boasting
a crisp, paper-thin crust and
a silky, tender crumb—is a

But as special as it is, it isn’t
difficult to make.

WHILE THE MOST UBLQUITOUS
bread in France is the baguette,
brioche comes in a close second,
This butter-egg-sugar-enriched
bread hails from northern France—
specifically Normandy, the land of
butter—and is eaten not just for
breakfast but throughout the day.

Although it can be baked ina
loaf pan or twisted into a bread
similar to challah, it most often
takes the form of brioche a téte,
or brioche with a “head”—a knob
of dough on top of the main body
of the bread, Brioche a téte can be
a single large loaf or, as it is here,
individual breads, And while using
brioche molds produces the most
classic shape—breads with fluted
sides that have narrow bottoms
that widen out at the top—regular
muffin cups work just fine.

there are just a few pointers on
the path to perfect brioche. First,
don't skimp on the kneading time.
Although a stand mixer does the
work for you, the dough requires a
good long kneading of 10 minutes
or so to develop proper smoothness
and elasticity.

If you can, use best-quality butter.
Standard American butter generally
has a fat content of 80 percent.
European-style butters have a lower
moisture content and an increased
fat content of 82 percent.

Finally, be sure to brush on an
egg wash right before baking to
ensure your brioche takes on the
characteristic golden-brown and
luminous crust.

Brioche is best eaten fresh. If
it does get a bit stale, it makes a
marvelous bread pudding.

LEMON BRIOCHE
SERVES 18

2 pkg. active dry yeast
Ys cup warm water (105°F to 115°F)
Ys cup warm milk (105°F to 115°F)
4 cups all-purpose flour
12 tsp. kosher salt

4 eggs, room temperature
Ya cup sugar

2 Ths. lemon zest

1 cup butter, room temperature

In the bowl of a stand mixer fitted
with a dough hook, dissolve the yeast
in the water and milk. Let stand 10
minutes until yeast is softened. Add
the flour and salt. Mix on medium-
low speed until flour is moistened,
about 2 minutes

Reduce the speed to low. Separate
1 egg. Add the yolk and the remaining
3 eggs (refrigerate the egg white for
later), Add the sugar and lemon zest
Increase speed to medium. Beat
3 minutes. Reduce speed to low. Add
butter 2 Tbs. at a time, beating until
each addition is incorporated before
adding the next. Once all the butter is
added, increase the speed to
medium-high, Continue to beat until

the dough pulls away from the sides
of bowl, about 10 minutes. Transfer to
a greased bowl. Cover and let rise in a
warm place until double in size, about
i hour. Using a spatula, release the
dough from the sides of the bowl to
deflate slightly. Cover with plastic
wrap. Chill overnight, 12 to 24 hours
Grease 18 3- to 4-inch fluted
individual brioche molds or large
muffin cups. Pat the cold dough into
a 12x6-inch rectangle, Using a dough
scraper or sharp knife, cut into
18 equal portions. From each remove
a small piece, then roll into 18 1-inch
balls. Roll the remaining portions into
18 larger balls and place in the
prepared pans. Using your fingers,
make a deep indentation in the center
of each ball. Brush with water, then
press the small balls into indentations
Cover and let rise in a warm place
until double in size, about 45 minutes
Preheat the oven to 375°F. Ina

white and 1 Tbs. water, then brush

SAVORY VEGETABLE TARTS are a singular kind of versatile, They can be
served for brunch, in small pieces as an appetizer, or as a casual main
course along with a salad and glass of wine. Whether they're round, square,
or rectangular—filled with an eggy custard or luxurious cheese—they offer
the opportunity to incorporate visual creativity into your culinary skills,

‘The start to a great tart? A perfect crust. To achieve that, there are just
a few pointers to heed. First, use best-quality butter and flour. Whatever
method you use to make the dough—by hand with a pastry cutter or ina
food processor—don't overwork it. Overworking—and adding too much
water—results in a tough, not tender, crust. If the recipe calls for chilling
the dough before pressing it into the pan, don't skip that step, Not only
does it make the dough easier to handle, it allows the flour to absorb the
liquid, which results in a smoother crust. Finally, blind bake it. Blind baking
is simply prebaking the crust before filling it (and usually baking it again)
so that it turns out tender, buttery, crisp, and flaky—not soggy. Lining the
unbaked dough with a double thickness of foil—or a single thickness along
with a layer of pie weights, dried beans, or uncooked rice—helps prevent
air bubbles from forming in the crust.

REGIONAL CAKES ARE THE STUFF of legends, and they say as much about a community's taste as they do
about its personality, perseverance, and sense of humor, Cakes like the chocolate-and-pecan Texas Sheet
Cake or ooey, gooey St. Louis Butter Cake enjoy some degree of national fame and are often made outside of
the region from which they originate, but others are secrets that seem to stay more tightly within state lines.

Some local favorites are elegant and complicated, like Maryland's nine-layer Smith Island Cake or New
Orleans’ unmatched Doberge Cake (pronounced DOE-bash or DOE-berj by locals), a deliciously split per-
sonality of lemon and chocolate, Other cakes, like Kentucky's blackberry-flavored Jam Cake or the teetering
Appalachian Apple Stack Cake, are spiced and warm, like the home bakers who make them.

I've always made a habit of digging for these hallowed cake recipes in spiral-bound community, junior
league, and church cookbooks. You know the ones: hand-me-down treasures found at yard sales or library book
sales, the ones with notes scrawled in the margins and dog-eared at the corners. The best recipes are always
on pages stained from sticky fingers, proof that the recipes were beloved and, most importantly, used often.

Learning about regional cakes means sometimes getting a history lesson, too, as is the case with the North-
east’s Hot Milk Cake, a depression-era recipe that’s popularity is often credited to Ruth Ellen Church, a food
editor at the Chicago Tribune in the 1950s. The newspaper's recipe archives first noted a simple version of hot
milk cake in the winter of 1911, but Church is the one who brought the recipe back to her readers’ attention,
lauding the cake's success with using baking powder as a leavener, in lieu of eggs only.

While some local favorites are a delicious twist on a classic cake that may already be in a baker's reper-
toire—I'll never miss a chance to dig into a slice of California's Chiffon Cake or Hawaii's Chantilly Cake—others
are utterly unique and beyond comparison. Enter Michigan's Bumpy Cake. Originally created at Detroit's Sanders
chocolate shop, it earns its name from the bumpy lines of vanilla buttercream that cut across the length of
the cake, hiding under a thick blanket of cooked fudge frosting. Making distinctive sweets like this one, with
its regional character in every bite, is like traveling the country’s back roads from the comfort of my kitchen.

AKING WITH OIL has so many fabulous attributes, it's hard
to know where to begin—but here goes: Baking with oil is easy.
‘There's no softening butter or pulling out the heavy stand mixer.
Instead, oil is (almost) always just whisked (or stirred) by hand
in a bowl with the other ingredients.

Moreover, oil-based baked goods have a much longer shelf-life
than butter-based baked goods (and they're moister too!). Oil is
liquid at room temperature. This not only keeps a baked good from
drying out the way one made with butter might (since butter is
solid at room temperature), it also results in baked goods with the tenderest crumb. A
baked good made with oil is even better the day after it’s made, Oil-based baked goods
are the ultimate make-ahead treats,

In addition, nut oils—such as those called for in Pistachio Chiffon Cake with Rasp-
berry Glaze and Milk Chocolate Hazelnut Cookies—have the added benefit of subtly
flavoring your baked goods. And although butter certainly has terrific flavor, it’s never
going to taste like a pistachio or a hazelnut. Even though coconut oil is not much of
a flavor booster, it does have the attribute of behaving like butter when solid—which
makes it a great butter substitute for biscuits. You cut the coconut oil into the flour
mixture just as you would butter,

Finally, oil also makes for a wonderful confectioners’ sugar-based glaze, subtly fla-
vored, glossy, and thick. One made with olive oil—drizzled over Strawberry Cornmeal
Loaf Cake—is truly the stuff of dreams.

Cook on
a Comal

Widely used in Mexican and
Latin American cooking, a
comal (pronounced KOH-mahl)
is a round or oblong flat griddle
with a shallow rim. Originally
made of clay but now more
commonly made of carbon steel
or cast iron, it's used primarily
for cooking tortillas, toasting
spices, and charring chiles and
vegetables. Even if you only cook
Mexican or Latin American food
occasionally, a comal is a handy
tool to have around for all kinds
of kitchen tasks.

A Drizzle
Will Do

Nut oils are to nuts what olive
oil is to Olives —the heart of the
fruit’s flavor. The best of them are
intensely, sensuously, wonderfully
perfumed. A little nut oil adds that
extra touch that can transform a
good dish into a memorable one.
Oil quality differs by brand, The
best nut oils, like the best olive oils,
are made from the first pressing
They taste and smell distinctly of
the nuts from which they're made
Inferior oils are often extracted
from the second pressing of the

nut paste and have hardly any flavor
and almost no aroma.

The best oils also start out with
top-quality nuts—inferior nuts will
never be able to offer the intricate
richness of their high-quality
counterparts. Look for nut oils made
by artisan producers who take the
time to toast the nuts before pressing
to give the oil a richer, toastier flavor.

All nut oils are fragile. Because
even the best nut oil may go rancid
if it sits for too long, buy in small
quantities, After opening, seal the oil
and store it in the refrigerator.

Nut oils are always at their best
when they're poured straight from
the bottle into food that needs no
further cooking. Don't use them
for high-heat frying, as you might
olive oil. A few moments of gentle
sautéing won't do much harm, but
beyond that, you're likely to lose
most of the oil's delicate flavor.
